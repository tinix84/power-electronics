Lesson 1 — The buck converter: what happens, why, and how to design it

Power Electronic Systems · HTWG · 2026
Teaching source: lecture 1, Anwendungen der Leistungselektronik, WS 2025/2026, Riccardo Tinivella / Kurt Schenk.
Prerequisite: the 2026 circuit-foundations recap. Language: English, following the intro lesson.

A converter design starts with a question, not a component value. What must the circuit do? Which physical limit matters first? We will use the answer to choose each part, then check whether the choice works.

Our example is TI’s PMP31349 reference design. This synchronous GaN buck converts a 30–60 V bus to 12 V at 20 A. An LM5148 peak-current-mode controller drives an LMG2100R044 half-bridge at 350 kHz.

We start from the specification and work towards a complete design. At each step, we compare our estimate with TI’s chosen part and published measurements.

How we work. First, estimate by hand. State the assumptions and predict what the circuit should do. Then simulate the same model and compare the results. Finally, compare with TI’s measurements and explain any gap.

A failed choice is useful evidence. We keep it on the page, marked No!, and explain what must change. Before moving on, explain why we chose this option. Name its main benefit and cost. Then say when a credible alternative would become preferable.

This sequence follows C. Basso, Switch-Mode Power Supplies: SPICE Simulations and Practical Designs, McGraw-Hill, 2008, §5.1. The worked examples are our own. The Example scripts reproduce the calculations. §9 explains the two PLECS models to draw.

Where the buck is used. The German name says what the buck can do: Tiefsetzsteller, the converter that only sets the voltage lower. Its output must stay below its input. The boost of a later lesson is the Hochsetzsteller.

Use a buck when the load needs a lower voltage and galvanic isolation is unnecessary. Applications range from small point-of-load regulators to high-current stages with several phases. Switching frequencies span roughly 20 kHz to 10 MHz. A few hundred kilohertz is common.

Efficiency depends on the complete design and operating point. PMP31349 reaches 95.5 % at 48 V and 20 A (TI, measured, §8).

Reading route. Follow Part I (§1–11) for the complete design. Use Part II (§12) to practise diagnosing a waveform, choosing a model and defending a decision. The appendices hold background and longer derivations. §13 gives the sources and numerical checks.

The recap supplies the circuit foundations. Return to it when a current path, RMS calculation or balance equation is unclear.
