Appendix D — The diode buck

The classical buck freewheels through a diode. PMP31349 replaces that diode with the low-side GaN switch (§6.1), but the diode buck remains the clearest place to see a constant-voltage loss, the drop at low duty and the third interval of DCM. The teaching model of Appendix E is a diode buck, and this appendix uses its numbers.

D.1 Realisation with a MOSFET and a diode

The changeover switch is built from a controlled switch and a diode. Low and medium powers use a MOSFET; larger powers use an IGBT. Bipolar transistors are no longer used for this purpose, because of their drive power. When the MOSFET is on, the diode blocks.

When the MOSFET turns off, the inductor current commutates into the diode, which turns on. Each state has its own equivalent circuit, the two switch positions of §2.1.

Let x be the switch node. Inductor current flows from x toward the output; inductor voltage is measured from x to the output. The diode has its anode at the common return and cathode at x. It supplies a path for positive inductor current when Q turns off. Its symbol is labelled Dᶠ to distinguish the device from duty ratio D.

The third interval occurs in DCM (Appendix E.4). The switch node is no longer clamped to zero by a conducting diode. In the ideal small-ripple model it sits at VoV_oVo​; real switch-node capacitances can ring. Replacing the diode by a second MOSFET gives the synchronous buck of Part I (§6) and of Appendix E.7.

At 30 V input, DDD = 0.8. The mean source current becomes 8 A and the mean diode current 2 A. D.6 adds the component losses.

D.5 Pulsed current: the buck input and the boost output

Case 2: pulsed charging through a diode or switch. The capacitor receives charge in pulses while the load draws nearly constant current I. Between pulses, the capacitor supplies that current alone. Call this interval txt_xtx​. The removed charge is a rectangle:

ΔV=I txC.\Delta V=\frac{I\,t_x}{C}.ΔV=CItx​​.

Like every switching converter, a real buck is not lossless. The losses are not caused by the operating principle (D.2), but by non-ideal components. The loss sources are:

MOSFET ON resistance RDS(on)R_{DS(\mathrm{on})}RDS(on)​ Resistive conduction loss Switch RMS current squared

Resonant converters were introduced historically to reduce switching losses. Today, where possible, soft switching is used instead: the circuit and timing make the voltage or the current zero at the transition (§6.2, D.10 and Appendix F). D.7–D.9 first model the three conduction losses analysed on the slides: winding resistance, MOSFET ON resistance and diode drop. §8.1 lists the losses of PMP31349, where the GaN low side has no body diode and so no reverse-recovery row.

D.7 A useful nonideal CCM model

Use inductor winding resistance rLr_LrL​, MOSFET ON resistance rQr_QrQ​ and a constant diode forward drop VfV_fVf​. With small current ripple, replace current in their voltage drops by IoI_oIo​:

vL,on≃Vin−Vo−Io(rL+rQ),v_{L,\mathrm{on}}\simeq V_{\mathrm{in}}-V_o-I_o(r_L+r_Q),vL,on​≃Vin​−Vo​−Io​(rL​+rQ​),

vL,off≃−Vo−Vf−IorL.v_{L,\mathrm{off}}\simeq -V_o-V_f-I_or_L.vL,off​≃−Vo​−Vf​−Io​rL​.

Volt-second balance gives:

Vo=DVin−(1−D)Vf−Io(rL+DrQ).V_o=DV_{\mathrm{in}}-(1-D)V_f-I_o(r_L+Dr_Q).Vo​=DVin​−(1−D)Vf​−Io​(rL​+DrQ​).

For a resistive load, substitute Io=Vo/RI_o = V_o/RIo​=Vo​/R:

Vo=DVin−(1−D)Vf1+(rL+DrQ)/R.V_o=\frac{DV_{\mathrm{in}}-(1-D)V_f}{1+(r_L+Dr_Q)/R}.Vo​=1+(rL​+DrQ​)/RDVin​−(1−D)Vf​​.

This is the model swept in buck_loss_parametric.ipynb. It is a CCM average model. Negative predicted output voltage or a computed current valley below zero tells us that its operating assumptions have failed; those regions must not be presented as valid diode-buck predictions. §8.2 gives its synchronous form for PMP31349.

Check the formula: units, then limit cases

Before you trust a derived formula, test it in two steps.

  1. Units. Both terms of the numerator are voltages, and (rL+D rQ)/R(r_L + D\,r_Q)/R(rL​+DrQ​)/R is a ratio of resistances. The result is a voltage.
  2. Limit cases. Set the parameters to values where you know the answer:
    • No losses, rL=rQ=0r_L = r_Q = 0rL​=rQ​=0 and Vf=0V_f = 0Vf​=0: the formula gives Vo=D VinV_o = D\,V_{\mathrm{in}}Vo​=DVin​, the ideal buck.
    • D=1D = 1D=1: the diode never conducts, so VfV_fVf​ drops out and Vo=Vin/[1+(rL+rQ)/R]V_o = V_{\mathrm{in}}/[1 + (r_L + r_Q)/R]Vo​=Vin​/[1+(rL​+rQ​)/R]. With the D.8 values (30 V, 0.1 Ω each, RRR = 2.4 Ω) the output is 27.69 V. With rL=Rr_L = RrL​=R and rQ=0r_Q = 0rQ​=0 the output is half the input: a plain voltage divider.
    • D=0D = 0D=0: the switch never conducts, so rQr_QrQ​ drops out and Vo=−Vf/(1+rL/R)V_o = -V_f/(1 + r_L/R)Vo​=−Vf​/(1+rL​/R), that is −0.96 V with the D.8 values. A diode buck cannot make a negative output. The CCM model is therefore invalid at this limit, as stated above.

What the limits teach. Near D=1D = 1D=1 the diode hardly matters, and the switch carries the current: spend the money on the switch. Near D=0D = 0D=0 the switch hardly matters: spend it on the diode. The winding resistance rLr_LrL​ carries the current in both intervals, so DDD never weights it. A good inductor pays at every duty.

D.8 Worked example: required duty changes

Choose illustrative rL=rQr_L = r_QrL​=rQ​ = 0.1 Ω and VfV_fVf​ = 1 V. At VoV_oVo​ = 24 V and IoI_oIo​ = 10 A:

D=Vo+Vf+IorLVin+Vf−IorQ.D=\frac{V_o+V_f+I_or_L}{V_{\mathrm{in}}+V_f-I_or_Q}.D=Vin​+Vf​−Io​rQ​Vo​+Vf​+Io​rL​​.

At 30 V the required duty is 26/30 = 0.86667; at 48 V it is 26/48 = 0.54167. The ideal values were 0.8 and 0.5. The controller must increase duty to overcome the losses.

With only VfV_fVf​ = 1 V and zero resistances, the corresponding duties are 25/31 = 0.80645 and 25/49 = 0.51020. Diode conduction loss at 10 A is then about 1.94 W and 4.90 W respectively. Why worse at high input? Lower required duty means a larger fraction of the period is spent freewheeling through the diode.

D.9 Estimate dissipation with the correct current measure

The approximate CCM conduction losses are:

PL=rLJ,PQ=rQDJ,PD=Vf(1−D)Io.P_L=r_LJ,\qquad P_Q=r_QDJ,\qquad P_D=V_f(1-D)I_o.PL​=rL​J,PQ​=rQ​DJ,PD​=Vf​(1−D)Io​.

For the full illustrative parasitic case at 48 V → 24 V, 10 A, DDD = 0.54167, the OFF-slope estimate gives ripple about 1.986 A. The winding dissipates about 10.03 W, Q about 5.43 W, and the diode about 4.58 W. Conduction-only efficiency is approximately 92.29%.

These illustrative resistances are deliberately large enough to make their effects visible. This is not a prediction for a chosen converter. The mean-drop voltage model neglects small waveform corrections; including ripple in the RMS losses adds a small correction to its DC power balance.

Other losses include MOSFET voltage/current overlap during transitions, gate drive, device capacitance, diode reverse recovery, inductor core loss and frequency-dependent winding/connection loss. A first switching-overlap estimate is:

Psw≃12VinIo(tr+tf)fs.P_{\mathrm{sw}}\simeq\frac{1}{2}V_{\mathrm{in}}I_o(t_r+t_f)f_s.Psw​≃21​Vin​Io​(tr​+tf​)fs​.

At 48 V, 10 A, 40 ns combined transition time and 100 kHz this gives 0.96 W. It excludes capacitance and reverse recovery and assumes the stated overlap shape. It is a screening estimate, not a complete loss model.

Switching loss from datasheet energies

Device datasheets give the switching energies EonE_{\mathrm{on}}Eon​ and EoffE_{\mathrm{off}}Eoff​ against current, at a test voltage and temperature. Read each energy at the current of its own edge. In a buck, the switch turns on at the valley Io−ΔIL/2I_o - \Delta I_L/2Io​−ΔIL​/2 and turns off at the peak Io+ΔIL/2I_o + \Delta I_L/2Io​+ΔIL​/2. Scale both energies to the operating voltage, to first order in proportion to Vin/VtestV_{\mathrm{in}}/V_{\mathrm{test}}Vin​/Vtest​. The switching loss is a power:

Psw=fs [Eon(Ivalley)+Eoff(Ipk)]P_{\mathrm{sw}}=f_s\,[E_{\mathrm{on}}(I_{\mathrm{valley}})+E_{\mathrm{off}}(I_{\mathrm{pk}})]Psw​=fs​[Eon​(Ivalley​)+Eoff​(Ipk​)]

An energy times a frequency gives watts. A 600 V IGBT that loses 5 mJ per cycle at its operating current dissipates 5 mJ · 100 kHz = 500 W at 100 kHz. This is why large IGBTs switch at a few kilohertz to some tens of kilohertz.

Which edge costs more depends on the device. In a hard-switched MOSFET the turn-on often dominates, because the switch also discharges its own output capacitance. In an IGBT the tail current can make the turn-off the larger loss (Appendix F). Read both curves, and do not assume a fixed ratio.

A figure of merit. Within one device technology, a lower on-resistance needs a larger die, and a larger die has more gate charge. The product Qg RDS(on)Q_g\,R_{DS(\mathrm{on})}Qg​RDS(on)​ is therefore nearly constant within a technology, and it compares technologies. A lower product allows a higher frequency for the same loss, and a higher frequency allows smaller magnetics and capacitors.

Why soft switching can help: reducing switch voltage or current during the transition reduces overlap loss. It requires suitable circuit and timing conditions; simply operating at the CCM/DCM boundary does not prove that those conditions are satisfied.

D.10 Identify which device switches at zero, and at which edge

Boundary and DCM operation create natural current-zero events. Those events can simplify commutation and enable higher-frequency designs. However, zero inductor current, zero semiconductor current and zero semiconductor voltage are different conditions. Label the device and the turn-on or turn-off event before calling a transition ZCS or ZVS.

For the ordinary positive-current buck sequence:

Event What the current waveform provides What remains to check
Freewheel diode finishes conducting Its forward current reaches zero naturally Stored charge, subsequent reverse voltage and ringing; recovery stress is often reduced
High-side Q is commanded ON after the zero-current boundary Initial load-related inductor current is zero Q may still support a large VDSV_{DS}VDS​; capacitive discharge can cause turn-on loss
High-side Q is turned OFF at the positive current peak Boundary/DCM alone does not make its current zero Current/voltage overlap, capacitance charging and device-specific turn-off loss
A synchronous/resonant dead-time transition moves x toward VinV_{\mathrm{in}}Vin​ Correctly directed current can discharge high-side output capacitance Enough commutating charge/energy and appropriate gate timing are required for high-side ZVS turn-on

ZCS describes negligible current through the relevant device at its switching event. ZVS describes negligible voltage across it at the relevant event, often gate turn-on for a MOSFET. A high-side buck MOSFET can start with zero load current while its output capacitance still holds substantial voltage. Thus a favorable load-current condition does not eliminate all turn-on energy.

To obtain high-side ZVS in a synchronous buck, the switch node must rise close to VinV_{\mathrm{in}}Vin​ before that device is turned on. A suitably controlled negative inductor-current interval can provide that node transition during dead time. Resonant extensions can also exploit the L/CossL/C_{\mathrm{oss}}L/Coss​ trajectory. The required polarity, energy and timing depend on VinV_{\mathrm{in}}Vin​, VoV_oVo​, parasitic capacitances and the actual conduction paths.

A strict positive-triangle boundary waveform touching zero does not by itself establish this transition. §6.2 checks this transition for PMP31349’s GaN half-bridge.

Once a deliberate negative valley or a finite resonant interval is added, recalculate the current area. The simple Io=Ipk/2I_o = I_{\mathrm{pk}}/2Io​=Ipk​/2 expression applies to the ideal zero-valley triangle, not automatically to triangular-current-mode variants with negative current. This distinction matters when combining average-current accuracy with soft-switching control. A published buck example explicitly optimizes the required negative current: Tang et al., Optimization of Minimum Negative Current BCM Synchronous Buck Converter.

D.11 Sensitivity means “at this operating point”

T4 (§12) applies this method to PMP31349 with its calibrated loss model. Here it runs on the diode buck of D.7, where every derivative has a closed form.

Start by stating what is fixed

A sweep shows how a model changes across a range; a local sensitivity is the slope at one specific point. Before differentiating, state the operating point, the mode, and what is held fixed. For the following open-loop CCM calculation, hold VinV_{\mathrm{in}}Vin​, duty DDD and resistive load RRR fixed when varying a parasitic parameter. The output is allowed to change.

Define:

N=DVin−(1−D)Vf,A=1+rL+DrQR,Vo=NA.N=DV_{\mathrm{in}}-(1-D)V_f,\qquad A=1+\frac{r_L+Dr_Q}{R},\qquad V_o=\frac{N}{A}.N=DVin​−(1−D)Vf​,A=1+RrL​+DrQ​​,Vo​=AN​.

The local prediction for several small changes is:

ΔVo≃∑p∂Vo∂pΔp.\Delta V_o\simeq\sum_p\frac{\partial V_o}{\partial p}\Delta p.ΔVo​≃p∑​∂p∂Vo​​Δp.

This is a tangent approximation, not a guarantee for a large parameter change. Recompute the operating point and mode when a change is large or approaches the critical boundary.

Derivatives explain the sweep shapes

∂Vo∂Vin=DA,∂Vo∂Vf=−1−DA.\frac{\partial V_o}{\partial V_{\mathrm{in}}}=\frac{D}{A},\qquad \frac{\partial V_o}{\partial V_f}=-\frac{1-D}{A}.∂Vin​∂Vo​​=AD​,∂Vf​∂Vo​​=−A1−D​.

∂Vo∂rL=−VoRA,∂Vo∂rQ=−DVoRA.\frac{\partial V_o}{\partial r_L}=-\frac{V_o}{RA},\qquad \frac{\partial V_o}{\partial r_Q}=-\frac{DV_o}{RA}.∂rL​∂Vo​​=−RAVo​​,∂rQ​∂Vo​​=−RADVo​​.

∂Vo∂D=Vin+Vf−VorQ/RA.\frac{\partial V_o}{\partial D}=\frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}+V_f-V_or_Q/R}{A}.∂D∂Vo​​=AVin​+Vf​−Vo​rQ​/R​.

Why: winding resistance acts throughout the conducting cycle. High-side ON resistance acts only during DDD. Diode drop acts during 1−D1-D1−D. Lower load resistance increases current and makes resistive drops more important. Notice also that the duty derivative includes the change of conduction resistance with duty; differentiating only the numerator would miss it.

For rQr_QrQ​ compared to rLr_LrL​, the ratio of the absolute slopes is exactly DDD in this model. But this compares equal changes measured in ohms. Component selection usually compares achievable changes, losses, cost and temperature, not just raw derivative values.

At DDD = 0.2, reducing diode drop by 0.1 V raises output by about 76.2 mV, whereas reducing rQr_QrQ​ by 10 mΩ raises it by only 3.93 mV. At DDD = 0.8, the same changes give about 18.6 mV and 68.7 mV respectively. The relative value of those two changes reverses with operating point.

This does not mean the diode is always the largest loss at low duty. The comparison includes the size of each proposed parameter change, and other losses may dominate. The lesson is to calculate the effect where the converter will actually operate.

The sensitivity map leaves points outside approximate CCM blank. A continuous-looking algebraic surface beyond a mode boundary can be mathematically smooth and physically wrong.

Absolute and normalized sensitivity answer different questions

An absolute sensitivity has units, such as V/Ω. A normalized sensitivity compares fractional changes:

SpVo=pVo∂Vo∂p,ΔVoVo≃SpVoΔpp.S_p^{V_o}=\frac{p}{V_o}\frac{\partial V_o}{\partial p},\qquad \frac{\Delta V_o}{V_o}\simeq S_p^{V_o}\frac{\Delta p}{p}.SpVo​​=Vo​p​∂p∂Vo​​,Vo​ΔVo​​≃SpVo​​pΔp​.

For the parasitics:

SrLVo=−rLRA,SrQVo=−DrQRA,SVfVo=−(1−D)VfN.S_{r_L}^{V_o}=-\frac{r_L}{RA},\qquad S_{r_Q}^{V_o}=-\frac{Dr_Q}{RA},\qquad S_{V_f}^{V_o}=-\frac{(1-D)V_f}{N}.SrL​Vo​​=−RArL​​,SrQ​Vo​​=−RADrQ​​,SVf​Vo​​=−N(1−D)Vf​​.

Use absolute sensitivity to assess a specified change such as “10 mΩ less resistance.” Use normalized sensitivity to compare percentage uncertainties. Normalization becomes misleading near zero output or zero nominal parameter; use absolute changes there.

A regulated converter has a different sensitivity

If a voltage loop holds VoV_oVo​ fixed, output voltage is no longer the freely moving result of the open-loop calculation. Duty must move to compensate. At fixed target VoV_oVo​ and RRR, so IoI_oIo​ is also fixed:

D=Vo+Vf+IorLB,B=Vin+Vf−IorQ.D=\frac{V_o+V_f+I_or_L}{B},\qquad B=V_{\mathrm{in}}+V_f-I_or_Q.D=BVo​+Vf​+Io​rL​​,B=Vin​+Vf​−Io​rQ​.

∂D∂rL=IoB,∂D∂rQ=DIoB,∂D∂Vf=1−DB.\frac{\partial D}{\partial r_L}=\frac{I_o}{B},\qquad \frac{\partial D}{\partial r_Q}=\frac{DI_o}{B},\qquad \frac{\partial D}{\partial V_f}=\frac{1-D}{B}.∂rL​∂D​=BIo​​,∂rQ​∂D​=BDIo​​,∂Vf​∂D​=B1−D​.

At the 48 V, 24 V, 10 A parasitic operating point, BBB = 48 V. An added 10 mΩ winding resistance requires approximately 0.002083 more duty, or 0.2083 percentage points. This keeps output near its target if the loop has enough gain and duty headroom, but adds approximately 1 W winding loss at 10 A. Regulation can hide output-voltage sensitivity while efficiency and temperature still deteriorate.

Why the operating point matters twice: it sets the derivative, and it sets whether the controller has enough range to compensate. Near maximum duty, a small added drop can make the requested voltage unattainable.

Bridge to small-signal dynamics

Static sensitivity describes the settled change. It does not say how fast or stably the converter gets there. For an ideal averaged CCM stage with resistive load:

LdiLdt=dvin−vo,Cdvodt=iL−voR.L\frac{di_L}{dt}=d v_{\mathrm{in}}-v_o,\qquad C\frac{dv_o}{dt}=i_L-\frac{v_o}{R}.LdtdiL​​=dvin​−vo​,Cdtdvo​​=iL​−Rvo​​.

Write each quantity as its operating value plus a small perturbation. Linearizing the product d⋅vind\cdot v_{\mathrm{in}}d⋅vin​ gives:

Ldi^Ldt=Vind^+Dv^in−v^o.L\frac{d\hat{i}_L}{dt}=V_{\mathrm{in}}\hat{d}+D\hat{v}_{\mathrm{in}}-\hat{v}_o.Ldtdi^L​​=Vin​d^+Dv^in​−v^o​.

The duty perturbation is multiplied by the operating input voltage; the input-voltage disturbance is multiplied by the operating duty. The neglected product of the two perturbations is second order. With fixed input and zero initial perturbations:

v^o(s)d^(s)=VinLCs2+(L/R)s+1.\frac{\hat{v}_o(s)}{\hat{d}(s)}=\frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}}{LCs^2+(L/R)s+1}.d^(s)v^o​(s)​=LCs2+(L/R)s+1Vin​​.

Even this ideal model has operating-point-dependent gain and load-dependent damping. Adding losses, current regulation or crossing into DCM changes the model further. This is why a notebook sweep and a Bode plot need stated conditions, not only a topology name.

D.12 Why is a diode drop especially visible at low duty?

Answer: the diode conducts during 1−D1-D1−D, so ∂Vo/∂Vf=−(1−D)/A\partial V_o/\partial V_f = -(1-D)/A∂Vo​/∂Vf​=−(1−D)/A (D.11). The same added forward voltage acts for more of each period at low duty. Its loss still depends on current, so voltage sensitivity alone is not a complete thermal ranking.
