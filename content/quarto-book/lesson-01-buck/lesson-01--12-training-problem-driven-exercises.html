12. Training: problem-driven exercises

Start each exercise by making a prediction. Sketch the current path or waveform before calculating. Then choose a model and solve the problem. Compare the alternatives and explain which one you would use.

Try the problem before reading its solution. For every exercise, state your choice, one benefit and one cost. Compare a credible alternative and name the condition or measurement that would change your choice. Keep the operating point and requirements clear.

  • T1–T4 are worked here, all seven parts included.
  • E1–E5 are assigned Exercises. This lesson shows their first four parts. Their Solution, the last three parts, is held in the Enrolled companion lesson and released after the deadline.

Operating points. T4 works on PMP31349 itself, with the calibrated model of §8.4. The others need a mechanism that the finished GaN design hides: a diode buck, a deliberately saturating core, an authored filter or an authored loop. They use the simpler teaching model of Appendix E, a diode buck from 30–48 V to 24 V and 10 A at 100 kHz with 60 µH and 100 µF, or a stated variant of it.

Every Problem states its own operating point; never carry a number from one operating point to another.

E2–E5 examine the same LC network through four questions. How does it filter voltage (E2)? What output impedance does the load see (E3)? Where are its poles (E4)? What changes when feedback is added (E5)?

The filter equations impose an ideal switch-node voltage and place R across C. They require continuous conduction or a synchronous stage that permits current reversal. A diode buck in DCM needs Appendix E’s three-interval model. The feedback examples describe small changes about a positive-current CCM equilibrium. Keep the load resistance R separate from the series resistance r.

The exercises go beyond the live route. Session 3 works one or two of them in class; the rest are assigned as reading.

T1 — Ripple shape: rounded, triangular or rectangular?

Problem

The scope shows the output ripple of a buck. On one board it is rounded, almost sinusoidal; on a second it is a triangle; on a third it carries a step at every switching edge. If the waveform becomes more angular, which part of the output capacitor is responsible, and how would you prove it? Start with the simplest current waveform and find the contribution of each parasitic on its own.

Operating point. A prescribed capacitor current: the 2 A peak-to-peak, zero-mean triangle of the teaching model at 48 V input and 100 kHz, into CCC = 100 µF with series resistance rCr_CrC​ (ESR) and series inductance lCl_ClC​ (ESL). The current is imposed; the converter around it is not solved.

Why we do it

The shape of the ripple is the first clue the scope gives about which impedance dominates. Read it right and you know whether to add capacitance, lower the ESR or shorten the current loop. Read it wrong and you buy the wrong part. The same ESR also moves the loop (§4.4, §7.2), so a misread parasitic costs twice.

What matters

Model the output-capacitor branch as an ideal capacitance CCC with series rCr_CrC​ and lCl_ClC​. For branch current iCi_CiC​, with a consistent passive voltage reference:

vterminal=vC+rCiC+lCdiCdt,dvCdt=iCC.v_{\mathrm{terminal}}=v_C+r_Ci_C+l_C\frac{di_C}{dt},\qquad \frac{dv_C}{dt}=\frac{i_C}{C}.vterminal​=vC​+rC​iC​+lC​dtdiC​​,dtdvC​​=CiC​​.

  • The three terms do three different operations on the same current: integrate, multiply, differentiate.
  • Their extremes need not occur at the same instant. Adding peak-to-peak magnitudes gives a bound, not the ripple.
  • A measured edge spike can also contain ringing, coupling and probe-loop pickup, so the shape alone does not uniquely identify a component.

Where it appears in practice

  • PMP31349’s output bank (§4.4). The polymer ESR sets the bank impedance at the loop crossover, while the ceramics carry most of the ripple current near fsf_sfs​. TI’s scope capture of the output ripple (§9.1) is the measured shape to read.
  • Point-of-load (POL) regulators for processors and FPGAs. An all-ceramic bank has little ESR, so the ESL of the parts, pads and vias shows up as steps at the edges.
  • Choosing between polymer, electrolytic and ceramic banks. The shape says which term to buy down first.

Possible solutions

Solution A — superpose the three terms in the time domain. For the ideal CCM buck with approximately constant load current, iCi_CiC​ is a continuous, zero-mean triangle. Its three voltage contributions have different shapes:

Term Operation on the triangular current Resulting shape
Ideal capacitance Integrate current Continuous, piecewise parabolic; rounded and often approximately sinusoidal in appearance
ESR Multiply current by rCr_CrC​ Continuous triangular component
ESL Multiply current slope by lCl_ClC​ Piecewise constant component, with steps where the current slope changes

Increasing the ESR alone therefore moves this ordinary buck’s output ripple toward a triangle; it does not generate a square wave. A rectangular contribution points to a different current waveform or to the derivative term. E1 asks which inductor model provides exactly such an additional current component.

Prescribe the 2 A peak-to-peak triangle at 100 kHz into CCC = 100 µF and vary only the capacitor parameters, so the comparison isolates their voltage contributions:

ESR, with ESL = 0 ESR-only ripple magnitude Total computed terminal ripple
0 0 25.0 mV peak-to-peak
10 mΩ 20 mV peak-to-peak 29.0 mV peak-to-peak
100 mΩ 200 mV peak-to-peak 200.0 mV peak-to-peak

Why 25 mV + 20 mV does not equal 45 mV here: the capacitive and ESR extremes occur at different instants. Adding their peak-to-peak magnitudes is a conservative bound; summing their instantaneous waveforms gives the actual result. At high ESR, the triangle dominates.

The current slopes of this symmetric triangle are ±0.4 A/µs. An illustrative 50 nH of effective series inductance adds ±20 mV, a 40 mV peak-to-peak rectangular contribution. This ESL value is an intentionally visible capacitor-plus-connection example, not a selected capacitor’s specification.

Solution B — read the branch impedance against frequency. In a simple model the ESR adds a zero at 1/(2πrCC)1/(2\pi r_C C)1/(2πrC​C) to the capacitor branch; the ESL adds a series resonance above it. Whichever term dominates ∣Z∣|Z|∣Z∣ at fsf_sfs​ and its first harmonics sets the shape: the capacitive slope gives the rounded wave, the flat ESR shelf the triangle, the inductive rise the steps.

This is the view §4.4 uses for TI’s bank, and it shows at once that the same ESR zero also changes the loop phase (§7.2).

Solution C — change one thing, in the model or on the bench. Set the ESL to zero and sweep the ESR. Then restore a fixed ESR and vary only the ESL. On hardware, swap one capacitor type at a time and probe at the capacitor terminals with the shortest ground connection available.

Analog Devices’ Input and Output Noise in Buck Converters Explained separates the capacitive, ESR and high-frequency parasitic contributions this way.

Pros and cons

Solution Pros Cons
A. Time-domain superposition Exact for a prescribed current; shows the instantaneous shape and why peak magnitudes do not add Needs the current waveform; does not solve how a changed component alters the converter’s own current
B. Impedance against frequency Fast; ranks the terms at fsf_sfs​; links ripple and loop through the same ESR zero Loses the time shape and the phase between the terms; one frequency hides the harmonics
C. Change one thing Separates cause from effect; works on real hardware Each run answers one question; the probe adds its own loop, ringing and pickup

Conclusion

Use time-domain superposition first to explain the shape. It separates capacitance, ESR and ESL with little calculation, but assumes a prescribed capacitor current. Use the impedance view when the frequency range matters, and a switching model when that current assumption fails.

For the 100 mΩ case, investigate ESR before buying more capacitance: its triangular contribution dominates. For an edge step, first repeat the measurement with a short probe return. Then change one suspected element. A terminal-current jump is another explanation, examined in E1.

Our next action follows the observed shape. We do not select a replacement capacitor until a change-one-thing check supports the cause.

T2 — Is a CCM buck a current source?

Problem

An LED string or a battery needs a set mean current, not a set voltage. A designer proposes the teaching-model buck at a fixed duty and argues that its inductor already makes the current smooth. Does a fixed-duty CCM buck regulate current? If not, how would you deliver a commanded mean current, and what does each way cost?

Operating point. Teaching model, diode buck: VinV_{\mathrm{in}}Vin​ = 48 V, LLL = 60 µH, fsf_sfs​ = 100 kHz. Fixed duty DDD = 0.5 into a 2.4 Ω or 4.8 Ω resistor, or into a stiff voltage such as an LED string or a battery. For the peak-controlled routes: a commanded peak of 1 A, with the load voltage between 12 V and 36 V.

Why we do it

Smooth current and regulated current are different requirements. An inductor makes current continuous on a short timescale; a current source must hold its mean delivered current close to a setpoint as the load voltage changes. Mixing up the two is a common design error, and it hides until the load voltage moves.

What matters

The duty sets a voltage. At fixed duty, the ideal CCM steady-state relations are:

Vo=DVin,Io=DVinR.V_o=DV_{\mathrm{in}},\qquad I_o=\frac{DV_{\mathrm{in}}}{R}.Vo​=DVin​,Io​=RDVin​​.

At DDD = 0.5 a 2.4 Ω load takes 10 A and a 4.8 Ω load takes 5 A, and both stay in CCM with 60 µH. The duty sets a voltage, not a current independent of the load.

A stiff load has no preferred current. A battery or an LED string imposes a fairly stiff voltage instead of behaving as a resistor. For the ideal averaged inductor:

LdILdt=DVin−Vo.L\frac{dI_L}{dt}=DV_{\mathrm{in}}-V_o.LdtdIL​​=DVin​−Vo​.

If the imposed output voltage equals DVinDV_{\mathrm{in}}DVin​ exactly, any initial mean current is an equilibrium of this simplified model: there is no preferred setpoint. A small mismatch makes the current drift instead of restoring it. At 48 V, an absolute duty error of 0.01 gives a 0.48 V average error across L, an initial slew of 8 A/ms with 60 µH, before load dynamics or resistance restore balance.

Compliance. The requested current must lie within the available output-voltage range and the component ratings. An open load cannot absorb a programmed current, so the controller needs a voltage ceiling. These limits define the compliance range.

Where it appears in practice

  • LED drivers. The forward voltage of a string is stiff and moves with temperature, so the driver regulates current; many use peak or boundary control.
  • Battery chargers in constant-current, then constant-voltage operation. TI’s CC/CV buck discussion gives an implemented current-feedback example.
  • PMP31349 itself. The LM5148 runs peak current mode (§7.1) as an inner loop under the voltage loop. Its peak control shapes the power stage; it does not hold the mean current at a setpoint.
  • Laser-diode, plating and electrolyser supplies, where the process is set by current, not by voltage.

Possible solutions

Solution A — let the passive stage do it: more inductance, or the series resistance. Double LLL while keeping VinV_{\mathrm{in}}Vin​, DDD and RRR fixed in ideal CCM: the ripple halves, and the settled mean current remains DVin/RDV_{\mathrm{in}}/RDVin​/R. The resistance does set a current, but poorly. At fixed DDD, a 0.2 V change of the imposed output voltage moves the current by about 1 A if the effective series resistance is 0.2 Ω. Solution B — an average-current loop in CCM. Sense the current, compare it with a reference and adjust the duty so that the mean current error is driven toward zero. This is a useful current-regulation architecture: the controller sets a peak and detects the zero-current boundary, while the load determines the voltage required to accept that current. A fixed-duty CCM buck does not set a preferred current. More inductance slows a current change but does not regulate its mean.

For accurate mean current at fixed frequency, choose the average-current loop. It needs sensing and compensation, but keeps ripple and RMS current low. A peak ceiling alone is not an equivalent regulator.

Boundary peak control is a valid alternative when variable frequency and higher RMS current are acceptable. Here it delivers half the peak current in the ideal model. Choose fixed-frequency DCM only with pulse-area or repetition-rate control, or feedback that corrects the mean.

For a charger requiring accurate current, keep the average-current loop as the design route. For a driver prioritising simple boundary operation, investigate the peak-controlled alternative. State the compliance range and current-error budget before selecting hardware.

T3 — Saturation: is the peak current safe?

Problem

An inductor passes the steady-state ripple and RMS checks, and the voltage loop is stable at every operating point. Can a load step or an input step still drive the inductor into saturation? If so, what would you add to keep the peak current safe, and what does it cost?

Operating point. Teaching model, a buck whose freewheeling path carries positive current only (diode emulation), regulated to 24 V at about 8 A, fsf_sfs​ = 100 kHz, CCC = 100 µF, winding resistance 50 mΩ. Disturbances: a load step from 3 Ω to 2 Ω at VinV_{\mathrm{in}}Vin​ = 48 V, and an input step from 30 V to 48 V at RRR = 3 Ω.

Authored magnetic curve: 60 µH up to 14 A, falling linearly to 6 µH at 18 A, and 6 µH above that, with no hysteresis, thermal dependence or core loss. A real Isat rating normally describes a specified percentage inductance drop, rather than an abrupt universal failure current; use the manufacturer’s bias and temperature information.

The sampled voltage controller sees only the output voltage:

en=24−vo(nTs),dn=clip(xn+Kpen, 0.02, 0.95),e_n=24-v_o(nT_s),\qquad d_n=\mathrm{clip}(x_n+K_pe_n,\ 0.02,\ 0.95),en​=24−vo​(nTs​),dn​=clip(xn​+Kp​en​, 0.02, 0.95),

with KpK_pKp​ = 0.03 duty/V and KiK_iKi​ = 100 duty/(V·s), one duty update per period, and the integrator frozen when it would push the duty further beyond a bound. This simple anti-windup acts at those bounds; it does not track the separate current-limit override. Its local linear sampled model has all poles inside the unit circle at the three nominal conditions, so local voltage-loop instability is not required to produce a current excursion.

This is an intentionally weakly damped teaching loop, not an optimised regulator or a model of a particular IC.

Why we do it

Voltage regulation commands energy delivery in response to the output error. By itself it imposes no instantaneous ceiling on the current in the inductor or the switch. The steady-state checks are the easy ones; the transient peak decides whether the part survives. This is a limitation of an unprotected voltage-only control path, not a rule that every voltage-mode regulator lacks protection.

A voltage-mode controller may include a separate fast current comparator, overload timer and shutdown logic; the PWM regulation architecture and its fault protections must be assessed separately.

What matters

Flux linkage accumulates over cycles. Do not impose the steady-state conversion ratio on every cycle:

λn+1−λn=∫nTs(n+1)TsvL(t) dt,vL=vx−vo−rsiL.\lambda_{n+1}-\lambda_n=\int_{nT_s}^{(n+1)T_s}v_L(t)\,dt,\qquad v_L=v_x-v_o-r_si_L.λn+1​−λn​=∫nTs​(n+1)Ts​​vL​(t)dt,vL​=vx​−vo​−rs​iL​.

For linear L this flux-linkage change is L times the current change. In periodic steady state the integral is zero. During a transient it can be positive for several consecutive periods, moving the current and the flux to a higher operating point. That is how the converter meets a new load; a nonzero cycle integral is not automatically a fault.

  • CCM does not require the current to return to zero, only to its previous-cycle value once periodic operation is re-established. The concern is whether the transient peak exceeds the magnetic and semiconductor limits before the controller restores balance. Saturation can also exist in an undesirable periodic waveform: zero net volt-seconds alone does not prove that the core stayed out of saturation within the cycle.
  • Load step: inductor current cannot jump, so the capacitor supplies the deficit and VoV_oVo​ falls. The controller lengthens the ON time to restore VoV_oVo​ and recharge CCC; the inductor must supply both the new load and the recharge current, so its transient peak can exceed the final load current plus half the ripple.
  • Input step: if VinV_{\mathrm{in}}Vin​ rises before the duty falls, each existing ON pulse applies more voltage to L. As a frozen-state estimate, at 8 A and 24 V from 30 V with 50 mΩ, DDD ≈ 0.81333; raising the input to 48 V adds about 14.64 V to the cycle-average inductor voltage. At 100 kHz that is 146.4 µV·s per cycle, an initial 2.44 A rise per cycle with 60 µH, until VoV_oVo​, current and duty respond.

Saturation steepens the ramp. For a nonlinear but memoryless magnetic characteristic, define the differential inductance:

Ldiff(i)=dλdi,didt=vLLdiff(i).L_{\mathrm{diff}}(i)=\frac{d\lambda}{di},\qquad \frac{di}{dt}=\frac{v_L}{L_{\mathrm{diff}}(i)}.Ldiff​(i)=didλ​,dtdi​=Ldiff​(i)vL​​.

As the core approaches saturation, LdiffL_{\mathrm{diff}}Ldiff​ falls, and the same 24 V across L rises at 0.4 A/µs with 60 µH but 4 A/µs with 6 µH. A controller still requesting ON time can therefore underestimate the peak severely if its design assumes constant L.

Do not use v=d[L(i)i]/dtv = d[L(i)i]/dtv=d[L(i)i]/dt with a datasheet differential-inductance curve as though L(i)iL(i)iL(i)i were the flux linkage: integrate the curve to obtain λ(i)\lambda(i)λ(i), and use λ\lambdaλ as the magnetic state.

Where it appears in practice

  • PMP31349 (§3.3). TI sets the peak current limit at 35 A, while the Würth inductor has lost 10 % of its inductance at 20.9 A. In an overload the inductor runs far past that point: always check the inductor at the current limit, not only at full load.
  • Start-up into a large capacitive load, where the inrush current, not the load, sets the peak.
  • 48 V battery buses and other supplies with line transients, where an input step is part of the specification.
  • Voltage-mode controllers with a separate fast current comparator: TI discusses such controllers in its voltage-mode/current-mode comparison.

Possible solutions

Each solution is compared with the same voltage controller and the same disturbances. Peaks and energy checks use a 2 ms simulation with the disturbance at 0.2 ms (scripts/saturation_transient_example.py):

Solution A — a bigger core. Keep the controller and choose an inductor that stays linear past the largest transient peak: the linear column says above 21.58 A in this example, plus margin for tolerance and temperature. No new circuit is needed, and the loop sees the inductance it was designed for.

Solution B — a separate fast peak-current comparator. Keep the existing inductor and voltage loop. Add a comparator with a 13 A trip, below the saturation knee. Allow an illustrative 200 ns propagation delay. After a trip, hold the switch off for the rest of the period.

If current remains at or above the threshold at the next cycle start, skip that ON pulse. The simulated peaks are 13.083 A and 13.076 A. They exceed 13 A because current keeps rising during the comparator and gate-drive delay:

With about 24 V, 60 µH and 200 ns the extra rise is about 80 mA, consistent with the protected traces. If the comparator acted only after LdiffL_{\mathrm{diff}}Ldiff​ had fallen to 6 µH, the same voltage and delay would add about 0.8 A. With zero delay the trip caps the current at 13 A, which makes the delay overshoot directly measurable.

Limiting the current below the knee does not by itself prevent an output overvoltage.

Solution C — shape the disturbance instead of clipping it. Input-voltage feedforward cuts the duty disturbance of a line step. Appropriate voltage compensation, soft start, anti-windup and enough transient energy storage reduce unnecessary excursions. Sustained overload may need longer OFF intervals, foldback, hiccup or shutdown. None of these sets a hard instantaneous ceiling.

Solution D — peak-current-mode control. Make the current comparator the PWM itself: every ON pulse ends when the sensed current reaches the command, and a clamp on the command is a cycle-by-cycle limit. This is how the LM5148 runs PMP31349 (§7.1). The sensing, blanking and timing constraints of B remain.

Conclusion

Keep the separate fast peak comparator for this transient problem. A stable voltage loop does not enforce the magnetic limit. The 13 A trip keeps the demonstrated peaks below the 14 A knee, while the delay explains why they exceed 13 A.

A larger core is an alternative when the required normal transient needs more current. It costs magnetic hardware and still needs an overload check. Better damping can reduce excursions, but does not impose a hard ceiling.

Retain the trip and improve the transient response if necessary. If it interrupts a required load step, reconsider the core and threshold together. Include sensing error, delay, tolerance and temperature before treating that threshold as a hardware setting.

The model predicts electrical stress, not component failure. A slower average-current limit can miss a peak inside one pulse.

Change one thing: replace the saturable element by constant 60 µH, then restore it and add only the peak trip. Finally set comparator delay to zero. Each comparison isolates one cause.

Problem

A colleague has budget to improve one thing on PMP31349, to raise its efficiency and tighten its open-loop output: a lower-DCR inductor, a lower-resistance GaN stage, a faster switching edge, a larger inductance or a higher switching frequency. Why we do it

A sweep shows how a model changes across a range; a local sensitivity is the slope at one specific point. A ranking without its operating point ranks nothing: the part that wins at full load can lose at light load. And the money for a better part is spent once, while the converter runs at every point of its range.

What matters

  • State what is fixed before differentiating. Two views answer different questions. Open loop: VinV_{\mathrm{in}}Vin​, the duty DDD and the load current are held, and VoV_oVo​ moves. Regulated: the loop holds VoV_oVo​ = 12 V, the duty moves, and the cost shows as efficiency.
  • Know which terms are fitted. The overlap time, the core-loss factor, the output-charge factor and the resistance rise with load are fitted to TI’s measurement (§8.4). Their sensitivities say what the fit contains.
