3.1 The ripple ratio sets L

Never use the mean current for a resistive loss. It is the most common error in loss estimates.

Same mean, very different RMS. A narrow pulse carries a much larger RMS value than a DC current with the same mean. Take a 1 A mean current as 10 A pulses for 10 % of the period. 3.3 Checks against the real part

  • Saturation. Würth specifies a 10 % drop of inductance at 20.9 A (reverse-engineering-checks.json, datasheet). Our 22.92 A peak at 60 V and full load lies past that point, so the strict rule “peak below the saturation current” fails: No! The metal-composite core loses inductance gradually. The cost is therefore a slightly larger ripple than (3.2b), not a collapse. TI’s schematic notes “~24 A Ipk” for this inductor, close to our 22.92 A.

  • Current limit. TI sets the peak current limit at 35 A (TIDMDL2 note: 60 mV across the 1.714 mΩ shunt). That leaves headroom for the load step, but in an overload the inductor sees 35 A, far past 20.9 A. Always check the inductor at the current limit, not only at full load.

  • Heating. The BOM lists the part as a 17 A, 3.85 mΩ inductor (TIDMDL6). At 20.07 A RMS it runs above that rating. TI measured 95 °C on the inductor at 20 A with a heat sink fitted (TIDT427A §2.3.2).

  • Slew rate. A larger L lowers the ripple but also the current slew rate the controller can command, (Vin−Vo)/L(V_{\mathrm{in}}-V_o)/L(Vin​−Vo​)/L, which limits the load-step response (§7.3).

  • Tolerance. Würth specifies the inductance at ±20 %. At −20 %, 3.76 µH, the ripple at 60 V grows to 7.29 A and the peak to 23.65 A (ours). Across parts, DC bias and temperature the spread can reach 20–50 %. Check the design at the low end of the inductance, and use a sensitivity study (T4) for the other parameters.

Hard and soft saturation. The inductance is L=μ0μrN2Ae/leL = \mu_0\mu_r N^2 A_e/l_eL=μ0​μr​N2Ae​/le​, with NNN turns, core area AeA_eAe​ and magnetic path length lel_ele​. A ferrite core has μr\mu_rμr​ of about 3000–5000. When it saturates, μr\mu_rμr​ falls towards 1 within a small increase of current. The inductance collapses, the current slope vL/Lv_L/LvL​/L rises steeply, and the current runs away within a few cycles until the current limit acts or the part overheats. With a gapped ferrite core, therefore, keep the peak below the saturation current, with margin.

A metal-composite core, like TI’s, has a distributed gap. Its inductance falls gradually, so a peak slightly past the 10 % point costs ripple, not a collapse. Saturation usually occurs in transients, not in steady state: at start-up, in a short circuit or after a fast duty step, when the volt-seconds do not balance yet. T3 simulates this case.

TI chose, TI measured. TI chose a 4.7 µH Würth 74439370047 (WE-XHMI, shielded metal composite). Our ripple ratio of 0.30 at 60 V asks for 4.571 µH. TI’s 4.7 µH is a standard value just above it. The ripple at 60 V, 5.836 A, is therefore slightly below our target.

TI did not measure the inductor current. §9 checks the ripple it causes at the output and the input. From here on the lesson continues with TI’s 4.7 µH.

3.4 What if we allow 10% or 100% current ripple?

Our 30% target is a design choice. To see its consequences, compare two extremes. Here ripple means peak-to-peak inductor current divided by the 20 A full-load current. It is not duty ratio. Keep 60 V input, 12 V output and 350 kHz fixed. Use the ideal CCM equations of §3.1–3.2.

Before calculating, predict which choice needs more inductance. Which one gives the capacitor more work?

Quantity at full load 10% ripple 100% ripple
Peak-to-peak inductor ripple 2 A 20 A
Required ideal inductance 13.71 µH 1.371 µH

Inductor RMS current 20.008 A 20.817 A
Output-capacitor RMS current 0.577 A 5.774 A
Current rise rate during high-side ON 3.50 A/µs 35.0 A/µs

Choose 10% when ripple-current stress matters. Peak current and capacitor RMS current are lower. The cost is ten times more inductance. At the same applied voltage, current changes ten times more slowly. Check the load-step response before accepting that cost.

Choose 100% when less inductance and faster current change justify the ripple. The capacitor carries ten times more RMS ripple current, and the inductor must handle a 30 A peak. This remains CCM at full load: the valley is still 10 A. It is not boundary conduction.

Less inductance does not by itself guarantee a smaller or more efficient part. Compare actual saturation curves, winding and core losses, capacitor losses and transient response. With diode emulation, reduced load can also move the high-ripple design into DCM sooner.

Is low ripple always more efficient? No. It depends on which loss dominates. Low ripple lowers the inductor core and AC losses and the output-capacitor RMS current. It barely changes the switch conduction loss, because IL,rmsI_{L,\mathrm{rms}}IL,rms​ stays close to IoI_oIo​ (20.008 A against 20.817 A in the table). It also barely changes the input-capacitor stress. At 60 V that RMS current is 8.00 A at 10 % ripple and 8.41 A at 100 % ripple (ours), because the pulsed input current dominates it. In industry the final choice is usually cost, weighed against efficiency and volume.

Our decision. Keep 30% for the base design and continue with TI’s 4.7 µH. The 100% alternative requires relaxing that ripple target. The 10% alternative reduces ripple, but its larger inductance still needs a transient check. Neither alternative is a qualified replacement part.
