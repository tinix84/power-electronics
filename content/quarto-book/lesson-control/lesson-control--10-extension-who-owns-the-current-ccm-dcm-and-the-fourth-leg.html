10. Extension: who owns the current? CCM, DCM and the fourth leg

This extension is not part of the 90-minute route. It answers a question from the lecture: why does the switching not set the output current of a buck in CCM? It then applies the answer to a converter where this question decides the design, the fourth leg that balances a split DC link. All numbers come from the Example script scripts/current_ownership.py, and its checks are in current-checks.json.

10.1 The question

Two intuitive answers are common. First, in CCM the inductor and the capacitor exchange only a ripple of energy in each period, so they act as a filter and not as an energy store. Second, the impedance of the inductor at DC is zero, so the inductor cannot limit the DC current. Both answers point in the right direction. Neither shows why the load, and not the inductor, sets the current. The averaged equations show it.

Predict. A buck runs in CCM at D = 0.5. Double the inductance. Does the output current change?

10.2 In CCM the inductor drops out of the operating point

Take the averaged buck (4.1) and set both derivatives to zero:

Vo=DVin,IL=Io=DVinR(10.1)V_o = DV_{\mathrm{in}},\qquad I_L = I_o = \frac{DV_{\mathrm{in}}}{R}\tag{10.1}Vo​=DVin​,IL​=Io​=RDVin​​(10.1)

L and C are gone. They set the ripple and the transient, not the operating point. The Lesson 1 teaching buck at D = 0.5 and 2.4 Ω gives 24 V and 10 A for every inductance that keeps it in CCM.

The inductor still stores energy, EL=LiL2/2E_L = Li_L^2/2EL​=LiL2​/2. In periodic steady state its current is the same at the start and at the end of each period, so the energy that it takes in one period it also returns:

ΔEL=∫0TsvL iL dt=0(10.2)\Delta E_L = \int_0^{T_s} v_L\,i_L\,dt = 0\tag{10.2}ΔEL​=∫0Ts​​vL​iL​dt=0(10.2)

The input supplies the net power. The inductor sets how fast its current can change, not the value at which the current settles.

The impedance argument agrees, with one limit. The 60 µH inductor has zero impedance at DC and 37.7 Ω at 100 kHz, against a 2.4 Ω load. It passes the DC current and blocks the ripple. But impedance belongs to a linear time-invariant network, and the switch network is time-varying. The argument holds only for the averaged model of §3. For an inverter, the filter reactance at the fundamental is small, not zero: the 1 mH of §5 has 0.31 Ω at 50 Hz, against a 50 Ω load.

In CCM the inductor current is a state with memory. Its value at the start of a period is its value at the end of the previous one. A controller can still set it. The duty-to-inductor-current transfer function of the buck is

GiLd(s)=Vin(Cs+1/R)LCs2+(L/R)s+1(10.3)G_{i_Ld}(s) = \frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}(Cs + 1/R)}{LCs^2 + (L/R)s + 1}\tag{10.3}GiL​d​(s)=LCs2+(L/R)s+1Vin​(Cs+1/R)​(10.3)

and current-mode control is built on it. Without a current loop, the output current follows the output voltage through the load:

Giod(s)=Vin/RLCs2+(L/R)s+1(10.4)G_{i_od}(s) = \frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}/R}{LCs^2 + (L/R)s + 1}\tag{10.4}Gio​d​(s)=LCs2+(L/R)s+1Vin​/R​(10.4)

10.3 In DCM the current resets in every period

In DCM the inductor current returns to zero before the next period starts. The peak current, the falling interval D2TsD_2T_sD2​Ts​ and the average current are

Ipk=(Vin−Vo)DLfs,D2=D Vin−VoVo,Io=Ipk2(D+D2)(10.5)I_{pk} = \frac{(V_{\mathrm{in}} - V_o)D}{Lf_s},\qquad D_2 = D\,\frac{V_{\mathrm{in}} - V_o}{V_o},\qquad I_o = \frac{I_{pk}}{2}(D + D_2)\tag{10.5}Ipk​=Lfs​(Vin​−Vo​)D​,D2​=DVo​Vin​−Vo​​,Io​=2Ipk​​(D+D2​)(10.5)

Substitute:

Io=Vin(Vin−Vo)2LfsVoD2(10.6)I_o = \frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}(V_{\mathrm{in}} - V_o)}{2Lf_sV_o}D^2\tag{10.6}Io​=2Lfs​Vo​Vin​(Vin​−Vo​)​D2(10.6)

Now L and fsf_sfs​ appear in the operating point. Each period moves one charge packet, and the duty cycle sets its size. DCM does not make the converter an ideal current source, because the current still depends on VoV_oVo​. Critical conduction mode (CrCM) is the boundary where D+D2=1D + D_2 = 1D+D2​=1.

Apply it to the Lesson 1 buck. At D = 0.5 the ripple is 2.0 A peak-to-peak, so the buck leaves CCM above 24 Ω. At 48 Ω it needs D = 0.354, not 0.5, to hold 24 V. The peak current is 1.41 A, and D2D_2D2​ = 0.354. A time-stepped switching simulation with an ideal diode settles at 24.06 V.

gd=Vin(Vin−Vo)DLfsVo,gv=−Vin2D22LfsVo2(10.7)g_d = \frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}(V_{\mathrm{in}} - V_o)D}{Lf_sV_o},\qquad g_v = -\frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}^2D^2}{2Lf_sV_o^2}\tag{10.7}gd​=Lfs​Vo​Vin​(Vin​−Vo​)D​,gv​=−2Lfs​Vo2​Vin2​D2​(10.7)

Gvd(s)=gdCs+1/R−gv(10.8)G_{vd}(s) = \frac{g_d}{Cs + 1/R - g_v}\tag{10.8}Gvd​(s)=Cs+1/R−gv​gd​​(10.8)

This plant has one pole. In the example it is at 99.5 Hz. The same L and C in CCM resonate at 2055 Hz (§4.4). 10.4 The fourth leg: a split DC link that must stay balanced

A three-phase four-wire inverter in a UPS or a vehicle-to-load system connects the load neutral to the midpoint of a split DC link. An unbalanced load draws a neutral current iNi_NiN​ from the midpoint and charges one half of the link more than the other. A fourth half-bridge drives an inductor into the midpoint and moves charge back. It must react within one tenth of a grid cycle, 2 ms at 50 Hz. The energy it moves is small, so the converter and its inductor should also be small. In practice, CCM control of such a balancer was slow and difficult, and DCM solved the problem. Why?

The switch node is at +v++v_++v+​ or −v−-v_-−v−​ relative to the midpoint. With a stiff total link, v−=(Vdc−e)/2v_- = (V_{\mathrm{dc}} - e)/2v−​=(Vdc​−e)/2, and the averaged model is

The denominator has no first-order term. The ideal balancing plant has a pair of poles on the imaginary axis. All damping must come from losses, from the load or from the controller. With the teaching values the resonance is at 112.5 Hz. The neutral current of a nonlinear load contains the triplen harmonics, and its 150 Hz component is close. Zhao et al. report the 180° phase change at this resonance as a source of instability. They stabilize the loop with inductor-current feedback and a feedforward of the neutral current.

10.5 In CCM the ripple dwarfs the useful current

CCM is not slow. The largest current slope is (Vdc/2)/L(V_{\mathrm{dc}}/2)/L(Vdc​/2)/L = 4 A/µs. A properly designed current loop is much faster than 2 ms. With an inner current loop, the outer plant is

e^(s)i^L(s)=−1Cs(10.11)\frac{\hat e(s)}{\hat i_L(s)} = -\frac{1}{Cs}\tag{10.11}i^L​(s)e^(s)​=−Cs1​(10.11)

and this plant is simple to control. The difficulty is elsewhere. With complementary PWM at D = 0.5, the inductor sees Vdc/2V_{\mathrm{dc}}/2Vdc​/2 for half a period:

ΔiL,pp=Vdc4Lfs(10.12)\Delta i_{L,pp} = \frac{V_{\mathrm{dc}}}{4Lf_s}\tag{10.12}ΔiL,pp​=4Lfs​Vdc​​(10.12)

This is 100 A peak-to-peak around a useful average of 2 A. The peak current is 52 A, and the RMS current is 28.9 A. The current loop must measure a 2 A average inside a 100 A triangle.

The memory of the CCM current makes small errors grow. Around balance, iˉ˙L=(Vdc/L)(d−1/2)\dot{\bar i}_L = (V_{\mathrm{dc}}/L)(d - 1/2)iˉ˙L​=(Vdc​/L)(d−1/2). A duty error of 0.1 percentage points, from dead time, PWM resolution or sampling, gives a slope of 8000 A/s: 8 A after 1 ms, four times the useful current. In CCM the duty cycle sets the rate of change of the current, so every duty error integrates.

10.7 The charge to move is the same in both modes

The capacitors fix the charge. A correction of Δe\Delta eΔe within Δt\Delta tΔt needs the average current

I=C ΔeΔt(10.17)I = \frac{C\,\Delta e}{\Delta t}\tag{10.17}I=ΔtCΔe​(10.17)

in CCM and in DCM. With 10 mF per half, 10 V within 2 ms needs 50 A, which is exactly the CrCM limit at these values. At 2 A the same 10 V take 50 ms. A faster or larger correction needs a lower switching frequency or a smaller inductance, both with a higher peak current, or a return to CCM. DCM changes how the current is made and controlled. It does not reduce the charge that must move.

Ask. Must the fourth leg carry the full neutral current of an unbalanced load, or only correct the imbalance of the two capacitors? These are different converters. The first carries tens of amperes continuously, and CCM with an inner current loop suits it. The second moves small charges at intervals, and DCM charge packets suit it.
