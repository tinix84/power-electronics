12. Training: problem-driven exercises

Start each exercise by making a prediction. Sketch the current path or waveform before calculating. Then choose a model and solve the problem. Compare the alternatives and explain which one you would use.

Try the problem before reading its solution. For every exercise, state your choice, one benefit and one cost. Compare a credible alternative and name the condition or measurement that would change your choice. Keep the operating point and requirements clear.

  • T1–T2 are worked here, all seven parts included.
  • E1–E4 are assigned Exercises. This lesson shows their first four parts. Their Solution, the last three parts, is held in the Enrolled companion lesson and released after the deadline.

Operating points. T1 works the boost of the lecture deck with its parasitic elements. T2 is the 2025 course exercise U2, a teaching boost from 10–16 V to 24 V. E1–E3 use their own authored values. E4 is the 2025 course exercise U3, the PLECS boost the lecturer assigned in the lecture of 2026-10-30. None of them uses PMP21274’s numbers. Every Problem states its own operating point; never carry a number from one operating point to another.

The exercises go beyond the live route. Session 3 works one or two of them in class; the rest are assigned as reading.

T1 — How far can a real boost step up?

Problem

The ideal boost promises any ratio: M=1/(1−D)M = 1/(1-D)M=1/(1−D) grows without limit as D→1D \to 1D→1. The real boost of the deck (slide 8) does not. At which duty does MMM peak, what are MMM and the efficiency η\etaη there, and at which MMM would you stop using CCM? Derive the answer from the model of Appendix C, then decide.

Operating point. The numerical example of slide 8: VinV_{\mathrm{in}}Vin​ = 10 V, resistive load RRR = 10 Ω, constant diode drop VDV_DVD​ = 0.7 V, inductor winding resistance RLR_LRL​ = 40 mΩ, MOSFET on-resistance RDSonR_{\mathrm{DSon}}RDSon​ = 60 mΩ. CCM and small ripple are assumed. The slide gives no LLL and no fsf_sfs​. Solution A checks what CCM needs. This is a teaching model, not PMP21274. Never carry its numbers to Part I, or the reverse.

Predict before calculating. Sketch M(D)M(D)M(D) for this boost. Guess the duty of the peak and the efficiency there. Then name the efficiency you would accept and read the highest MMM it permits.

Why we do it

In the ideal model the duty sets the ratio. In a real boost the duty also sets the input current, and the input current sets the losses. A designer who reads M=1/(1−D)M = 1/(1-D)M=1/(1−D) and sets DDD = 0.95 for a ratio of 20 gets a ratio of about 4 and a hot board. The lecture of 2026-10-30 called the unlimited ideal gain a promise from Santa Claus. This exercise puts a number on that remark.

The peak also matters for control. On the far side of the peak, a larger duty gives a lower output. A voltage loop that raises DDD to raise VoV_oVo​ then drives the duty to its limit.

What matters

  • For a fixed load, the input current is Iin=Io/(1−D)I_{\mathrm{in}} = I_o/(1-D)Iin​=Io​/(1−D), and IoI_oIo​ itself rises with MMM. The losses in RLR_LRL​ and RDSonR_{\mathrm{DSon}}RDSon​ grow with Iin2I_{\mathrm{in}}^2Iin2​.
  • The ideal boost presents the load to the source as (1−D)2R(1-D)^2R(1−D)2R. The series resistances compete with this reflected resistance, which falls quickly as D→1D \to 1D→1.
  • In this model the output power is VoIoV_oI_oVo​Io​ and the input power is VinIinV_{\mathrm{in}}I_{\mathrm{in}}Vin​Iin​, so η=M(1−D)\eta = M(1-D)η=M(1−D) (Appendix C).
  • Model limits: CCM, constant diode drop, no switching loss, no ripple in the I2I^2I2 terms. A real board adds switching loss at VoV_oVo​, so its peak is lower.

Where it appears in practice

  • PMP21274 at 12 V input (Part I, §2): DDD = 0.7778 for MMM = 4.5. The low-side switch and the inductor carry the input current, and §8 shows what it costs.
  • Photovoltaic and fuel-cell front ends. A low source voltage is boosted to a DC bus. The ratio and the source current set the efficiency.
  • High-ratio supplies at light load, such as LED strings, piezo drivers or detector bias supplies. They reach large ratios because their load current is small.

Problem

A boost converter must supply 24 V at a nominal 120 W from a 10–16 V source at 50 kHz. Why we do it

A periodic steady state has no net change of inductor current over a switching period. Charging a capacitor changes the operating point continuously. A duty cycle that balances the inductor at the target can produce a large positive current increment earlier in the charge.

The diagnostic task is to distinguish useful charging from uncontrolled current accumulation. CCM itself is not a fault, and a zero-current start alone does not bound the peak of the next pulse.

What matters

  • Label capacitor voltage, inductor current and switch state on the same time axis. Compare successive valleys as well as peaks.
  • Keep the input-connection transient separate from controlled charging after precharge.
  • State whether duty, peak current, frequency or average power is held fixed before comparing modes.
  • Distinguish current limiting from saturation. A protection trip is an event in the controller; saturation changes the magnetic component’s current slope.
  • Treat the frozen-voltage calculation as a local model. Estimate its voltage-change error before extending it to the whole charge.

Where it appears in practice

  • Conduction and switching losses follow RMS current, blocking voltage and switched energy. A smaller duty-cycle number alone changes none of them.
  • Follow the AC current loop of each capacitor back to its source. A different capacitor reference can move ripple current to another component.
  • Keep the voltage contribution of the source separate from the power that a converter stage processes.

Where it appears in practice

Series partial-power converters appear in photovoltaic strings, battery chargers and electric-vehicle fast-charging stations. In these applications the input voltage already supplies most of the output voltage. Wind and fuel-cell sources with a large DC offset raise the same question. A converter stacked on its own input is a real partial-power stage only if a stress analysis shows lower component loading.
