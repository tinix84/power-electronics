10. A switched RC: time-domain envelopes and Fourier harmonics

10.1 Define the circuit before calling it a buck without L

An ideal two-position switch alternately connects to VinV_{\mathrm{in}}Vin​ and zero. It feeds a series R and shunt C. Assume negligible loading:

The two-position switch is shown beside the continuous-input RC in section 9. The solid switch arm marks the high state; the dashed arm marks the alternative zero-volt state. They represent alternative positions, not simultaneous connections.

This is a useful dissipative PWM-to-voltage filter, resembling a buck whose energy-transfer inductance has been replaced by resistance. The low state is actively connected to zero. A switch that merely opens during the off interval is a different circuit. With a finite resistive load, the DC gain becomes RL/(R+RL)R_L/(R+R_L)RL​/(R+RL​) and the time constant becomes (R∥RL)C(R\parallel R_L)C(R∥RL​)C.

Choose Vin=5V_{\mathrm{in}}=5Vin​=5 V, D = 0.4, switching frequency 100 kHz, R = 10 Ω and C = 10 µF. Then:

Ts=10 μs,τ=100 μs,fc=12πτ≈1.592 kHz.T_s=10\ \mathrm{\mu s},\qquad \tau=100\ \mathrm{\mu s},\qquad f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}\approx1.592\ \mathrm{kHz}.Ts​=10 μs,τ=100 μs,fc​=2πτ1​≈1.592 kHz.

10.2 Exact piecewise time-domain response

During the high interval, starting at vmin⁡v_{\min}vmin​:

vo(t)=Vin+(vmin⁡−Vin)e−t/τ.v_o(t)=V_{\mathrm{in}}+(v_{\min}-V_{\mathrm{in}})e^{-t/\tau}.vo​(t)=Vin​+(vmin​−Vin​)e−t/τ.

During the low interval, measuring local time from the falling edge:

vo(t)=vmax⁡e−t/τ.v_o(t)=v_{\max}e^{-t/\tau}.vo​(t)=vmax​e−t/τ.

Both intervals have the same RC time constant in this circuit. There are upper and lower cycle envelopes, rather than two independent time constants. Define:

a=e−DTs/τ,b=e−(1−D)Ts/τ.a=e^{-DT_s/\tau},\qquad b=e^{-(1-D)T_s/\tau}.a=e−DTs​/τ,b=e−(1−D)Ts​/τ.

At periodic steady state:

vmax⁡=Vin1−a1−ab,vmin⁡=b vmax⁡.v_{\max}=V_{\mathrm{in}}\frac{1-a}{1-ab},\qquad v_{\min}=b\,v_{\max}.vmax​=Vin​1−ab1−a​,vmin​=bvmax​.

Δvpp=Vin(1−a)(1−b)1−ab.\Delta v_{\mathrm{pp}}=V_{\mathrm{in}}\frac{(1-a)(1-b)}{1-ab}.Δvpp​=Vin​1−ab(1−a)(1−b)​.

The example gives minimum 1.9402 V, maximum 2.0602 V, mean 2.0000 V, and ripple 119.98 mV peak-to-peak.

For cycle-start samples, starting from arbitrary v0v_0v0​:

vmin⁡,n=vmin⁡,∞+(v0−vmin⁡,∞)e−nTs/τ.v_{\min,n}=v_{\min,\infty}+(v_0-v_{\min,\infty})e^{-nT_s/\tau}.vmin,n​=vmin,∞​+(v0​−vmin,∞​)e−nTs​/τ.

The maxima follow a corresponding exponential envelope at the end of each high interval. The slow settling comes from the RC pole; the fast repetition is imposed by switching.

10.3 The same response in the frequency domain

Fourier analysis describes frequency content. For suitable signals the continuous transform is:

X(jω)=∫−∞∞x(t)e−jωt dt.X(j\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt.X(jω)=∫−∞∞​x(t)e−jωtdt.

A periodic waveform is conveniently described by a Fourier series: a DC term plus harmonics at integer multiples of its fundamental frequency. Its transform can be interpreted as a line spectrum. For a stable LTI system, each sinusoidal component is multiplied by the corresponding complex frequency response: its magnitude changes and its phase shifts.

Evaluating a Laplace-domain transfer function on the imaginary axis gives that response where a bounded sinusoidal steady state exists.

For a unipolar rectangular pulse train, each high interval starting at the cycle boundary, the DC component and nth harmonic peak amplitude are:

V0=DVin,An,in=2Vinnπ∣sin⁡(nπD)∣.V_0=DV_{\mathrm{in}},\qquad A_{n,\mathrm{in}}=\frac{2V_{\mathrm{in}}}{n\pi}|\sin(n\pi D)|.V0​=DVin​,An,in​=nπ2Vin​​∣sin(nπD)∣.

Each harmonic has its own phase. Its output peak amplitude is:

An,out=An,in∣HRC(jnωs)∣,ωs=2πfs.A_{n,\mathrm{out}}=A_{n,\mathrm{in}}\left|H_{RC}(jn\omega_s)\right|,\qquad \omega_s=2\pi f_s.An,out​=An,in​∣HRC​(jnωs​)∣,ωs​=2πfs​.

∣HRC(jnωs)∣=11+(nωsRC)2.\left|H_{RC}(jn\omega_s)\right|=\frac1{\sqrt{1+(n\omega_sRC)^2}}.∣HRC​(jnωs​)∣=1+(nωs​RC)2​1​.

For the first harmonic, input peak amplitude is 3.027 V, attenuation magnitude is 0.01591, and output peak amplitude is approximately 48.18 mV. DC plus this harmonic predicts 96.35 mV peak-to-peak, which is smaller than the exact 119.98 mV because higher harmonics still shape the waveform. This is a useful exercise in the limits of a first-harmonic approximation.

The complete response consists of a periodic forced solution plus the decaying natural response. In this linear circuit their difference from any other solution with the same input decays as exp(−t/τ). A switching harmonic is a forced frequency, not a second settling pole.

Power-electronics connection: the resistor dissipates energy while charging and discharging the capacitor. At high switching frequency relative to the RC corner, this example dissipates approximately Vin2D(1−D)/R=0.60V_{\mathrm{in}}^2D(1-D)/R=0.60Vin2​D(1−D)/R=0.60 W even without an external load. Adding an energy-storage inductor changes the efficiency tradeoff fundamentally.

10.4 FFT: reading a sampled switching waveform

An FFT is an efficient algorithm for computing a discrete Fourier transform. It analyses a finite set of samples; it is not a separate physical transform or a transfer function. Distinguish the acquisition sampling frequency from the converter’s switching frequency.

For N uniformly spaced samples taken at acquisition rate fₐ:

Trecord=Nfa,Δf=faN=1Trecord.T_{\mathrm{record}}=\frac{N}{f_a},\qquad \Delta f=\frac{f_a}{N}=\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{record}}}.Trecord​=fa​N​,Δf=Nfa​​=Trecord​1​.

The record duration sets the spacing between FFT bins. A longer record gives finer bin spacing; increasing the sample rate at fixed duration does not. Zero-padding interpolates the sampled spectrum but does not resolve two tones that the original record cannot separate.

Apply it to the PWM example: acquire at 10 MS/s for 1 ms. There are 10,000 samples, 1 kHz bin spacing and exactly 100 periods of the 100 kHz switching waveform. The first switching harmonic is at bin 100. Compare its properly normalized peak amplitude with the analytical 3.027 V at the input and approximately 48.18 mV at the RC output.

Finite sampling represents the continuous waveform approximately; the analytical coefficients remain the reference.

For the usual unnormalized DFT, a coherent real sinusoid whose positive-frequency bin is k has peak amplitude:

Ak,peak=2∣X[k]∣N.A_{k,\mathrm{peak}}=\frac{2|X[k]|}{N}.Ak,peak​=N2∣X[k]∣​.

The DC bin uses |X[0]|/N. Do not double the Nyquist bin for an even-length real record. Divide a sinusoidal peak amplitude by √2 to obtain RMS. Do not apply this sine conversion to the total time-domain square-wave peak.

Four practical checks make an FFT meaningful:

  • Aliasing: components above the acquisition Nyquist frequency can fold into the record. Use an appropriate analogue bandwidth/anti-alias filter; sampling above twice the switching frequency alone does not capture all switching harmonics or edge ringing.
  • Leakage: a non-integer number of periods spreads a tone across bins. Coherent acquisition or a suitable window helps, with a main-lobe-width tradeoff.
  • Scaling: a window changes coherent tone gain and noise bandwidth. Apply the relevant normalization, and state whether the result is peak amplitude, RMS, power spectral density or dB relative to a reference.
  • Operating state: exclude startup if the goal is a periodic steady-state spectrum. The startup envelope otherwise contributes to the low-frequency content.

The NumPy FFT reference specifies its frequency ordering and normalization conventions. These conventions must also be checked when using an oscilloscope or another software package.

Choose the FFT record. A settled, coherent record makes harmonic amplitudes easier to interpret. A startup record retains transient information but mixes it with the forced response. Choose according to the question; changing the window does not change the circuit poles.

10.5 FFT as a tool for real-power analysis

An FFT can show which frequency components transfer average power. Acquire voltage and current at the same port, with synchronized samples, consistent probe polarity and a known channel delay. Use current into the positive-voltage terminal for absorbed power. Relative phase matters: multiplying only the voltage and current spectrum magnitudes loses the information that distinguishes real power from reactive exchange.

For a coherent periodic record, define complex peak phasors for each nonzero harmonic k as twice the corresponding unnormalized FFT bin divided by N. Retain their phases. Write the associated sinusoids as:

A harmonic with a 90° voltage–current phase difference contributes zero real power, even when both spectral amplitudes are large. A negative Pk indicates net returned power at that harmonic under the chosen sign convention. Section 6 explains why reactive current can nevertheless cause resistive heating elsewhere.

What does a spectral peak mean? The voltage/current amplitude spectra in section 10.4 use peak sinusoidal amplitudes, in V and A. Their RMS equivalents are smaller by √2. A plot of Pk instead shows average watts contributed by each harmonic, calculated from RMS phasors or equivalently half the conjugate product of peak phasors. Its line height is neither peak instantaneous power nor “RMS power.” Label the axes explicitly:

Worked check. Let v(t) = 12 + 2 cos(ωt) V and i(t) = 1 + 3 cos(ωt − 60°) A. The DC contribution is 12 W. The fundamental contributes (2 × 3 / 2) cos(60°) = 1.5 W, giving 13.5 W total. Multiplying the peak magnitudes alone gives 6 VA and misses both the factor 1/2 and the phase.

Multiplying the RMS magnitudes alone gives 3 VA; the phase factor is still needed.

Cross-check against the time record. For the usual unnormalized full, two-sided DFTs Xv and Xi, discrete Parseval gives an exact identity for the sampled record:

This full-spectrum expression needs no extra one-sided factor. If positive and negative bins are combined, double only the interior positive-frequency cross-products; DC and the Nyquist bin (for even N) remain undoubled. The peak-phasor 1/2 rule above applies to resolved sinusoids strictly below Nyquist. Leakage spreads a harmonic across bins, and windowing changes the weighting: do not apply a single-tone amplitude correction blindly to a broadband power estimate.

Check channel timing, bandwidth and spectral scaling, and compare the final spectral sum with mean(v × i).

Finally, FFT(v × i) is a different spectrum: it describes the DC and oscillating components of instantaneous power p(t), with its DC component equal to average power. Time-domain multiplication corresponds to frequency-domain convolution, whereas the conjugate cross-product above decomposes average power by voltage/current frequency. A peak in FFT(p) at twice a sinusoid’s frequency is power pulsation, not an additional average-power contribution.

10.6 A pulse train needs both a spectral view and an energy budget

A periodic pulse load is not characterised by its repetition frequency alone. For current alternating between Ilow and Ihigh, with period T, pulse duration tp and duty ratio D = tp/T, its mean is Ilow + (Ihigh − Ilow)D. The nonzero peak harmonic amplitudes are:

I^n=2(Ihigh−Ilow)D∣sin⁡(πnD)πnD∣,fn=nT.\widehat I_n=2(I_{\mathrm{high}}-I_{\mathrm{low}})D\left|\frac{\sin(\pi nD)}{\pi nD}\right|,\qquad f_n=\frac{n}{T}.In​=2(Ihigh​−Ilow​)D​πnDsin(πnD)​​,fn​=Tn​.

An ideal infinite periodic train has discrete spectral lines, not a continuous white spectrum. Those lines can occupy a broad frequency range: pulse duration sets a sinc envelope, with its first null at 1/tp, while edge duration controls additional high-frequency roll-off. An ideal rectangular edge has no finite bandwidth; real finite edges suppress the higher harmonics without introducing a universal hard cutoff.

A finite burst broadens the lines through its time window, and slow workload modulation introduces additional low-frequency content. Use §10.4’s coherent sampling/scaling checks before interpreting an FFT.

Filtering and energy buffering answer connected but different questions. Impedance and transfer functions determine ripple, current sharing and propagation of disturbances. If upstream power is deliberately prevented from following the load, an energy store must supply the difference. Define positive cumulative deficit as energy withdrawn from the buffer, with all powers referred to the same port/conversion boundary:

Edef(t)=∫0t[Pload(u)−Psource(u)] du.E_{\mathrm{def}}(t)=\int_0^t[P_{\mathrm{load}}(u)-P_{\mathrm{source}}(u)]\,du.Edef​(t)=∫0t​[Pload​(u)−Psource​(u)]du.

For a lossless repeating cycle with no energy drift, the minimum usable storage swing is max(Edef) − min(Edef). Repetition rate and pulse width matter through both the spectrum and the area under the power deficit. If average supply power is insufficient, cumulative deficit grows cycle after cycle; no finite capacitor fixes that indefinitely.

An LC filter already stores energy. It can also perform buffering if its energy and allowed voltage/current excursions are sufficient. The point is not that one must always add a battery to a filter, but that an EMC attenuation calculation alone does not qualify millisecond-scale energy delivery, hold-up or recharge.
