6. Active power, reactive power and heating

6.1 Use average current when voltage is constant

P=⟨vi⟩.P=\langle vi\rangle.P=⟨vi⟩.

When the port voltage is constant at U:

P=UIavg.P=U I_{\mathrm{avg}}.P=UIavg​.

Example: an ideal 12 V input bus supplies 20 A rectangular pulses at 25% duty. The average current is 5 A, so the average input power is 60 W. Its current RMS is 10 A; multiplying 12 V by 10 A would incorrectly give 120 W. This is an input-energy calculation at a constant-voltage port.

A diode approximated by a constant 0.7 V forward drop, conducting those same pulses, dissipates approximately 3.5 W, using 0.7 V × 5 A. If the forward characteristic is instead modelled as a fixed drop plus resistance, both average and RMS current enter the loss calculation.

In general:

⟨vi⟩=VavgIavg+⟨v~ i~⟩.\langle vi\rangle=V_{\mathrm{avg}}I_{\mathrm{avg}}+\langle\widetilde v\,\widetilde i\rangle.⟨vi⟩=Vavg​Iavg​+⟨vi⟩.

Do not multiply averages when correlated voltage and current ripple make the second term significant.

For periodic waveforms, section 10.5 calculates the same average power from synchronized voltage/current FFTs, retaining phase and distinguishing peak from RMS spectral scaling.

6.2 Use RMS current for ohmic heating

For a constant resistance:

PR=R⟨i2⟩=RIrms2.P_R=R\langle i^2\rangle=R I_{\mathrm{rms}}^2.PR​=R⟨i2⟩=RIrms2​.

With a 20 mΩ conductor or on-state MOSFET resistance, the 20 A / 25% pulses cause:

PR=0.020×102=2.0 W.P_R=0.020\times10^2=2.0\ \mathrm W.PR​=0.020×102=2.0 W.

A constant 5 A current has the same average but causes only 0.50 W in that resistance. Pulsing increases heating by a factor of four here. A capacitor’s current can have zero mean while its ESR still dissipates power; for 2 A RMS and 50 mΩ ESR the loss is 0.20 W.

At high frequency, resistance and ESR may vary with frequency and temperature, so harmonic loss calculation may be necessary.

6.3 Active power is not synonymous with loss

Active power is net average energy transfer. It may become useful mechanical power, charge a battery, or become unwanted heat. Converter losses are the unwanted part of this real-power balance:

Pin=Puseful+Ploss,η=PusefulPin.P_{\mathrm{in}}=P_{\mathrm{useful}}+P_{\mathrm{loss}},\qquad \eta=\frac{P_{\mathrm{useful}}}{P_{\mathrm{in}}}.Pin​=Puseful​+Ploss​,η=Pin​Puseful​​.

In periodic operation an ideal inductor or capacitor returns the energy it stores over a cycle. Its net average power is zero, despite nonzero instantaneous power and potentially large RMS current.

6.4 Why reactive current still increases losses

For sinusoidal voltage and current, with lag angle φ:

P=VrmsIrmscos⁡φ,Q=VrmsIrmssin⁡φ.P=V_{\mathrm{rms}}I_{\mathrm{rms}}\cos\varphi,\qquad Q=V_{\mathrm{rms}}I_{\mathrm{rms}}\sin\varphi.P=Vrms​Irms​cosφ,Q=Vrms​Irms​sinφ.

S=VrmsIrms,S2=P2+Q2.S=V_{\mathrm{rms}}I_{\mathrm{rms}},\qquad S^2=P^2+Q^2.S=Vrms​Irms​,S2=P2+Q2.

Reactive power describes cyclic exchange in the ideal reactive part; it is not itself dissipated there. For fixed active power and voltage, the required current rises as power factor falls:

Irms=PVrmscos⁡φ,Pline=RloopP2Vrms2cos⁡2φ.I_{\mathrm{rms}}=\frac{P}{V_{\mathrm{rms}}\cos\varphi},\qquad P_{\mathrm{line}}=R_{\mathrm{loop}}\frac{P^2}{V_{\mathrm{rms}}^2\cos^2\varphi}.Irms​=Vrms​cosφP​,Pline​=Rloop​Vrms2​cos2φP2​.

Example: 2.30 kW at 230 V RMS requires 10 A at unity power factor. At power factor 0.5 it requires 20 A. With 0.20 Ω total line-loop resistance, losses rise from 20 W to 80 W. The load still receives 2.30 kW; the conductors and source carry more current.

For distorted waveforms use true power factor:

PF=PVrmsIrms.\mathrm{PF}=\frac{P}{V_{\mathrm{rms}}I_{\mathrm{rms}}}.PF=Vrms​Irms​P​.

With purely sinusoidal voltage, this equals displacement factor cos φ₁ times the fundamental-current RMS divided by total-current RMS. Harmonic current can therefore lower power factor even when the fundamental current is in phase. The simple sinusoidal P/Q/S triangle is not a complete decomposition of arbitrary distorted waveforms.

THD, displacement factor and true power factor

For periodic AC without DC, define total harmonic distortion relative to the fundamental RMS component:

THDI=∑k=2∞Ik,rms2I1,rms,THDV=∑k=2∞Vk,rms2V1,rms.\mathrm{THD}_I=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}I_{k,\mathrm{rms}}^2}}{I_{1,\mathrm{rms}}},\qquad \mathrm{THD}_V=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}V_{k,\mathrm{rms}}^2}}{V_{1,\mathrm{rms}}}.THDI​=I1,rms​∑k=2∞​Ik,rms2​​​,THDV​=V1,rms​∑k=2∞​Vk,rms2​​​.

Multiply these ratios by 100 for percent. This is the fundamental-referenced convention (THD-F); a total-RMS-referenced instrument setting gives a different number. State the harmonic range and measurement bandwidth. Switching ripple, interharmonics and broadband noise are not automatically included in a line-harmonic THD number. See TI’s PFC/THD discussion.

Displacement power factor uses only the fundamental phase difference: DPF = cos φ₁. For sinusoidal voltage, and current with no DC:

PF=cos⁡φ11+THDI2.\mathrm{PF}=\frac{\cos\varphi_1}{\sqrt{1+\mathrm{THD}_I^2}}.PF=1+THDI2​​cosφ1​​.

For example, DPF = 1 and current THD = 50% give PF = 0.894, not one. With distorted voltage the shortcut above no longer applies, because matching voltage/current harmonics can also transfer active power:

P=∑k=1∞Vk,rmsIk,rmscos⁡φk,PF=PVrmsIrms.P=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}V_{k,\mathrm{rms}}I_{k,\mathrm{rms}}\cos\varphi_k,\qquad \mathrm{PF}=\frac{P}{V_{\mathrm{rms}}I_{\mathrm{rms}}}.P=k=1∑∞​Vk,rms​Ik,rms​cosφk​,PF=Vrms​Irms​P​.

Section 10.5 obtains these signed harmonic powers from synchronized FFTs. DPF, true PF, voltage THD and current THD answer different questions; report all four when assessing a distorted supply.

Why a high PF can hide distortion: the conductance-controlled PFC

A common PFC strategy commands a switching-cycle-averaged line current proportional to the sensed instantaneous line voltage:

iref(t)=Gvline(t).i_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)=Gv_{\mathrm{line}}(t).iref​(t)=Gvline​(t).

Here G is a positive conductance command, approximately constant over a line cycle, set by a slow output-voltage/power loop. With sinusoidal voltage, this produces an in-phase sinusoidal current. With distorted voltage, an ideal tracking loop copies the voltage harmonics into current: Ik = GVk, with matching phase, so THD-I = THD-V within the tracking bandwidth.

It does not need to generate new harmonic orders to cause harmonic current; real control errors and switching add further distortion. Input-voltage disturbances contaminating the current reference are also discussed in TI’s PFC guidance.

For exact proportional tracking:

P=GVrms2,Irms=GVrms,PF=1.P=G V_{\mathrm{rms}}^2,\qquad I_{\mathrm{rms}}=G V_{\mathrm{rms}},\qquad \mathrm{PF}=1.P=GVrms2​,Irms​=GVrms​,PF=1.

This is why quoting PF alone can feel like “cheating” as a power-quality claim: the number can be perfect while both waveforms contain large harmonics. The PF calculation is correct; it measures normalized voltage–current alignment, not waveform purity or harmonic compliance. Conversely, a controller that extracts the fundamental voltage and commands a clean in-phase sinusoidal current can have THD-I = 0 yet PF below one on distorted voltage.

With no DC, its PF is 1/√(1 + THD-V²).

The design objective therefore matters: proportional conductance gives minimum total RMS current for a specified total active power at a fixed terminal-voltage waveform. A clean fundamental current instead avoids copying supply harmonics. High PF, low THD and minimum frequency-weighted cable loss are related objectives, but are not interchangeable. Specify the actual waveform and harmonic-current requirements when selecting or testing PFC control.

Cable heating: count each harmonic at its own AC resistance

For a linear conductor model at a fixed temperature, with each resistance representing the complete outgoing/return path:

Pcable=∑k=1∞Ik,rms2Rloop,ac(kf1).P_{\mathrm{cable}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{k,\mathrm{rms}}^2R_{\mathrm{loop,ac}}(kf_1).Pcable​=k=1∑∞​Ik,rms2​Rloop,ac​(kf1​).

There are two effects. Adding harmonic current while holding the fundamental current fixed increases total RMS and heating even if resistance is constant. Skin and proximity effects can additionally increase AC resistance at higher frequencies, so high-order currents can cost more watts per ampere squared.

The increase depends on conductor size, arrangement and frequency; it is not necessarily large for every cable at low harmonic orders. Sections 2.7–2.8 explain the mechanisms. See also Schneider Electric’s harmonic-loss explanation.

Even identical RMS current and THD can produce different heating if their harmonic content occurs at different frequencies. PF alone does not contain the cable’s frequency-dependent resistance. At fixed total power and distorted voltage, do not assume that copying harmonics always increases heating: with frequency-independent resistance, proportional current actually minimizes it. Frequency-dependent resistance can reverse that comparison.

Worked counterexample: PF = 1 with greater cable loss

Use a prescribed 50 Hz voltage at the load port with 230 V RMS fundamental and 23 V RMS fifth harmonic, hence THD-V = 10%. Compare two ideal current-reference strategies delivering the same 2.30 kW at that port. Assume a loop resistance of 0.20 Ω at 50 Hz and 0.60 Ω at 250 Hz.

This makes the limitation precise: perfect PF does not guarantee low THD or minimum AC cable loss, but harmonics do not automatically make conductance control the worse choice under every comparison.

Wires and fuses: continuous conductor heating is principally an RMS-current issue, with additional AC skin/proximity effects where relevant. Fuse operation depends on thermal history, time-current characteristics, fault conditions and pulse I2tI^2tI2t, rather than a universal peak-current threshold. AC and DC interruption ratings are also different; DC has no natural periodic current zero.

Do not infer a DC rating by multiplying an AC fuse rating by √2. Eaton’s fuse technology guidance distinguishes current and voltage ratings; its high-speed fuse guide discusses AC/DC application limits.

Grid connection: AC made voltage transformation straightforward with transformers and remains widely used for generation and distribution. HVDC can be advantageous for long cables, long-distance transfer and asynchronous interconnections; cable charging current and reactive exchange are major differences. Converter cost and loss, insulation, protection, conductor count and topology also matter.

A two-wire peak-limit example does not establish that every AC system is oversized or compare it fairly with three-phase transmission. See ABB’s discussion of offshore HVDC.

Compare under one constraint. State the held voltage, current, insulation or conductor limit before choosing AC or DC. A benefit under one constraint may disappear under another. Use the same delivered-power requirement and loss accounting for both candidates.
