12.7 Worked choice: RC, LC or ferrite bead on a low-current supply rail?

Classroom riddle: a small IC needs a quiet supply. Must the series component be an inductor or a ferrite bead? Start with the allowed rail drop, load-current spectrum and noise frequency. A resistor may give useful attenuation with very small absolute loss when the current is low. The same reasoning that keeps linear regulators useful also keeps RC supply filters useful.

This exercise filters the power rail, not the digital data trace or the gate-driver output. Signal termination, gate resistance and supply decoupling solve different problems. A gate driver operating at 1 MHz can draw sharp supply-current pulses even though its average consumption is modest.

A. Specify what “better” means

Assume a nominal 3.3 V rail, a 100 mV DC-drop budget for the series component, and source-voltage ripple near 1 MHz. Compare the following authored models with the same 1 µF local capacitor:

Series element Parameters What it buys / what it costs
Resistor 10 Ω Predictable resistance and damping; DC drop proportional to current
Inductor 1 µH, 0.1 Ω DCR Small DC drop; second-order filtering with a resonant region
Ferrite bead 0.1 Ω DCR + parallel combination of 0.2 µH, 100 Ω and 2 pF Small DC drop; frequency-dependent reactance and dissipation

The bead equivalent is an illustrative passive model, not a measured curve, a selected 0805 product, or a fit to a manufacturer’s data. The inductor model omits its parallel capacitance; do not extrapolate its plotted response beyond the stated 10 MHz range. All output capacitors include 20 mΩ ESR and 0.5 nH total ESL. The source has 0.05 Ω resistance. PCB coupling and regulator-control dynamics are omitted.

For the ripple calculation the IC has a fixed 1 kΩ positive incremental input impedance. Its DC current is a separate parameter in the current sweep; an active IC’s incremental impedance need not equal its nominal voltage divided by DC current. These are not constant-power converter models. The curves hold component parameters fixed: they do not include bead DC-bias changes or temperature drift.

Write the voltage divider using the actual impedances:

ZC(s)=RESR+sLESL+1sC,Zp=ZC∥Zload.Z_C(s)=R_{\mathrm{ESR}}+sL_{\mathrm{ESL}}+\frac{1}{sC},\qquad Z_p=Z_C\parallel Z_{\mathrm{load}}.ZC​(s)=RESR​+sLESL​+sC1​,Zp​=ZC​∥Zload​.

H(s)=VrailVsource=ZpRs+Zseries+Zp.H(s)=\frac{V_{\mathrm{rail}}}{V_{\mathrm{source}}}=\frac{Z_p}{R_s+Z_{\mathrm{series}}+Z_p}.H(s)=Vsource​Vrail​​=Rs​+Zseries​+Zp​Zp​​.

For the illustrative bead:

Zbead(s)=RDC+(1sLb+1RAC+sCpar)−1.Z_{\mathrm{bead}}(s)=R_{\mathrm{DC}}+\left(\frac{1}{sL_b}+\frac{1}{R_{\mathrm{AC}}}+sC_{\mathrm{par}}\right)^{-1}.Zbead​(s)=RDC​+(sLb​1​+RAC​1​+sCpar​)−1.

Here the 100 Ω model resistor is not a 100 Ω DC resistance: at DC the parallel inductance bypasses it. The bead approaches its low DCR at low frequency; its real impedance grows over a higher-frequency band.

B. Predict the response before plotting it

Neglect source resistance, loading and parasitics for the first estimate:

HRC(s)=11+sRC,fc=12πRC=15.9 kHz.H_{RC}(s)=\frac{1}{1+sRC},\qquad f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi RC}=15.9\ \mathrm{kHz}.HRC​(s)=1+sRC1​,fc​=2πRC1​=15.9 kHz.

HLC(s)=11+s2LC,f0=12πLC=159 kHz.H_{LC}(s)=\frac{1}{1+s^2LC},\qquad f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}=159\ \mathrm{kHz}.HLC​(s)=1+s2LC1​,f0​=2πLC​1​=159 kHz.

The second expression is an undamped ideal approximation, not a sensible hardware design at resonance. RC has a −20 dB/decade region and no intentional LC resonance. LC eventually has a −40 dB/decade region, but a larger slope does not guarantee more attenuation at a particular frequency.

Here the RC corner is a decade below the LC natural frequency. The bead’s low-frequency inductance and 1 µF capacitor give an approximate resonance at 356 kHz.

Result from the specified nonideal models RC LC Bead + C
Voltage-noise gain at 1 MHz −36.13 dB −31.81 dB −17.02 dB
Resonant gain without extra damping None in the plotted band +15.37 dB at 158 kHz +8.46 dB at 342 kHz
Peak after adding the shown damping branch Not added +1.32 dB +1.85 dB

Thus 10 mV RMS of source-voltage ripple at 1 MHz becomes approximately 0.156 mV, 0.257 mV or 1.41 mV RMS, respectively, without the added damping branch. Positive gain near resonance means magnification relative to the input voltage; a passive resonator can magnify voltage without generating net energy. These numbers are circuit voltage ratios, not standardized 50 Ω insertion-loss measurements.

The dashed curves add 1 Ω in series with 4 µF from the rail to return, in parallel with the original 1 µF capacitor and load. This reduces the peak substantially but does not remove all peaking. Its capacitor blocks DC after charging, while its resistor dissipates AC/transient energy.

The extra capacitance, charging current and board area are part of the cost. As in section 12.6, choose damping for the actual source/load combination rather than copying a universal value.

A bead’s AC loss is often the intended filtering mechanism. It helps when that resistive impedance lies in the noise band; it does not guarantee damping at a much lower resonance. A headline such as “600 Ω at 100 MHz” gives neither the impedance nor the attenuation at 1 MHz. Use the complex impedance versus frequency and DC bias. ADI AN-1368 explains these limitations and practical damping approaches.

C. Find the usable current range: headroom before heating

For the series component under DC load:

ΔV=IDCR,PDC=IDC2R.\Delta V=I_{\mathrm{DC}}R,\qquad P_{\mathrm{DC}}=I_{\mathrm{DC}}^2R.ΔV=IDC​R,PDC​=IDC2​R.

DC current 10 Ω resistor: drop / loss 0.1 Ω magnetic DCR: drop / loss
1 mA 10 mV / 0.010 mW 0.1 mV / 0.0001 mW
5 mA 50 mV / 0.25 mW 0.5 mV / 0.0025 mW
10 mA 100 mV / 1 mW 1 mV / 0.010 mW
100 mA 1 V / 100 mW 10 mV / 1 mW

For the requested package comparison, assume each candidate has a usable 0.125 W dissipation allowance on the chosen board. This is a teaching constraint, not a universal 0805 rating. For the resistor:

Ithermal=0.125 W10 Ω=112 mA,Iheadroom=0.100 V10 Ω=10 mA.I_{\mathrm{thermal}}=\sqrt{\frac{0.125\ \mathrm W}{10\ \Omega}}=112\ \mathrm{mA},\qquad I_{\mathrm{headroom}}=\frac{0.100\ \mathrm V}{10\ \Omega}=10\ \mathrm{mA}.Ithermal​=10 Ω0.125 W​​=112 mA,Iheadroom​=10 Ω0.100 V​=10 mA.

The rail-drop requirement limits the resistor much earlier than heating does. At 10 mA it uses only 1 mW, so the absolute loss is small even though it is 100 times the magnetic component’s DC loss. There is no DC-loss crossover for these fixed resistances.

At 100 mA, being below the assumed thermal limit does not rescue a 1 V rail drop. Include the separate source/wiring drop in the complete rail-tolerance budget.

The plotted sweep extends beyond the feasible rail-drop region to expose the limits; it does not imply that a 3.3 V source can maintain an arbitrary imposed current after the load runs out of voltage headroom.

For a constant resistor carrying ripple as well as DC:

PR=R(IDC2+IAC,rms2).P_R=R\left(I_{\mathrm{DC}}^2+I_{\mathrm{AC,rms}}^2\right).PR​=R(IDC2​+IAC,rms2​).

For a linear bead model, sum each AC harmonic’s heating using its own real impedance:

Pbead=IDC2RDC+∑n≥1In,rms2Re⁡{Zbead(jωn)}.P_{\mathrm{bead}}=I_{\mathrm{DC}}^2R_{\mathrm{DC}}+\sum_{n\geq1}I_{n,\mathrm{rms}}^2\operatorname{Re}\{Z_{\mathrm{bead}}(j\omega_n)\}.Pbead​=IDC2​RDC​+n≥1∑​In,rms2​Re{Zbead​(jωn​)}.

A total-loss crossover can exist for a specified ripple spectrum and component models. It cannot be inferred from DC current or package size alone. The two plotted loss curves include DC heating only; they do not assign arbitrary core loss to make a resistor appear more efficient. Evaluate each circuit’s actual AC current, since changing the series impedance changes that current too.

An 0805 outline does not determine thermal performance by surface area alone. Ratings depend on construction, pads/copper, ambient temperature and derating. A bead also needs acceptable impedance at DC bias, and an inductor needs saturation and RMS-current checks. A resistor is often a simple, economical component, but exact cost comparisons require actual parts and quantities.

D. Does RC always need the smaller capacitor?

Exercise: require 40 dB attenuation at 1 MHz, initially ignoring loading, parasitics and tolerances. Set a voltage-amplitude target a = 0.01. For RC:

CRC≥a−2−12πfR.C_{RC}\geq\frac{\sqrt{a^{-2}-1}}{2\pi fR}.CRC​≥2πfRa−2−1​​.

At 10 Ω, this gives 1.59 µF. For an ideal LC evaluated above resonance:

CLC≥1+a−1(2πf)2L.C_{LC}\geq\frac{1+a^{-1}}{(2\pi f)^2L}.CLC​≥(2πf)2L1+a−1​.

At 1 µH this gives 2.56 µF. In this particular comparison, RC reaches the target with the smaller capacitance and avoids an intentional LC resonance. At only 1 mA, the same 100 mV drop allowance permits 100 Ω, reducing the ideal RC requirement to 0.159 µF. At 100 mA it permits only 1 Ω, increasing that requirement to 15.9 µF. This explains why RC becomes attractive at low current.

The result changes with permitted resistance, selected inductance, frequency and damping. A larger inductor can reverse the capacitance comparison. Neither formula includes capacitor DC-bias reduction, ESR/ESL or design margin. The full bead model is frequency dependent and should be evaluated through the divider, rather than assigned a single RC corner. These ideal calculations are starting estimates, not final component selections.

E. Transfer the reasoning to a 1 MHz gate-driver supply

For a separate 12 V gate-driver supply example, assume one MOSFET with 10 nC total gate charge per turn-on at the chosen gate voltage, one turn-on per period and 1 MHz operation. Neglect driver quiescent and internal switching currents initially:

Iavg≈Qgfs=10 mA.I_{\mathrm{avg}}\approx Q_g f_s=10\ \mathrm{mA}.Iavg​≈Qg​fs​=10 mA.

A 10 Ω supply resistor then causes approximately 100 mV average drop and 1 mW DC heating. A local 1 µF capacitor supplying one 10 nC event alone loses approximately:

ΔVC=QgC=10 mV.\Delta V_C=\frac{Q_g}{C}=10\ \mathrm{mV}.ΔVC​=CQg​​=10 mV.

This is the charge contribution only; add ESR/ESL effects and verify pulse recharge, startup, UVLO margin and the actual gate-charge waveform. The supply resistor carries recharge ripple, so its true RMS loss exceeds the 1 mW DC-only estimate. A 100 nC gate at the same frequency instead needs roughly 100 mA average: the same resistor loses 1 V and 100 mW before other consumption, making the choice much less attractive.

For multiple independently charged gates, add their charge per cycle. Local bypassing remains necessary with RC, LC and bead filtering.

Finish the riddle by reversing the disturbance: if the IC injects a current pulse while source noise is zero, calculate the rail driving-point impedance, not only H. With the same port definitions:

A resistor can improve incoming-noise attenuation while worsening low-frequency load-step droop. Choose RC when its headroom and transient performance meet the requirements; choose and damp an LC or bead network when low DC drop or another noise band makes it worthwhile. An LDO adds regulation and has its own headroom, noise and stability requirements, as discussed in chapter 14.
