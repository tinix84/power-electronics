16.40 The duty cycle is free to choose: where do the switch conduction losses go? — Ben-Yaakov-inspired

Source and credit: this exercise follows Sam Ben-Yaakov, The effect of PWM converters’ duty cycle on conduction losses, lecture video, 2025. The switched-inductor view, the loss expressions per topology and the DCM ratio check are his; the derivation conventions, component values, tables, figure 92 and the numerical script are authored here.

Predict. A synchronous buck delivers 12 V at 10 A. The input can be 15 V, 24 V or 48 V, so the duty cycle can be 0.8, 0.5 or 0.25. Which input gives the lowest total conduction loss in the two MOSFETs? Then answer the same question for a boost that delivers 12 V at 10 A from 3 V, 6 V or 9 V. Sketch both answers before reading on.

Sometimes the input and output voltages are given and the duty cycle follows from them. Often the designer can choose the input bus, the turns ratio of a coupled inductor, or the operating mode, and the duty cycle becomes a degree of freedom. This exercise asks what that choice does to the conduction loss of the switches. The model is deliberately simple: constant on-resistance R_on for every transistor, constant forward drop V_D for a diode, inductor ripple neglected, continuous conduction unless stated. Switching loss, ripple-dependent RMS and magnetics loss are left out; §13 and §16.8 touch on them.

The switched inductor: one circuit, three converters

Buck, boost and buck-boost are the same cell: one inductor whose free terminal is connected alternately to two switches, Q1 for a fraction D of the period and Q2 for the rest. The inductor current I_L is nearly constant. Seen from the inductor, there is always exactly one R_on in series, so the total conduction loss of the pair is:

Ptot=IL2Ron,PQ1=D Ptot,PQ2=(1−D) Ptot.P_{\mathrm{tot}}=I_L^2 R_{\mathrm{on}},\qquad P_{Q1}=D\,P_{\mathrm{tot}},\qquad P_{Q2}=(1-D)\,P_{\mathrm{tot}}.Ptot​=IL2​Ron​,PQ1​=DPtot​,PQ2​=(1−D)Ptot​.

The duty cycle only decides how P_tot is shared between the two devices. Whether P_tot itself depends on D is decided by one question: is the output port the inductor, or one of the switches?

Converter Output port I_L in terms of I_out P_tot = I_L² R_on
Buck Inductor I_out I_out² R_on, independent of D
Boost Switch Q1 I_out/(1 − D) I_out² R_on/(1 − D)²
Buck-boost Switch Q1 I_out/(1 − D) I_out² R_on/(1 − D)²

The ratio I_out/(1 − D) is the lossless power balance: Q1 conducts I_L for the fraction (1 − D) of the period, and its average must equal I_out. For the buck-boost it is also I_in + I_out.

Worked numbers: 12 V, 10 A, R_on = 10 mΩ

I_out² R_on = 1 W.

Converter V_in D I_L P_tot P_Q1 P_Q2
Buck 15 V 0.80 10 A 1.00 W 0.80 W 0.20 W
Buck 24 V 0.50 10 A 1.00 W 0.50 W 0.50 W
Buck 48 V 0.25 10 A 1.00 W 0.25 W 0.75 W
Boost 9 V 0.25 13.3 A 1.78 W 1.33 W 0.44 W
Boost 6 V 0.50 20 A 4.00 W 2.00 W 2.00 W
Boost 3 V 0.75 40 A 16.0 W 4.00 W 12.0 W
Buck-boost 24 V 0.33 15 A 2.25 W 0.75 W 1.50 W
Buck-boost 6 V 0.67 30 A 9.00 W 6.00 W 3.00 W

For the buck, the input voltage does not change the total: it only moves loss from the low-side to the high-side device as D grows. For the boost, halving the input quadruples the loss, because the inductor and both switches carry the full input current. The answer to the opening question: for the buck all three inputs tie at 1 W, for the boost the 9 V input wins, and the 3 V input is sixteen times worse. In a boost the high-side device Q1 is on for (1 − D) and still carries the large I_L; at D = 0.75 the low-side Q2 takes three quarters of 16 W.

Non-synchronous buck: a diode has a drop, not a resistance

Replace Q2 by a diode. The transistor keeps I_out² R_on D. The diode conducts I_out for (1 − D) at a constant drop, so its loss is I_out V_D (1 − D), linear in current rather than quadratic:

PQPD=D1−D⋅IoutRonVD.\frac{P_Q}{P_D}=\frac{D}{1-D}\cdot\frac{I_{\mathrm{out}}R_{\mathrm{on}}}{V_D}.PD​PQ​​=1−DD​⋅VD​Iout​Ron​​.

With 10 A, 10 mΩ and 0.5 V the second factor is 0.2: the transistor drop is 0.1 V against the diode’s 0.5 V. At D = 0.5 the diode loses 2.5 W and the transistor 0.5 W. The two are equal only at D = V_D/(V_D + I_out R_on) = 0.83. Below that the diode dominates, which is why low-voltage bucks are synchronous. Reverse-recovery loss is a separate, switching-type term.

Flyback in CCM: the turns ratio moves the loss and the stress together

A flyback is a buck-boost with a coupled inductor of ratio n = N_p/N_s. Take 48 V to 12 V at 2 A, R_on = 50 mΩ. The gain is V_out/V_in = D/(n (1 − D)), so for fixed voltages choosing D fixes n. The secondary plateau is I_out/(1 − D), the primary plateau is that divided by n, and the primary RMS is the plateau times √D. Substituting n gives a short result:

PQ=Iin2RonD,Iin=IoutVoutVin,VDS,off=Vin+nVout=Vin1−D.P_Q=\frac{I_{\mathrm{in}}^2 R_{\mathrm{on}}}{D},\qquad I_{\mathrm{in}}=\frac{I_{\mathrm{out}}V_{\mathrm{out}}}{V_{\mathrm{in}}},\qquad V_{DS,\mathrm{off}}=V_{\mathrm{in}}+nV_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}}{1-D}.PQ​=DIin2​Ron​​,Iin​=Vin​Iout​Vout​​,VDS,off​=Vin​+nVout​=1−DVin​​.

The primary carries the average input current, 0.5 A here, compressed into a fraction D of the period, so its RMS squared is I_in²/D. A small D means a large n, a tall narrow primary pulse and high loss. A large D means a small n and a low pulse, but the reflected voltage n V_out and the switch stress grow as V_in/(1 − D). The diode loses I_out V_D = 1 W whatever D is.

D n I_pri,rms P_Q V_DS,off
0.25 1.33 1.00 A 50 mW 64 V
0.50 4.0 0.71 A 25 mW 96 V
0.75 12 0.58 A 17 mW 192 V

Two limits bound the choice from the other side. The off-state voltage plus the leakage spike must fit the device rating. Peak-current-mode control without slope compensation needs D ≤ 0.5 to avoid the subharmonic oscillation; that is a nonlinear control limit, not a loss argument.

Flyback in DCM: energy per cycle sets the peak, D sets the RMS

In DCM every cycle stores L I_pk²/2 in the primary and delivers all of it, so P = L I_pk² f/2 and the peak follows from the power. The primary current is a triangle of width D T, whose RMS squared is I_pk² D/3. Eliminating I_pk:

Irms2=2PD3Lf,PQ=2PD3LfRon,PDIrms2=3Lf2.I_{\mathrm{rms}}^2=\frac{2PD}{3Lf},\qquad P_Q=\frac{2PD}{3Lf}R_{\mathrm{on}},\qquad \frac{PD}{I_{\mathrm{rms}}^2}=\frac{3Lf}{2}.Irms2​=3Lf2PD​,PQ​=3Lf2PD​Ron​,Irms2​PD​=23Lf​.

At a given power and inductance, a larger D now costs more loss, the opposite of the CCM trend: the peak is fixed by P, so a longer on-time only widens the triangle. The last expression is the check the lecture ran in simulation: for fixed L and f the ratio P D/I_rms² must be a constant, independent of D and of V_in. scripts/pwm_duty_conduction_losses.py repeats it on a constructed waveform with L = 50 µH, f = 100 kHz, n = 1, 100 Ω load, D from 0.20 to 0.65 and V_in of 30 V and 15 V. The predicted constant is 3 L f/2 = 7.5 Ω; the numerical ratio is 7.4998 to 7.4999 Ω at every point.

D at 30 V P_out V_out I_pk I_pri,rms P D/I_rms²
0.20 3.6 W 19.0 V 1.20 A 0.310 A 7.500 Ω
0.40 14.4 W 37.9 V 2.40 A 0.876 A 7.500 Ω
0.65 38.0 W 61.7 V 3.90 A 1.815 A 7.500 Ω

The reset time L I_pk/V_out is 3.16 µs at every point because V_out scales with I_pk; the converter leaves DCM at D = 1 − 1/√(R/(2 L f)) = 0.684, so the 0.7 point of the lecture’s sweep is already marginal. The lecture’s simulated ratio was about 7 against 7.5 because its diode and MOSFET capacitances add ringing current to the RMS; the ideal waveform here has none.

Change one thing: give the buck a 5 mΩ high-side and a 20 mΩ low-side device and find the D that minimizes the total; raise V_D to 0.9 V for a non-synchronous 48 V buck and recompute the crossover D; hold n = 4 in the CCM flyback and vary V_in instead of D; in the DCM case halve L and predict the new constant before running the script.

Recall questions: Why does the buck total not depend on D while the boost total does? Which quantity is held fixed in that statement? Why is a diode loss linear in current and a MOSFET loss quadratic? In the CCM flyback, which two stresses move in opposite directions when D changes? Why does a larger D increase the loss in DCM but decrease it in CCM? What does the constancy of P D/I_rms² test, and why did the lecture’s simulation give 7 instead of 7.5?

Representation choice. Express every loss in terms of the quantity the load dictates, I_out, and translate to the inductor or primary current through the duty cycle. The buck’s independence from D is the exception, not the rule: the moment the output leaves the inductor port, the total loss acquires a 1/(1 − D)² factor, and the same inductor current drives the switching loss. Treat the duty cycle as a design variable that trades conduction loss against device stress and control limits, and check which converter you are in before quoting a trend.
