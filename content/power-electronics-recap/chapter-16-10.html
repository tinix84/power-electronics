16.10 Optional advanced extension: ask for more boost voltage and initially get less — Basso-inspired

Outside the two-session core. Predict. An ideal continuous-conduction boost converter operates at 12 V input, 24 V output, D = 0.5, L = 100 µH, C = 100 µF and Rload = 24 Ω. Increase duty slightly. Does the output immediately rise?

The output load draws 1 A and the nominal inductor current is 2 A. Use the averaged state equations:

Step duty from 0.500 to 0.505, so ΔD = 0.005. At the instant of the change, inductor current and capacitor voltage cannot jump. Output-delivery current therefore falls by iL ΔD = 10 mA. The initial output slope is:

Meanwhile the increased on-time builds inductor current. Eventually that larger current can deliver more output power. The local linear model predicts an initial 0.828 mV undershoot at 16.6 µs and a final +0.24 V change. The exact ideal DC equilibrium at D = 0.505 would be 24.2424 V; the small difference from 24.24 V is linearization error, not a simulation inconsistency.

Linearizing the two state equations gives the duty-to-output transfer function:

Thus the zero is at s = +60,000 s⁻¹, or 9.549 kHz in frequency. The numerator 1 − s/ωz produces increasing magnitude but additional phase lag above its corner, unlike a left-half-plane zero. The model's poles remain stable; an initial inverse response is not itself instability.

Change one thing: vary load resistance or operating duty and recalculate the zero. Its position depends on the operating point, so a compensation design must check the relevant range. This example has no controller; it does not prescribe a universal crossover frequency or phase margin.

The model is continuous-time, local, averaged and assumes CCM. Switching ripple, DCM transitions, losses and sampling effects are excluded; the plotted 100 kHz Bode endpoint is mathematical, not evidence of physical accuracy up to an unspecified switching frequency.

Why the basic matters: more on-time initially means less output-delivery time. Bode phase follows the physical energy path. Basso's Understanding the Right-Half-Plane Zero explains the consequence for control design. The Python script independently compares the transfer-function response with the linearized state-space equations.

Control decision. Use the model containing the right-half-plane zero for compensation. The simpler DC conversion ratio predicts the final direction but misses the initial dip. It cannot justify a fast voltage loop.

Choose bandwidth only after checking the zero across the operating range. If the required response cannot tolerate that restriction, reconsider the power-stage or control architecture. Increasing gain alone does not remove the inverse response.
