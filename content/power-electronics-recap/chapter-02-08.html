2.8 Proximity effect and a high-power square-wave transformer

Physical idea: neighbouring turns impose a magnetic field on a conductor. The induced currents can reinforce transport current on one surface and oppose it elsewhere, creating additional copper loss. A conductor can even carry zero net transport current while supporting local eddy currents and heating. Thus, total ampere-turn cancellation in a transformer does not imply zero field everywhere in its winding window.

Skin effect already makes a thick conductor costly at high frequency. Proximity effect adds sensitivity to where the turns and their return ampere-turns are placed. A single-layer winding avoids the accumulation across many layers, but still experiences fields from adjacent turns and the other winding. The two calculations below deliberately distinguish these mechanisms.

Specify power transfer and the current waveform first

Use a 1:1 transformer with four primary and four secondary turns, driven from a bipolar bridge. At the load terminals, specify the following operating point:

The load voltage and current have matching, symmetric, flat-topped waveforms with finite transitions. An ideal square wave is their zero-transition-time limit. Here the resistive load makes current follow voltage; a square-wave transformer voltage feeding a different circuit need not produce square-wave current.

Single-layer construction therefore does not guarantee a low-loss high-frequency winding; the full winding can dissipate more than this skin-only estimate.

Step 2: keep the operating point, compare layered winding arrangements

Now consider a separate conductor construction: four one-turn radial layers per winding. Each foil turn is 20 mm wide and 0.20 mm thick, giving 4.000 mm² copper area, with the same 0.20 m mean turn length. Compare two orders across the window:

  • Grouped: P P P P S S S S.
  • Interleaved: P S P S P S P S.

All four P turns are connected in series, as are all four S turns. Interleaving changes their physical order, not the 4:4 electrical turns ratio. The foil comparison holds conductor dimensions, copper length, load waveform and turn counts fixed. Its copper area differs from the single-layer round-wire reference's 3.142 mm², so the two geometries are not an arrangement-only comparison.

Summing Havg² across eight layers gives 42K² grouped and 2K² interleaved: a factor of 21 in the foil model's proximity term, even though total primary and secondary ampere-turns cancel in both stacks.

Why proximity loss remains in the interleaved case: the field alternates between zero and K rather than disappearing. Each turn still has a nonzero mean field. More interfaces can also increase primary-secondary capacitance and consume space for insulation; minimum copper loss is only one design objective. TI's winding-loss note, especially its transformer and interleaving examples, provides the physical background for these field profiles.

Step 3: replace each foil turn with ideal transposed litz

For a nearly equal copper area, use 800 individually insulated strands of 80 µm diameter per turn. Their total copper area is 4.021 mm². Retain the same electrical turn counts and 0.20 m strand length per turn for the comparison. Assume equal transport-current sharing and ideal transposition within each turn.

Model the macroscopic field as varying linearly across each litz turn, with a locally uniform applied field at each strand position and no bundle shielding.

Core loss, bridge loss and rectifier loss are outside the table, so these numbers are not total converter loss or efficiency.

Engineering conclusion for this example: interleaving strongly reduces the applied field, while subdivision into fine insulated strands reduces conductor eddy loss. They address different parts of the problem. Under the specified model, interleaved foil even outperforms grouped ideal litz. Litz selection therefore needs the harmonic spectrum and winding field, not just a strand-diameter check at f₀.

The litz numbers are idealized applied-field estimates, not predictions for a purchased bundle. Real bundle packing and shielding, local strand-to-strand fields, incomplete transposition, terminations, twist length and departures from the assumed linear macroscopic field change the result. A transposed bundle also occupies more space than its copper area and usually uses more strand length than the winding centreline length.

Consult Ansys's litz modelling assumptions and Elektrisola's construction information when moving beyond this teaching comparison.

Classroom checks: why does zero total load ampere-turns not imply zero copper eddy loss? Fields can remain between and inside winding layers. Why is a litz bundle still lossy in a large external field? Insulated strands still support internal eddy currents, and imperfect construction can add further losses.

Winding choice. Interleaving can reduce leakage and proximity loss, but changes capacitance and insulation geometry. Compare it with the grouped winding under the same waveform and constraints. Choose from the combined loss, coupling and construction requirements rather than one curve.
