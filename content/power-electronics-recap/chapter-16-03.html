16.3 Why did the “better” ceramic input capacitor cause failure?

Predict. Connect a 24 V source through cable inductance to an initially empty ceramic input capacitor. Its voltage rating is 35 V. Is the rating obviously sufficient because 24 < 35?

Let L = 1 µH, C = 10 µF, and initial conditions iL = 0, vC = 0. With no load, the series-RLC equation is:

With R = 0.05 Ω, the damping ratio is 0.0791 and the calculated first peak is 42.71 V. In the lossless ideal-step limit, the capacitor reaches 48 V. At 0.6325 Ω, the response is critically damped; at 1 Ω, it is overdamped. The latter two approach 24 V without overshoot in this model.

Check the energy after settling: the capacitor stores ½CV² = 2.88 mJ. For each nonzero series R, this ideal step also dissipates 2.88 mJ; the ideal source supplies 5.76 mJ net. Changing resistance changes the peak and duration, not this final loss for the specified unloaded initial/final states. The numerical script independently checks source work against resistor heat plus stored energy.

Change one thing: add damping or a suitable damping branch. Simply adding main-path resistance has a DC cost once a load is connected: 0.6325 Ω carrying 1 A loses 0.6325 W and drops 0.6325 V. Real designs may use a deliberately damped capacitor branch instead.

Include source current limits, connector bounce, capacitor bias dependence and load behaviour when predicting hardware. The plotted source is ideal and can absorb reverse current; a real adapter may behave differently.

Why the basic matters: startup is not DC steady state, and lower ESR is not universally better. AN88, Ceramic Input Capacitors Can Cause Overvoltage Transients is by Goran Perica, not Jim Williams. It is included as a directly relevant companion source.

Damping choice. Reject the 0.05 Ω case: its 42.71 V peak exceeds the 35 V capacitor rating. For this unloaded model, use 0.6325 Ω as the critically damped reference. The 1 Ω alternative also avoids overshoot but settles more slowly.

With a load, series resistance costs DC voltage and heat. Then compare a damped shunt branch before keeping the main-path resistor. Its values must be checked in that circuit.
