16.7 Why does a 1 A DC supply heat its transformer and capacitor?

Predict. A low-voltage full-wave rectifier supplies a nominally constant 1 A load through a reservoir capacitor. Is a 1 A RMS secondary current a sufficient heating estimate?

For an intentionally simple assigned waveform, let rectifier/secondary current be 10 A for 1 ms, then zero for 9 ms. This gives 1 A average, a 100 Hz charging repetition rate and duty d = 0.1. It is not a solved diode-bridge waveform from a sinusoidal source; actual conduction angle depends on source impedance, voltage, diode drops, load and capacitance.

The capacitor current is iC = iD − 1 A: +9 A during charging, then −1 A during discharge. Its mean is zero, but:

With 0.2 Ω secondary winding resistance, copper loss is 2 W, rather than the 0.2 W associated with 1 A DC. With 0.05 Ω capacitor ESR, its heating is 0.45 W. The plotted secondary/bridge-output current magnitude should not be mistaken for each individual diode's current: in a symmetric full-wave bridge each diode conducts on alternate charging events.

For C = 4700 µF, the 9 ms discharge removes 9 mC and produces 1.915 V ideal capacitor ripple. The assigned waveform charges it by exactly the same amount. The plotted capacitor voltage starts at 11 V, rises to approximately 12.915 V, then returns to 11 V.

ESR is excluded from that voltage trace; if included, the 10 A capacitor-current change would add an ideal 0.5 V ESR step. Capacitance determines charge ripple; RMS current and ESR determine heating.

Change one thing: reduce conduction duty while retaining 1 A average. Peak and RMS charging currents rise. This original exercise applies the average/RMS and charge-balance fundamentals from chapters 5–7; it is not attributed as a particular Williams, Pease, Ott or Basso worked circuit.

What should we change? First size the winding and capacitor for their RMS currents. The assigned pulses give 2 W of winding loss and 0.45 W of capacitor loss despite a 1 A DC load.

A larger reservoir is an alternative when voltage droop is the problem. It is not yet a cure for heating: changing capacitance changes the real charging waveform, which this assigned-current model does not solve.
