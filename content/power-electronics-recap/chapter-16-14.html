16.14 Why does stopping the motor raise the DC-link voltage?

Predict. A motor drive has a 470 µF DC-link capacitor initially at 48 V. During deceleration, 1 J of net electrical energy reaches the capacitor after motor/converter losses. Assume the upstream supply cannot sink current, and no other load or braking branch absorbs energy. Is the bus still approximately 48 V?

For the stated values, Vf ≈ 81.0 V. A small-looking amount of energy can therefore produce a large voltage increase. Do not use ΔV = E/C: its units are wrong, and capacitor energy depends on voltage squared. For small excursions only, ΔV ≈ E/(CV₀) is a local approximation; it is inaccurate for this large excursion.

Storing the full 1 J between 48 V and 60 V would require at least 1.54 mF of effective capacitance in this ideal energy calculation. This is an energy minimum, not a complete component selection: operating margin, capacitance tolerance/bias, ripple current, ESR and control delay remain relevant.

Connect it to mechanics. A rotor/load with inertia Jm has kinetic energy Jmω²/2. Reducing speed releases the difference in kinetic energy; only the fraction actually returned electrically reaches the bus. The energy calculation above deliberately starts with the net returned 1 J so it does not assume lossless motor regeneration.

Change one thing: add capacitance, provide an appropriately controlled braking resistor, or use a source/storage interface capable of absorbing the returned energy. A diode-fed supply or a supply that only sources current does not automatically provide that sink. A resistor/chopper must handle the returned energy, peak power and repetition rate; a finite capacitor cannot absorb continuous regeneration indefinitely. See TI's discussion of motor-drive overvoltage and bulk capacitance.

Why the basic matters: the supply voltage setting cannot hold the bus down unless there is a path for the returned energy. Ask the same question for an actuator lowering a load, an externally driven motor or a shared DC bus: where can the energy go?

Where should the energy go? The 470 µF bank fails the hypothetical 60 V ceiling: the energy calculation gives about 81 V. For one bounded 1 J event, investigate a bank above the 1.54 mF ideal minimum, with margin.

Repeated braking needs a repeatable energy sink. A braking resistor spends the energy as heat; a regenerative interface can return it, with more conversion and control hardware. Choose that path from repetition and sink capability, not capacitance alone.
