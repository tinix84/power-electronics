12.4 Feedback and stability

Refresh the ideal op-amp concept: high open-loop gain makes the input error small under stable negative feedback, provided the output is not saturated and the amplifier stays within its operating limits. A regulator compares a divided output voltage with a reference and drives a pass device or switching modulator.

For a conventional negative-feedback loop with forward gain A and feedback factor β:

The loop gain T, rather than an arbitrary output-filter gain, is used to discuss gain crossover and stability margins. At a unity-gain crossover, phase margin is 180° plus the loop phase under the conventional sign convention. A margin near 45–60° is a common design starting point, not a universal requirement or a complete stability proof. Multiple crossovers, right-half-plane dynamics, sampling and delays can require more careful analysis.

Bode stability checks: which curve crosses which reference?

Here write the loop transfer as L(s) = A(s)β(s), to distinguish it from inductance. Negative feedback gives the characteristic condition 1 + L(s) = 0. For an already stable conventional loop, these indicate gain-only or phase-only perturbations that can bring the frequency response to the critical point. They are not independent universal bounds on arbitrary simultaneous model changes. With multiple crossings, inspect all crossings and establish the nominal stability first. MathWorks' all-margin reference distinguishes all crossover margins from the nominal closed-loop stability result.

A downward −20 dB/decade crossing is a useful loop-shaping target, not a stability theorem. One dominant minimum-phase pole contributes roughly this slope and about −90° phase in its asymptotic region, motivating the heuristic. But local magnitude slope does not specify total loop phase. Other poles/zeros, right-half-plane zeros and delays can change that phase, and a later resonance can return the magnitude above unity.

Even a delay has unit magnitude while adding phase −ωτ. The rule therefore cannot certify a loop from one short segment of its magnitude plot.

Nyquist: count the whole loop around the critical point

For the scalar loop, plot the complex locus of L(jω). The critical point is −1 + j0 because it makes 1 + L vanish. Assume first a proper rational loop with no poles on the imaginary axis and no hidden unstable cancellations. The full Nyquist contour traverses the imaginary axis from −j∞ to +j∞ and closes through the right half-plane.

For a strictly proper loop the large-arc image tends to the origin. Include negative frequencies; for real coefficients they are the conjugate of the positive-frequency trace.

Use this explicit sign convention: N is net clockwise encirclements of −1, P is the number of open-loop RHP poles, and Z is the number of closed-loop RHP characteristic roots, counted with multiplicity. The argument principle gives

If P = 0, closed-loop stability requires zero net encirclements and no boundary roots. If P > 0, stability instead requires N = −P: the necessary net encirclements are counterclockwise. “Never encircle −1” is therefore a special case, not the general rule. Poles on the imaginary axis, including an integrator at zero, require a suitably indented contour.

A passage through −1 needs boundary analysis; it is not a positive stability margin. See Swarthmore's Nyquist stability derivation.

For a finite-dimensional minimal closed-loop realization, Z = 0 with no imaginary-axis roots establishes asymptotic stability. Internal modes hidden by unstable cancellation still require the full-state check of §16.33. The Nyquist plot here is unrelated to the Nyquist sampling frequency in FFT discussions.

How Bode, Nyquist, Fourier and Laplace relate

The parallel is visual rather than an equality of mathematical extensions. Complete Bode magnitude and phase and the Nyquist locus encode the same loop-frequency values:

Use radians for φ inside sine/cosine. Bode separates log-magnitude and phase versus frequency; Nyquist places each value in the complex L plane, making winding around −1 visible. Keeping both Bode curves, frequency labels and the appropriate phase branch does not discard the information in L(jω). Keeping only local magnitude slope does.

Description What it shows
Fourier/frequency response Behaviour along imaginary frequency s = jω where the relevant response exists
Laplace Complex frequency s = σ + jω, natural modes and convergence (§9.1)
Bode Magnitude and phase of the frequency response on separate frequency axes
Nyquist stability plot The image of the frequency/contour path in the complex loop-gain plane, combined with the known open-loop RHP pole count

Nyquist does not add the σ coordinate to a Bode plot. Its stability power comes from the argument principle and the open-loop pole count, while Laplace supplies the complex-frequency framework. A finite measured sweep needs adequate bandwidth/resolution and a model of omitted dynamics; changing the display alone does not establish those assumptions. Section 16.34 makes the distinction concrete with verified counterexamples.

Digital control: the actuator applies a command after the measurement

A pure delay multiplies a continuous loop by exp(−sTd). Its magnitude is one on jω, but its phase contribution is −360 fTd degrees. Thus it can erase phase margin without changing the magnitude plot. A held average PWM command also contributes a baseband hold effect, often approximated by half an update period of delay.

The acquisition, computation, register-update schedule and modulator must be distinguished; sampling alone does not have one universal fixed delay.

Finite ADC/PWM resolution introduces another effect that cannot be certified from the unquantized loop's margins alone. A stable linear model can coexist with a steady quantization cycle if the controller cannot hold an attainable actuator level inside its measured zero-error band. Section 16.36 contrasts static ADC error, PWM-induced cycling and a finer-resolution remedy.

Use the actual discrete plant/controller model when the crossover is a significant fraction of the update rate. A discrete closed loop is asymptotically stable when all its state eigenvalues lie strictly inside the unit circle, not merely in the left half of the z plane.

The mapping z = exp(sTs) connects the continuous and discrete stability regions. Section 16.35 supplies an exact sampled/held counterexample and separates it from the approximate Bode delay budget. Section 16.31 treats a different sampled mechanism: the inner peak-current comparator's period-to-period instability.

Classroom question: why might adding a capacitor change stability? It changes the plant's poles and possibly ESR zeros, affecting loop gain and phase. More capacitance is not automatically a better control design.
