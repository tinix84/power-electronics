16.36 The linear poles are stable: why does the digital regulator keep cycling?

Riddle: the unquantized sampled loop has every pole inside the unit circle. Without changing its gain, sampling schedule or plant, round the ADC measurement and PWM duty command to finite grids. Can the regulator oscillate forever even with noiseless measurements?

Yes. Linear asymptotic stability describes the unquantized equations. Quantizers change those equations into a nonlinear, piecewise system and can prevent the existence of the desired static equilibrium. The phenomenon is deterministic; it is not necessarily measurement noise, linear instability or an unfinished startup transient. Peterchev and Sanders' original paper discusses ADC/DPWM resolution, zero-error bins, steady limit cycling and controlled dither.

The following numbers and reduced plant are newly authored for this exercise.

Define the plant, quantizers and update order

This is an authored output-envelope model with a buck-like DC duty conversion, not a complete LC buck or a carrier-switching simulation. At each sample, measure voltage, update the integrator, quantize its duty command and hold that average command through the next interval:

Set r = 5 V and g = 0.015 duty/V/update. Start from v[0] = 4.8 V and c[−1] = 0.4001. There is no additional one-period command delay in this example; that separate effect was studied in §16.35. Quantizers round to the nearest grid point, with exact half-step ties upward.

All reported steady states and cycles avoid ties and active saturation. Integrator arithmetic is not rounded further; for ADC cases, summing integer error codes avoids floating-point accumulation drift.

For the ADC, define an output-referred span of 8 V, including any measurement-divider gain. An ideal 8-bit quantizer then has ΔvADC = 8/256 = 31.25 mV. The 5 V reference is exactly code 160. Its zero-measured-error bin is

For the coarse PWM, use 64 duty increments, Δd = 1/64. A one-count change corresponds to VinΔd = 187.5 mV in the static output model. These are output-referred comparisons; comparing the two nominal bit counts without full-scale ranges and plant gain would be misleading.

First prove that the unquantized linear controller is stable

Remove both quantizers while retaining sampling and integral action. In error coordinates [v[k] − r, c[k−1] − r/Vin], the closed-loop matrix is

M = [ a - b g    b ]
    [   -g       1 ]

Here a = 0.606531 and b = 4.721632 V per unit duty. The poles are 0.767853 ± j0.130124, with magnitude 0.778801. The unquantized loop therefore converges to 5 V. The quantization example is not explained by an unstable pole already present in the baseline.

Why the 6-bit PWM cannot provide a static zero-error solution

The ideal static duty is 5/12 = 0.416667, between two available PWM codes:

PWM code Duty Static output Vin × duty
26 26/64 = 0.40625 4.875 V
27 27/64 = 0.421875 5.0625 V

Neither static output is inside the ADC's zero-error bin. At a genuine fixed point the integrator would have to stop, which requires vq = r, while the plant would have to settle to Vin times an available duty. Those two requirements are incompatible here.

The integrator therefore changes its internal command until the actuator jumps to another PWM code, then corrects in the opposite direction as the measured error changes sign. The absence of a suitable fixed point does not by itself prove that every trajectory must enter one particular limit cycle: nonlinear systems may have other behaviours.

For the stated initial conditions, however, the simulation and an analytical periodic-state calculation demonstrate a repeating cycle.

Five controlled comparisons

Use exactly the same plant, integral gain, timing and initial state in every case. Only the quantizers change:

Quantization Steady result in this experiment Mean output Output ripple, peak-to-peak
Neither Fixed point 5.000000 V 0
8-bit ADC only; continuous duty Fixed point inside ADC bin 4.998075 V 0
6-bit PWM only; ideal voltage measurement Six-update cycle, 3.333 kHz 5.000000 V 107.852 mV
8-bit ADC + 6-bit PWM Three-update cycle, 6.667 kHz 5.000000 V 60.029 mV
8-bit ADC + 10-bit PWM Fixed point inside ADC bin 5.003906 V 0

The ADC-only case has a static true error, although the digital controller reads zero error. That is not a limit cycle. The two coarse-PWM cases keep moving after startup has finished. Their exact periods depend on the quantizers, controller and state; the amplitudes do not simply add as independent noise powers. The zero-ripple entries refer to this averaged-envelope model and do not mean that real switching ripple vanishes.

Follow the three-update orbit

For ADC plus coarse PWM, one repeating sequence is:

Sample in cycle True output ADC reading Error r − vq Applied PWM code
1 4.967535 V 4.968750 V +31.25 mV 27
2 5.004901 V 5.000000 V 0 27
3 5.027564 V 5.031250 V −31.25 mV 26

The error sum over the three updates is exactly zero, so the integrator returns to its earlier state. The mean applied duty is (27 + 27 + 26)/(3 × 64) = 5/12. Thus the average output can be exactly correct while the instantaneous output never settles.

For a candidate repeating duty sequence d0, d1, d2, the plant's exact periodic initial voltage is

Using (27, 27, 26)/64 gives 4.967535 V, reproducing the simulation's orbit. The script also checks that every ADC reading and PWM decision lies strictly inside its corresponding quantization bin, and that the integrator closes after the third update. A small +1 mV output-state perturbation retains the same code sequence and shrinks by a³ = 0.22313 per complete cycle.

This establishes a locally persistent periodic output, rather than mistaking residual linear startup oscillation for a cycle. It is not a claim of a globally unique isolated orbit for every possible integrator state.

Remedies and the accuracy tradeoff

Increase PWM resolution to Δd = 1/1024. The output-referred step becomes 11.71875 mV, and code 427 gives 5.00390625 V, strictly inside the ADC zero-error bin. The observed trajectory can now stop. This removes the demonstrated envelope cycle, but leaves a +3.906 mV true offset. Eliminating cycling is not the same requirement as exact regulation.

Having an attainable actuator level inside the zero-error bin is an equilibrium-feasibility check, not a general guarantee that nonlinear limit cycles are absent for every controller gain or initial condition. Conversely, adding ADC bits alone can narrow the zero-error band without adding any available actuator level. Compare the output-referred ADC and PWM steps over the operating range, including the voltage-to-duty gain; do not adopt a universal bit-count difference.

Other design choices include controlled dither or pulse-density sequences to synthesize an average duty between codes, coordinated gain/resolution changes, or a deliberate deadband. Dither trades the location and form of residual ripple rather than making finite resolution disappear; a wider deadband trades regulation accuracy for fewer corrections.

Their behaviour must be evaluated with the actual plant and update schedule. The present five-way comparison explicitly verifies the finer-PWM remedy; it does not claim an implemented dither or deadband controller.

Recall questions: Why can a stable linear model coexist with a periodic nonlinear response? Why does ADC-only error not necessarily cause cycling? Why does a 5 V mean fail to prove that the output has settled? Finally, can a more accurate ADC make the absence of a compatible PWM equilibrium more apparent?

Resolution or tolerated ripple? For a settled output, choose the demonstrated 10-bit PWM case over the 6-bit case. It removes this orbit but leaves the reported static output error inside the ADC bin. More PWM resolution costs implementation capability.

Deadband or dither are alternatives when some error or ripple is acceptable. They are not verified here. A correct 5 V mean does not justify retaining the 6-bit regulator when instantaneous ripple matters.
