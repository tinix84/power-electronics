16.13 The DC link has a huge capacitor: why does the switch still see overvoltage?

Predict. A 48 V switching stage has a large bulk capacitor, but the capacitor is connected through a cable or a long PCB loop. During commutation, 20 A in a stipulated 100 nH stray commutation inductance falls to zero in 100 ns. Can increasing the distant bulk capacitance eliminate the switch-voltage overshoot?

First draw the complete commutation loop, including the bulk/local capacitor, switches or diodes, outgoing path and return. A DC voltage rating alone says nothing about the voltage needed to change current in that loop. With voltage referenced in the direction of the original inductive current:

In a turn-off circuit where this induced voltage adds to a 48 V blocking voltage, the estimate is 68 V at the switch, before additional ringing or device effects. The sign and stressed component follow from KVL in the actual topology; an arbitrary cable-current change does not always add 20 V to every node.

Here the specified current is the current in the stray inductance being commutated, not automatically the load current or the distant supply current.

Change one feature, predict again.

Change, keeping the other stated quantities fixed Inductive voltage magnitude
Original: 100 nH, 20 A change in 100 ns 20 V
Reduce effective commutation inductance to 20 nH 4 V
Slow the current transition to 200 ns 10 V
Double the distant bulk capacitance, keeping loop inductance and current slew fixed 20 V

A local low-inductance capacitor can close the fast switching-current loop near the devices, allowing the remote cable current to change more slowly. Its value, ESL, mounting, vias and return path all matter. More microfarads at the far end do not remove the intervening inductance. Slowing a transition can reduce the inductive voltage but may increase switching loss (§16.8).

The initial energy in this stray inductance is:

It must be transferred to capacitances, returned to another port or dissipated in a clamp/snubber or device. This energy estimate helps size an absorption path; it is not an additional loss to add blindly to a waveform-integrated loss calculation. Sections 16.6 and 16.11 develop the clamp and snubber cases.

Why the basic matters: bulk energy storage and a fast local current path are different design needs. Compare this with §16.3: hot-plug excites the cable-L/input-C network from its initial state, whereas this example concerns an already energised switching circuit. Neither the prescribed-slew estimate nor the lumped LC example predicts distributed cable reflections; electrically long cables need a transmission-line model. Measure with the short-return or differential arrangement discussed in §16.1.

Placement choice. Put the fast capacitor path near the commutating devices. It reduces local loop inductance but consumes placement area. The remote bulk bank remains useful for slower energy demands. Compare the two time scales before paying for more distant capacitance.
