2.7 Skin effect: why copper resistance depends on frequency

Physical idea: a changing current produces a changing magnetic field inside the conductor. The induced electric field redistributes current toward the surface. At low frequency, current density in a uniform round copper wire is approximately uniform; at high frequency, much of the interior carries little AC current.

The same total RMS current then produces more resistive heating. This is electromagnetic diffusion, rather than an increase in copper's intrinsic resistivity caused by frequency alone.

At one skin depth, current-density amplitude is 1/e ≈ 37% of its surface value; it is not abruptly zero beyond δ. This exponential profile assumes a locally flat, sufficiently thick conductor. A round wire whose radius is comparable to δ needs the cylindrical solution used below.

For the teaching calculations, take copper at approximately 20 °C with ρ = 1.724 × 10⁻⁸ Ωm and μ ≈ μ₀. Then:

Frequency Copper skin depth Compared with a 1 mm diameter wire
50 Hz 9.35 mm Much larger than the 0.5 mm radius
1 kHz 2.09 mm Current nearly uniform
10 kHz 0.661 mm Redistribution beginning
100 kHz 0.209 mm Noticeable current crowding
1 MHz 0.0661 mm = 66.1 µm Strong current crowding

For this wire, δ equals its radius at about 17.5 kHz; this is a scale, not a sharp onset frequency. Larger busbars can exhibit significant skin effect even at mains frequency. Temperature changes resistivity, resistance and skin depth, so state the temperature when comparing values. TI's winding-loss note, pages 1–3, introduces skin depth and the redistribution mechanism.

In a multiwinding component, proximity losses can depend on the amplitudes and phases of currents in the other windings as well.

Skin effect, proximity effect and conductor choice

Skin effect describes redistribution by a conductor's own alternating field. Proximity effect describes additional redistribution caused by nearby current paths and their fields. In a multilayer transformer winding, proximity losses can dominate. Their size depends on layer arrangement, winding currents and air-gap fringing fields. The isolated-wire curve therefore establishes a reference, not a complete winding-loss prediction.

Construction Useful effect What still needs checking
Thicker solid round wire Reduces DC resistance At high frequency, effective area grows mainly with perimeter; added copper is less useful than in DC
Thin foil or PCB winding Can keep conductor thickness small relative to δ Return geometry and neighbouring layers can crowd current onto one face or toward edges
Several ordinary bare/contacting strands Provides mechanical flexibility Does not guarantee independent strand currents or the performance of litz wire
Litz wire Individually insulated strands and transposition can reduce AC loss Strand diameter, bundle construction, external field, terminations, insulation fill and extra strand length

For an equal-copper-area comparison, 100 strands of 0.10 mm diameter contain the same copper cross-section as one 1.00 mm wire. At 1 MHz, the isolated 0.10 mm strand has RAC/RDC ≈ 1.0068, versus 4.045 for the solid 1 mm wire. With equal strand currents and the same 1 m strand length, the skin-only bundle estimate is 22.10 mΩ.

This illustrates the benefit of subdivision; it is not a full litz-winding prediction. The bundle's outside diameter is larger because of insulation and packing, and actual transposition increases strand length. Elektrisola's litz-wire technical information describes strand insulation and construction; Ansys's litz-wire modelling documentation states the ideal transposition assumptions.

On a PCB, compare the copper thickness with δ, then include the return and layer fields. At 100 kHz, 35 µm copper is much thinner than the 209 µm skin depth; thickness alone does not suggest severe skin effect, but geometry can still cause proximity or edge crowding.

Skin effect concerns penetration into copper; dielectric distance concerns separation between conductors and changes capacitance, external inductance and proximity fields. They are different dimensions of the same physical layout.

Classroom check: would doubling a thick wire's diameter halve its resistance? For DC it ideally reduces resistance by four; in the strong skin-effect limit, isolated-wire AC resistance is approximately halved because the perimeter doubles. Check which regime applies first.
