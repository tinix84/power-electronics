4. Worked design exercise: how close must the CPU's source be?

This exercise emulates a fast die-side CPU load transient. It does not assert the measured slew rate of a particular processor. The regulator, board, package and die capacitors supply different frequency ranges.

Given

  • Nominal CPU rail: 1.00 V, with ±30 mV allowed deviation.
  • Initial rail is settled at 1.00 V under a 40 A load.
  • Load rises from 40 A to 60 A in 20 ns: an incremental 20 A ramp.
  • First model: no local capacitance, an ideal source, negligible resistance, and a supply/return pair with assumed loop µH/m.
  • Assign the entire 30 mV downward deviation budget to this one mechanism. The full 60 mV tolerance band is not available from an initially nominal rail.

Step 1: inductance and distance limit

With the same assumptions, a 1 mm connection would produce a 1 V inductive drop. The result exposes the failure of a remote source alone to supply this fast current ramp. The immediate source must include local stored energy, often on the die or in the package.

It is not a board-placement rule requiring the VRM to sit 30 µm from the CPU. Different interconnect geometry changes , and capacitance changes the circuit.

Step 3: where the capacitors enter

The charge deficit determines capacitance:

If upstream current stays at its initial value during the 20 ns linear ramp, the deficit is triangular. Its charge is 0.20 µC; capacitance alone would need at least 6.67 µF for 30 mV. If a capacitor must subsequently deliver the full extra 20 A for a separate 100 ns interval, that interval alone needs 66.7 µF. Real selection also includes capacitance derating, ESL, ESR, package impedance and resonances.

4.1 Classroom riddle: why put 10 nF and 100 nF beside a digital IC?

Start with the question, then choose the disturbance. Is ripple arriving from the supply, or is switching current being drawn by the IC? These are different inputs to the same network. A CPU/GPU supply current can contain sharp pulses and many harmonics; it need not be a literal clock-frequency square wave. The following model isolates the mechanism rather than representing a measured processor.

Let Zs be the regulator-plus-interconnect impedance seen from the chosen IC supply port. Let ZC include the local capacitor's ESR and complete loop inductance. An independent small-signal current sink represents the load-current disturbance; a separate ZIC can represent the IC's incremental port impedance. First take ZIC sufficiently large to omit it. If ZIC is retained, include it in the parallel admittance when calculating the response to the independent current disturbance. Do not use the same load-current response once as an independent source and again inside ZIC. An IC's DC ratio V/I is not generally its high-frequency impedance: 0.8 V / 100 A = 8 mΩ is a static operating-point ratio, not a measured ZIC(f).

Quiz A — make the source ideal at the IC pins. Set Zs = 0. The load-current-induced voltage ripple is zero in this ideal model, with or without the capacitor. The ideal source supplies the disturbance current; a capacitor across a perfectly fixed voltage cannot further improve that voltage. This does not describe a remote regulator separated from the die by a package, vias and traces.

Quiz B — apply supply-voltage ripple instead. With the independent load-current disturbance set to zero and ZIC omitted:

For Zs = 0 this ratio is one: a shunt capacitor does not attenuate ripple imposed by an ideal voltage source at its own node. A finite series impedance can create source-to-load filtering. The identical algebra above describes two different measurements: voltage-noise transfer and the fraction of IC disturbance current flowing upstream. State the excitation and measurement before saying “attenuation.”

Quiz C — add the connection and move the capacitor. Use 1 nH/mm, not nΩ/mm, as the specified two-wire-loop estimate from section 2.3. Inductance becomes impedance through jωL; 45 nH has reactance about 283 Ω at 1 GHz. This number is not a universal PCB-trace impedance, especially when the interconnect is electrically long.

Local capacitance can provide fast load current while the upstream path changes current more slowly. Keep the capacitor-to-power-pin-and-return loop inductance small. “Close” includes the return, vias, package and mounting geometry. A capacitor directly underneath the IC can provide a short loop when the stackup and via arrangement support it; placement on the opposite face is not inherently best. TI's processor-PDN layout guidance, section 3, discusses mounting and spreading inductance.

Numerical comparison, separate from the 100 A exercise below: choose a small-signal source path of 20 mΩ + jω·5 nH, a 100 nF capacitor with 10 mΩ ESR, and total capacitor-loop inductance of either 0.5 nH or 5 nH. A third case parallels the close 100 nF with 10 nF, 20 mΩ ESR and 0.5 nH loop inductance. These are authored lumped examples, not a GPU rail design.

With 0.5 nH, the 100 nF and 10 nF branches have self-resonances at 22.5 MHz and 71.2 MHz. The 100 nF branch's impedance at 1 GHz is approximately 3.14 Ω, dominated by inductance. A smaller capacitance can move branch resonance upward, but paralleling capacitor values can also create antiresonance peaks.

Select values, packages and mounting from the complete impedance profile, rather than assuming that adding 10 nF always improves 100 nF.

The plots show conducted current division and port impedance. They do not predict radiated-emission attenuation in dB. Radiation also depends on loop area, return discontinuities and common-mode paths. A passive PDN resonance peak is also not, by itself, proof of control-loop instability; section 12.6 shows how an actively regulated load can destabilize an input filter.

Which capacitor helps? Start by shortening the mounted path of the 100 nF capacitor. The 10 nF addition is a separate candidate, not an automatic improvement. Keep both only if their combined impedance improves the required band without unacceptable peaking. Otherwise, the single close capacitor is the simpler choice.

4.2 Board-diagonal quiz: will a 1 GHz signal disappear?

Given: a square board with 10 cm² area, components at opposite corners, and an approximately diagonal route. A 3 cm × 3 cm board would instead have 9 cm² area; neither description means a 10 cm × 10 cm board.

First distinguish plane capacitance from trace capacitance. If two overlapping planes use the entire 10 cm² area, εr = 4 and h = 1 mm between copper surfaces, their plate-model capacitance is:

At 0.10 mm dielectric separation, the same estimate becomes 354 pF. Total PCB thickness is not necessarily the signal-to-reference spacing. A signal trace does not use the full board area as its plate: its width, reference geometry, dielectric and fringing fields determine its capacitance and inductance. One cannot infer “45 nH and a few hundred pF” for that trace from board area and diagonal alone.

Does the voltage-divider arithmetic work? If we deliberately build an ideal lumped series 45 nH followed by a shunt 300 pF capacitor, unloaded, its calculated gain at 1 GHz is approximately 0.00188, or −54.5 dB. That is the result for the specified lumped LC circuit. It does not establish that distributed PCB traces behave as one inductor followed by the whole board capacitance at their far end.

A counterexample with a defined signal line. Choose a uniform, lossless 50 Ω line with effective relative permittivity 4 and a matched 50 Ω receiver. This is an illustrative line, not a stackup extracted from the incomplete board geometry:

Its magnitude is one: the receiver sees a delayed signal. The ratio is measured from the line entrance, so any source-resistance voltage division during launch is outside this ratio. A real PCB adds frequency-dependent conductor/dielectric loss; a mismatched receiver adds reflections. Neither implies that a 4.5 cm path automatically removes a 1 GHz signal.

For CPU-to-RAM signals, use driver/receiver models, the reference path, line impedance, delay and termination. Edge rise time and harmonics matter as well as clock rate. Intel/Altera's signal-integrity white paper covers distributed interconnects, impedance mismatch and loss. Supply decoupling across a power rail and adding capacitance to a data signal are different circuit interventions.

4.3 CPU/POL exercise: translate tolerance into an impedance budget

Use a teaching scenario, not a claimed Intel or NVIDIA device limit: a nominal 1.00 V rail, initially settled at nominal, with 30 mV allowable downward deviation and an additional 100 A load-current demand at the defined IC port. The one-sided voltage budget gives the target-impedance scale:

This applies to the total PDN seen at that port over the specified frequency range. It is a screening/design target, not a guarantee of peak transient voltage for every waveform. The DC/DC's output impedance alone is insufficient when interconnect and local storage matter. In a simple lumped model, Zs = Zreg + Rpath + jωLpath, with local capacitor branches connected at their actual locations.

A die-side current requirement also needs the package and die network; it must not silently be moved to a board-side port.

Resolve the slew-rate unit before calculating distance. Allocate all 30 mV to inductance alone for this comparison, neglect capacitors and resistance, and retain the assumed loop L′ = 1 nH/mm:

Specified slew rate Equivalent A/s Maximum L Equivalent pair length under the stated L′
100 A/ms 10⁵ A/s 300 nH 300 mm
100 A/µs 10⁸ A/s 0.30 nH 0.30 mm
100 A/ns 10¹¹ A/s 0.30 pH 0.30 µm

A millisecond versus a microsecond changes the inductance allowance by a factor of 1000. These distances expose the limit of a remote-source-only model; they do not prescribe practical VRM placement or apply unchanged to closely spaced power/return planes.

Step 2 — negligible inductance: if the full 30 mV incremental drop is resistive, total Thévenin resistance must be at most 0.30 mΩ. If instead we divide the budget into 10 mV for resistance, 10 mV for inductance and 10 mV for capacitor charge deficit:

  • Total incremental resistance allowance is 10 mV / 100 A = 0.10 mΩ. With an assumed path resistance of 0.04 mΩ, only 0.06 mΩ remains for the regulator's resistive contribution in this simplified model.
  • At 100 A/µs, the inductance allowance is 10 mV / (100 A/µs) = 0.10 nH, corresponding to 0.10 mm only under the assumed loop-inductance-per-length model.
  • A 100 A rise over 1 µs, while upstream current remains at its initial value, leaves a triangular 50 µC charge deficit. Limiting the capacitive contribution to 10 mV requires 5 mF effective capacitance. If the upstream current responds during the ramp, integrate the actual smaller deficit; a different waveform or interval gives a different result.

This is why 10 nF and 100 nF local capacitors address a different portion of the transient spectrum from bulk storage and the closed-loop POL regulator. Account for actual DC-bias derating, ESR, ESL and the handover between board, package and die. Do not assign the full voltage allowance independently to every mechanism and then sum them.

Connection to published methodology: the Intel/Altera PDN Tool User Guide, sections on target impedance and decoupling, uses allowable ripple, transient current and a target frequency range. It is FPGA PDN guidance, not a CPU or NVIDIA GPU electrical specification. To make this a device-specific assignment, provide the exact processor and rail documentation, its allowed DC/load-line/AC deviation, transient current waveform and the port where each requirement is defined.

Then replace the teaching values and verify both impedance and time-domain response.

Where to spend the voltage budget. Use local energy storage for the fast load change and a short path to replenish it. The remote source alone cannot meet the fastest stated slew under the assumed inductance per length. If the actual device has a slower transient, recalculate the allocation before adding capacitors. These distance estimates are not layout dimensions for a named processor.
