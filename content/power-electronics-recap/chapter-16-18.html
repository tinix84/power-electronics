16.18 The source is rated 300 W: why can it not deliver 300 W through this cable?

Predict. A supply holds 24 V up to its 300 W output rating. A cable has 0.50 Ω total outgoing-plus-return resistance. At the far end, a regulated converter requests 300 W input power. Can its controller simply increase input current until it gets that power?

Ignoring cable inductance and transients for this steady-state calculation, KVL gives Vload = Vs − RI. Delivered load power is:

This parabola has a maximum at I = Vs/(2R), with load voltage Vs/2:

Even an unlimited stiff 24 V source could not deliver 300 W through this resistance in the stated model. At the 288 W maximum, the cable dissipates another 288 W, and the source must supply 576 W. This is the familiar maximum-power-transfer point, with only 50% efficiency; it is not an attractive design target.

Now apply the source rating. The actual source reaches 300 W at 12.5 A, before the parabola's 24 A peak. At 12.5 A, cable drop is 6.25 V, cable heating is 78.125 W, and the load receives only 221.875 W at 17.75 V. A supply may enter current limit, foldback or shutdown beyond its rating; it cannot be assumed to hold 24 V indefinitely.

Thus 288 W is the unrestricted cable-model bound, while about 222 W is the bound with this stipulated source limit.

Try a feasible request. At 200 W load power, the high-voltage solution is 18.633 V and 10.734 A. The cable dissipates 57.60 W, so source power is 257.60 W, within the source rating. Solving the quadratic can also produce a low-voltage/high-current root; it must be checked against source limits and converter operating range, not accepted merely because it is mathematically real.

Change one thing: halve cable resistance or raise transmission voltage while respecting device ratings. With half the resistance and the same 24 V / 300 W source limit, the maximum delivered power becomes 260.94 W. With a 48 V / 300 W source and the original 0.50 Ω cable, rated current is 6.25 A and the load can receive 280.47 W.

Explain both improvements through I²R rather than memorising a voltage preference.

Why the basic matters: source watts and delivered watts are different quantities. A constant-power converter asks for more current when its input voltage falls; cable drop then increases further. These static limits complement the negative incremental resistance and dynamic input-filter discussion in §§1.5 and 12.5–12.6. A feasible operating-point root alone does not establish startup success or closed-loop stability.

Cable or bus voltage? Reject the 300 W load request with the stated source and cable. Halving cable resistance raises delivered power only to 260.94 W. Moving to 48 V with the same 300 W source gives 280.47 W. Neither delivers 300 W at the load.

The 200 W request has a feasible static operating point. Keep it only if the converter accepts 18.633 V and starts reliably. If 300 W at the load is mandatory, revise both the source allowance and delivery path.
