16. Practical example bank: why the fundamentals matter

A familiar fault rarely arrives with the name of the right equation. It arrives as a hot resistor, a false current reading or a waveform that makes no sense. Start there.

This bank uses the practical approach associated with Jim Williams, Bob Pease, Henry Ott and Christophe Basso: pose a concrete problem, trace the mechanism and test an explanation. Source links identify the inspiration. The numerical exercises, simplified models and artwork are our own.

On a first reading, pause at the question and sketch your answer. Then follow the calculation and change one feature. On a return visit, use the index below to find the symptom and the first check. Keep a note of what you changed, what happened and why you accepted the result. In your answer, name one benefit, one cost and a condition that would change your choice. For a diagnosis, your choice may be the next measurement.

Four puzzles around one DC link. Start with hot-plug overshoot (§16.3), then use the remote-capacitor switching problem (§16.13), regenerative braking (§16.14) and capacitor equalisation/precharge (§16.15). Ask students to identify the event and initial conditions before choosing an equation. The governing ideas change from resonance to current slew, energy absorption and charge sharing.
