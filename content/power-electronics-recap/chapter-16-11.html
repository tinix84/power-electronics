16.11 Optional converter-design extension: extract and tune a series-RC snubber

Design task. A switching-node ringing tail suggests an LC resonance. Estimate the effective parasitics from two frequency measurements, select a series-RC branch, then compare voltage stress and heat. This extends section 16.6: the resistor is now in series with a capacitor, rather than continuously across the node. It is detailed worked material for a later converter lecture, outside the two-session core.

The method follows the frequency-shift approach in Nexperia AN11160, Designing RC Snubbers. All numbers and waveforms below form an authored synthetic measurement/design exercise, not recorded hardware data or a reproduction of Nexperia's device example.

A. First confirm the ring belongs to the circuit

Use a short, consistent measurement return as in section 16.1. Keep operating voltage, load, probe loading and component placement fixed between tests. Identify the same ringing mode before and after adding a known, low-inductance test capacitor directly across the ringing node and its local return. The test assumes the effective loop inductance stays unchanged and capacitance can be treated as linear around the observed excursion.

Suppose the fitted natural frequencies are f₀ = 50 MHz before the test and f₁ = 25 MHz with Ctest = 1.2 nF added. From the two LC equations:

The inferred Cp includes the effective capacitance present during the test, including the probe. Subtract or otherwise account for known probe loading if the probe will not remain in service. These are effective circuit parameters, not necessarily individual package or device values. Nonlinear Coss, multiple resonances and a changed ringing operating point can invalidate the simple extraction.

Damping refinement: an oscilloscope measures the damped frequency fd, rather than exactly the natural frequency fn. If the envelope is proportional to exp(−αt):

For this fixture, the synthetic tails have fd ≈ 49.975 MHz and 24.951 MHz, with α ≈ 9.870 × 10⁶ s⁻¹. Correcting gives the 50 and 25 MHz values above. Neglecting the correction is a small approximation here, but can be poor for a strongly damped ring.

The script checks the decay and period from successive positive peaks. Under the assumed series-loss model, the decay also gives Rloop = 2Lpα = 0.5 Ω.

B. Choose the starting capacitor and resistor

Use a first capacitor candidate Cs = 3Cp = 1.2 nF. This is a starting trade-off, not a universal optimum. The direct test capacitor is removed or reused as the final capacitor behind a series resistor; do not leave an unintended extra 1.2 nF directly across the node.

The original tank's characteristic impedance is:

Choose the nearby standard value Rs = 8.2 Ω and check it with the actual series-RC network. The snubber time constant is RsCs = 9.84 ns, much shorter than the illustrative 5 µs half-period at 100 kHz and 50% duty. At 50 MHz, the capacitor's reactance magnitude is 2.65 Ω, so the branch presents a substantial real component in the ring band while blocking steady DC current.

Do not label this automatically “critical damping.” The added capacitor creates a third-order network. The best resistance depends on the capacitance ratio, source impedance and commutation waveform. The numerical sweep below checks 2.2, 4.7, 8.2, 15 and 33 Ω for the same 1.2 nF capacitor.

C. Compare the same finite edge in three circuits

Use an idealized edge-test fixture: a prescribed 0 → 48 V linear rise in 5 ns, followed by a held input, drives Rloop = 0.5 Ω and Lp = 25.33 nH in series. The output has the 400 pF parasitic capacitance to return. Compare no added branch, a direct 1.2 nF capacitor, and the 8.2 Ω + 1.2 nF series branch. Initially all currents and capacitor voltages are zero.

The series RC cuts overshoot amplitude by approximately 60% and shortens settling substantially in this model. The direct capacitor mainly changes the frequency; it does not provide the desired damping. It also increases charging current and energy throughput. The circuit already has 0.5 Ω loss, so “capacitor only” is not literally lossless.

The final 63.73 V peak would still be unsuitable for a hypothetical 60 V absolute-maximum device. The example demonstrates improvement, not an automatic device-rating pass. More capacitance, a different resistance, lower loop inductance, controlled slew rate, a clamp or a different device rating may be required. Recheck tolerances and operating corners after any change.

At fixed Cs, the selected 8.2 Ω gives the lowest peak among the five sampled resistor values. Neither “as little resistance as possible” nor “as much resistance as possible” gives the best result. This is a discrete tuning comparison, not proof of a global optimum.

D. Check losses without double-counting the ringing energy

A useful fast-edge budget for a capacitor charged and discharged through a full bus swing once per switching cycle is:

There are two edges per cycle: ½CsV² per edge, not CsV² per edge. This budget assumes complete charge/discharge. Finite edge duration, the actual voltage across Cs and the surrounding converter determine the real loss distribution.

For the explicit linear fixture, integrate each resistor's instantaneous heating over one rising edge. A falling edge from the fully settled high state has equal heating by linear symmetry. Multiply the one-edge integrals by 2fs:

Model dissipation at 100 kHz, two settled edges per cycle No snubber Direct 1.2 nF only Series RC
Existing 0.5 Ω loop resistance 0.0750 W 0.3504 W 0.0382 W
Added 8.2 Ω resistor 0 W 0 W 0.3131 W
Total model resistor loss 0.0750 W 0.3504 W 0.3513 W

The added total loss is 0.2762 W, close to the simple 0.2765 W budget. But the snubber resistor itself loses 0.3131 W: it also takes over some dissipation that previously occurred in the loop. Thus CsV²fs is an added-system-loss estimate, not an exact component-temperature calculation. The no-snubber total is slightly below the ideal abrupt-step CpV²fs budget because the model uses a finite 5 ns drive.

Do not add another leakage-energy term to these already integrated losses: the simulated currents and energy balance already include the tank dynamics. A real converter may have additional recovery/commutation energy absent from this fixture, and it requires a full topology-specific accounting.

E. Average power is not enough for resistor selection

The simulated snubber resistor sees approximately 4.52 A peak and 167 W peak instantaneous power, despite only 0.313 W cycle-average heating. For a separate ideal forced-discharge estimate, if a fully charged 1.2 nF capacitor is suddenly discharged through 8.2 Ω:

This discharge estimate is a different limiting waveform, not an extra pulse to add to the simulated energy. Check repetitive pulse energy, pulse duration, peak voltage, average-power derating and mounting conditions in the selected resistor datasheet. A hypothetical 0.125 W part is inadequate for the calculated average loss; even a nominal 0.5 W part is only a candidate until its pulse and thermal limits are checked.

Use a suitable low-inductance capacitor and short snubber loop, and verify its voltage and pulse-current capability. Extra lead/trace inductance can bypass the intended high-frequency damping.

Snubber choice. Retain 8.2 Ω with 1.2 nF as the starting candidate: it gives the lowest peak among the five sampled resistances. Adding the capacitor alone has almost the same total model loss, but does not provide the same tuned damping.

The RC resistor dissipates 0.3131 W and sees a much larger pulse peak. Carry those stresses into component selection. If the voltage target still fails, compare new RC values rather than assuming more capacitance is free.

Model limits: this is a linear edge fixture with constant parasitic capacitance and an imposed source that can absorb current. It excludes nonlinear Coss, diode/body-diode clamping, reverse recovery, driver impedance, snubber ESL and temperature. The full-cycle loss comparison assumes complete settling and symmetric edges. It is not a complete half-bridge or flyback simulation, and its peaks are illustrative rather than hardware ratings.
