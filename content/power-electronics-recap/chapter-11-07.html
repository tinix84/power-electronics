11.7 Common and differential modes: choose a more useful vector basis

Two conductor measurements can hide a simple physical pattern. The common-mode direction is (1, 1): both conductors move together. The differential-mode direction is (1, −1): they move oppositely. These directions are orthogonal, so any two-component voltage or current vector has a unique sum of the two patterns. This is a change of coordinates, not an extra physical current or a filter that removes noise.

Define the ports first. Take two conductors at the same cable cross-section. Measure v1 and v2 relative to the same reference, and orient i1 and i2 in the same longitudinal direction. A normal outgoing/return current then has i1 = −i2. Do not copy the entering currents at opposite ends of an ABCD two-port into these equations without checking their arrows.

A conductor pair at one reference plane supplies two coordinates; a complete input/output differential interconnect generally needs four single-ended ports to retain both modes at both ends.

Orthogonal projections onto sum and difference

Normalize the basis vectors to unit length. For either voltage or current, the modal coordinates are

In matrix form:

Q = (1/sqrt(2)) [ 1   1 ]
                [ 1  -1 ]

v_modal = Q v_conductor       i_modal = Q i_conductor
Q Q^T = I                    v_conductor = Q^T v_modal

Geometrically the axes lie at ±45° to the original conductor axes. With the displayed signs and CM-first ordering, det Q = −1: strictly this is an orthogonal basis change, including an orientation reversal, rather than a pure rotation matrix. Reversing one modal axis gives a rotation convention without changing the physics. The useful insight is the projection onto the symmetric and antisymmetric directions.

For measured currents (3, −1) A, the normalized coordinates are √2 A and 2√2 A. Reconstruct the physical conductor contributions:

(3, -1) A = (1, 1) A + (2, -2) A
             CM          DM

The coordinates tell us how much of each basis vector is present; the two vectors on the right show the corresponding currents in the original conductors.

Some EMC references call half the conductor-current sum the CM current per conductor. State the convention before comparing values or power formulas. Analog Devices' common-mode explanation uses the average of the conductor voltages for common-mode voltage.

Power recall: with v = (12, 8) V and i = (3, −1) A, vCM = 10 V, vDM = 4 V, iCM = 2 A and iDM = 2 A. Original power is 12 × 3 + 8 × (−1) = 28 W. Modal power is 10 × 2 + 4 × 2 = 20 W + 8 W.

A coordinate transformation has changed neither the circuit nor its power.

Symmetry explains when the modes decouple

For a conductor-domain relation v = Zi, using orthonormal coordinates gives

A reciprocal, exchange-symmetric two-conductor network has the following form:

Z = [ zs  zm ]          Q Z Q^T = [ zs+zm    0   ]
    [ zm  zs ]                    [   0    zs-zm ]

Its symmetric and antisymmetric directions are eigenvectors. CM current creates only CM voltage, and DM current only DM voltage. The basis change always exists, but decoupling requires the circuit symmetry. For example:

Balanced:    Z = [30 10] ohm     normalized modal Z = [40  0] ohm
                 [10 30]                             [ 0 20]

Unbalanced:  Z = [35 10] ohm     normalized modal Z = [40  5] ohm
                 [10 25]                             [ 5 20]

The off-diagonal 5 Ω terms expose mode conversion. In the engineering normalization above, these modal matrices become diag(20, 40) Ω and [[20, 5], [5, 40]] Ω respectively. Modal impedance numbers depend on normalization, while reconstructed conductor voltages and currents agree. For the unbalanced network, a pure differential loop current of 1 A, i = (1, −1) A, gives v = (25, −15) V: vDM = 40 V and vCM = 5 V.

Differential excitation has created a common-mode response.

Why this helps EMC analysis, and where the PCA analogy stops

A current probe enclosing both conductors responds to their signed sum: the differential components cancel and the remaining net current is iCM in the stated convention. A nonzero net conductor current needs a return elsewhere, such as chassis or parasitic capacitance. The basis makes this external return requirement visible; it does not identify that return path by itself or predict radiation from a modal amplitude alone.

Common and differential modes can contain useful signals as well as disturbances.

Section 16.27 is a concrete example. Balanced interwinding capacitances preserve symmetry: secondary CM injection gives primary CM motion with zero primary differential voltage in the stated fixture. Unequal capacitances introduce mode conversion. Keeping only a differential transformer model would hide that excitation and response.

The analogy with principal component analysis (PCA) is useful because both project vectors onto a new orthogonal basis. In CM/DM analysis the basis is fixed by conductor exchange symmetry and physical interpretation. PCA learns directions from the data covariance and orders them by explained variance; see the scikit-learn PCA guide.

If a real two-channel covariance matrix has equal diagonal entries, the sum/difference directions form an eigenbasis. With nonzero cross-covariance these directions are fixed up to signs and ordering; with zero cross-covariance the equal eigenvalues allow any orthonormal basis. Arbitrary data need not have CM/DM as their principal components.

A change of basis retaining both modes loses no information. Discarding one mode is an additional approximation.
