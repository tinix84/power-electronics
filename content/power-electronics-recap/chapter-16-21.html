16.21 A T-network: when can Z be inverted to obtain Y?

Predict. Take a T-shaped circuit, not a transformer: series impedances Za and Zb connect the input and output to a middle node; impedance Zc connects that node to the common return. Derive Z, then decide whether Y = Z⁻¹ always exists.

Port 1 -- Za --o-- Zb -- Port 2
              |
              Zc
              |
         common return

Z = [ Za + Zc     Zc    ]
    [   Zc     Zb + Zc ]

Its determinant is ZaZb + ZaZc + ZbZc. It is not identically zero: for three positive resistances, both finite Z and Y exist. For example, Za = Zb = Zc = 10 Ω gives Z₁₁ = Z₂₂ = 20 Ω, Z₁₂ = Z₂₁ = 10 Ω and determinant 300 Ω². The topology alone does not prevent the conversion.

Make the inverse fail at a specific frequency. Replace both series arms by ideal 10 µH inductors, and the shunt arm by an ideal 1 µF capacitor. With Za = Zb = sL and Zc = 1/(sC):

At this frequency, each series impedance is j4.472 Ω and the shunt impedance is −j2.236 Ω. None of the three branch impedances is zero or infinite, yet:

Z(fr) = j 2.236 ohm [ 1  -1 ]
                   [-1   1 ]
det Z(fr) = 0.

Z is a valid finite representation, but it is singular, so no finite unrestricted Y = Z⁻¹ exists at that ideal frequency. In particular, equal entering currents I₁ = I₂ give V₁ = V₂ = 0. The inverse problem cannot uniquely recover currents from prescribed port voltages. In common mode, the two inductors act in parallel against the shunt capacitor; the ideal resonant mode explains the singularity physically.

Can another representation still work? Yes. At fr, ABCD is finite and invertible:

T(fr) = [    -1          0 ]
        [ j 0.4472 S    -1 ]
det T(fr) = 1.

The equal-real-reference scattering matrix is also finite. Thus det Z = 0 does not mean the physical two-port is undefined or that its transmission matrix must have zero determinant. Add 0.10 Ω series resistance to each inductor: at the same frequency, det Z becomes approximately 0.010 + j0.4472 Ω², restoring a finite Y. The ideal singularity becomes a loss-limited resonant response; small parasitics can matter greatly near it.

Change one thing: compare three resistors, the ideal L–C–L T, and the same T with winding resistance. Ask students to identify the independent variables and check the determinant before calling a matrix inverse. The numerical script verifies all three cases and the finite ABCD/S descriptions.

Additional comparison: an ideal transformer has different constraints

Predict. Describe an ideal transformer with ratio n = N₁/N₂ = 2, using the signs of §11.4. Can you measure its open-circuit Z parameters by independently imposing I₁ with I₂ = 0?

The transformer imposes V₁ = nV₂ and I₁ = −I₂/n. Open-circuiting port 2 forces I₁ = 0, so an arbitrary nonzero input test current cannot be imposed within this ideal model. Likewise, its two port voltages cannot be independently prescribed for Y measurements. There is no finite unrestricted Z matrix or Y matrix for the ideal transformer. Its transmission and equal-real-reference scattering matrices are nevertheless well defined:

T = ABCD = [ n    0  ]        For n = 2:  S = [ 0.6   0.8 ]
           [ 0   1/n ]                      [ 0.8  -0.6 ]

The transmission determinant is 1, not zero. With port 2 matched, reflected and transmitted power fractions are 0.6² = 0.36 and 0.8² = 0.64, summing to one for this lossless network. The winding voltage ratio remains V₂/V₁ = 0.5, while S₂₁ is 0.8; these are different quantities.

Where does the singular inverse appear? For equal real R₀, write the conversions using the identity matrix Id:

Z = R0 (Id + S) (Id - S)^(-1)
Y = (1/R0) (Id - S) (Id + S)^(-1)

For this ideal transformer, both Id − S and Id + S are singular. The required inverses fail. It would be incorrect to invent a finite Z matrix, assign it determinant zero and claim that was the transformer's missing representation.

Two simpler counterexamples. A two-port consisting only of a nonzero series impedance Zs between its signal terminals, with common return, has a valid singular Y matrix. A nonzero shunt admittance Yp at a through-connected signal node has a valid singular Z matrix:

Series impedance only:                 Shunt admittance only:
Y = (1/Zs) [ 1  -1 ]                   Z = (1/Yp) [ 1   1 ]
           [-1   1 ]                             [ 1   1 ]
det(Y) = 0; no finite unrestricted Z.   det(Z) = 0; no finite unrestricted Y.

A singular matrix can therefore be a valid representation in its own variables, while preventing conversion to another representation. The resonant T-network above has a finite singular Z; the ideal transformer has no finite unrestricted Z in the first place. These are different reasons that a requested parameter conversion can fail.

Why the basic matters: a matrix representation is a choice of independent variables, not a universal mandatory description. Keep the network's constitutive constraints, the existence of a representation and the invertibility of a parameter matrix separate. Practical transformer parasitics can regularize ideal constraints over an applicable frequency range, but do not justify silently converting the ideal transformer with a singular inverse.

Representation choice. Use parameters whose independent variables remain valid for the circuit. Z is convenient for open-circuit relations; ABCD helps with cascades. At a singular conversion, keep a valid representation or solve the circuit constraints directly. Adding fictitious loss merely to invert a matrix changes the problem.
