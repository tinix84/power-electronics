16.17 The precharge resistor passes average watts: why can it still overheat?

Predict. Reuse §16.15's isolated two-capacitor equalisation: two 1 mF capacitors, 48 V initial difference and a 10 Ω resistor. The resistor sees 230.4 W initial power, 0.576 J total energy, and a 5 ms electrical RC time constant. It is not a constant 230.4 W pulse lasting 5 ms:

The power-decay time constant is 2.5 ms, and integrating power gives the known 0.576 J. Now assume the authored thermal node from §13.3: Rth = 100 K/W, Cth = 0.020 J/K. Its 2 s thermal time constant is much longer than the electrical pulse. The approximately adiabatic estimate is:

Solving the thermal equation with the actual exponential power gives a 28.56 K peak at 16.73 ms from cold. The slight reduction comes from heat escaping during the event. A short energy pulse can produce appreciable temperature rise despite a small long-term average power.

Repeat the event once per second. Assume the initial capacitor voltages are restored externally before each event; simply leaving the equalised capacitors connected does not repeat the pulse. Average resistor power is 0.576 W, giving 57.6 K mean temperature rise. But the steady periodic peak is 72.67 K above ambient, about 14.40 ms after each connection, because each event starts from a warm baseline.

At 40 °C ambient, this model predicts approximately 112.7 °C peak, not the 97.6 °C inferred from mean power alone. A hypothetical 60 K temperature-rise budget would pass the average calculation and fail the peak calculation.

Figure 63 compares the isolated and repetitive cases. The peak temperature was computed by convolution with the one-node thermal impulse response; integrating heat flow plus the change in stored thermal energy independently recovers the pulse energy.

Change one thing: compare three different interventions. Increasing thermal mass reduces short-event temperature rise; improving the heat path lowers steady/accumulated temperature; increasing the repetition interval gives more cooling. Changing the electrical precharge resistor changes the pulse shape and duration, but the equalisation energy remains 0.576 J for the stated isolated initial/final conditions. With a sufficiently long electrical pulse, more energy leaves during precharge, reducing its temperature peak.

What this does and does not select. Rth and Cth above are illustrative parameters, not a specific 10 Ω part. A real precharge resistor also needs a pulse-energy rating for the waveform/duration, applicable voltage/current limits, repetitive endurance and mounting-dependent thermal data. Its internal resistive layer can be hotter than the package surface.

Manufacturer transient data or an appropriate multi-node model is preferable to assigning the whole body one thermal capacitance. A stiff 48 V source charging one empty 1 mF capacitor dissipates 1.152 J in the resistance (§16.15), so it is a different thermal load. See Vishay's pulse-loading guidance.

Cooling or repetition? At one event per second, use the 72.67 K periodic peak for the thermal check, not the 57.6 K mean rise. More thermal mass reduces short-event rise; a better heat path reduces accumulated heating.

If throughput permits, a longer interval is another route. Keep the one-second schedule only after a suitable part and mounting meet the peak-temperature and pulse limits. No part is selected by the illustrative thermal node alone.
