1. Networks: what the source actually supplies

1.1 Voltage, current, power and reference directions

Voltage is the potential difference between two nodes. Current is the rate of charge transfer.

Ground is a reference. A ground symbol defines a reference potential. The symbol alone does not identify protective earth. It does not guarantee an ideal conductor or galvanic isolation.

Power depends on the reference directions. Use the passive sign convention: current enters the terminal marked positive. With these references, positive instantaneous power means that the element absorbs energy.

Worked example continued. The divider of section 1.2 is, as a Norton source, 12 mA in parallel with 500 Ω. The 1 kΩ load takes 12 mA × 500/(500 + 1000) = 4 mA, that is 4 V, the same answer as before. Use the Thévenin form when the question is about voltage drop under load and the Norton form when branches share a current; a source transformation never changes what the load sees.

Converter connection: a regulated supply still has an output impedance. In a local model, a load-current perturbation causes a voltage perturbation approximately equal to minus the supply impedance times that current perturbation.

Choose the convenient equivalent. Thévenin makes voltage drop easy to see; Norton makes branch-current division easy to see. Both describe the same linear port. Change representation when it simplifies the question, retaining dependent sources and the same load.

1.4 Units, prefixes and reference levels

Keep physical units in calculations: Ω = V/A, F = A·s/V and H = V·s/A. For example, nH × A/ns gives volts directly. Remember m = 10⁻³, µ = 10⁻⁶, n = 10⁻⁹ and p = 10⁻¹²; confusing millimetres with metres changes a geometry estimate by a factor of one thousand.

Dimensional-analysis check. For the plate-capacitance estimate , permittivity has units F/m, relative permittivity is dimensionless, area has units m² and separation has units m. Thus (F/m) × m²/m = F. Reversing A/d would fail this check. Also check the physical trend: larger overlap increases capacitance, while greater separation reduces it. Section 3 applies this estimate to PCB conductors.

A decibel is a logarithmic ratio, not an independent physical quantity. An absolute level needs a reference: dBV refers to 1 V, dBµV to 1 µV, and dBm to 1 mW. For the same RMS voltage, the numerical dBµV level is 120 dB above its dBV level.

Converting dBm to voltage also requires the impedance. Section 12 explains amplitude versus power ratios; never add a dBm power level to a voltage without defining the conversion.

1.5 Static resistance versus incremental resistance

An ideal positive resistor has both a positive voltage/current ratio and a positive slope. An actively regulated load can behave differently. For an approximately constant input power P, its input current is P/V: a small drop in input voltage makes it demand more current. The DC ratio V/I is still positive, but the incremental resistance is negative.

This is a local model of the controlled load, not a passive resistor delivering free energy. Section 12.5 connects this behaviour to input-filter stability and Middlebrook's impedance criterion.
