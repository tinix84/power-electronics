13. Semiconductor and thermal basics

Before detailed converter topologies, students should be able to sketch the basic states of a diode and MOSFET, identify current paths, and explain why an inductor needs a commutation path.

  • Diode: ideal unidirectional conduction; forward-drop and differential-resistance models; junction capacitance and reverse recovery as later refinements.
  • MOSFET: voltage-controlled channel, gate charge, on-resistance and body diode. Gate voltage is measured relative to the source. Threshold voltage is not a promise of low on-resistance.
  • Switching versus pass operation: an ideal on-switch has zero voltage and an ideal off-switch has zero current, so both have zero instantaneous loss. Real switching transitions overlap voltage and current and cause loss. A pass transistor can sustain both simultaneously.
  • Safe operating area: voltage, current, time and temperature limits must all be respected. Low on-resistance alone does not establish suitability for linear pass operation.

Where does come from? Not from a resistor inside the package. It is read from the output characteristic: the family of versus curves, one per gate voltage, that a curve tracer measures. An amplifier biases the transistor in the flat, current-source part of these curves. A switch uses only two places on the same graph: the vertical axis when off () and the steep region near the origin when on (). Avoid the middle, where voltage and current coexist; that is where a linear regulator lives (section 14).

The on-resistances are 10 mΩ and 15 mΩ, and the conduction losses are 4.0 W and 6.0 W. With the assumed 1.5× hot-resistance multiplier of section 13.2, they become 6.0 W and 9.0 W.

Drive at 10 V or at 6 V? The lower gate voltage saves gate-drive power and may allow a cheaper driver supply. It costs 2 W at 25 °C in this example, and that heat needs copper or a heat sink. The higher gate voltage is preferable whenever conduction loss dominates. The lower one becomes reasonable at light load, or when the gate-drive supply is fixed by another part. Check the gate's absolute maximum voltage before driving harder: it is typically about ±20 V for silicon MOSFETs and much lower for many GaN devices.

Switching frequency, magnetic size and loss. For a fixed applied winding voltage and duty ratio, on-time falls as switching frequency rises. The relation in section 2.4 therefore allows a smaller turns–area product for the same flux swing. Similarly, the inductor ripple relation allows a lower inductance for the same ripple.

These relations help explain why higher switching frequency can reduce magnetic size. Actual size also depends on energy storage, winding space, core loss and cooling.

The tradeoff is visible in the switching-loss equation: with unchanged energy per transition, switching loss rises with frequency. Core and AC winding losses also depend on frequency and waveform, while fast edges increase sensitivity to parasitic inductance and capacitive coupling. Choose frequency together with the devices, magnetics, thermal design and EMC requirements; neither low nor high switching frequency guarantees high efficiency.

Si, SiC and GaN: a first orientation. Si means silicon, SiC silicon carbide and GaN gallium nitride. SiC and GaN are wide-bandgap semiconductors whose properties can enable higher-voltage and/or faster-switching power devices than comparable silicon implementations. Lower transition losses can make higher switching frequency and smaller magnetics practical.

The material name alone does not determine efficiency or a safe switching frequency: compare actual device ratings, switching energy, conduction loss, gate-drive requirements, layout and cooling at the intended operating point. This is an introduction to device selection, not a claim that every SiC or GaN design outperforms every silicon design.

13.1 Thermal resistance and capacitance: use the RC concepts again

Heat flow can be modelled with the same tools as a first-order electrical circuit:

Electrical quantity Thermal analogue Thermal units
Voltage difference Temperature rise ΔT K; a temperature difference of 1 K equals 1 °C
Current Heat flow / dissipated power P W
Resistance R Thermal resistance Rth K/W
Capacitance C Thermal capacitance Cth J/K
Stored charge CΔV Stored thermal energy CthΔT, for constant Cth J
RC time constant Rth Cth s

Rth sets the steady rise; Cth sets how much energy is needed to change temperature. For a uniform body, Cth = mass × specific heat capacity. A real semiconductor or resistor has several spatial regions and time scales, so a single whole-package thermal capacitance may miss a hot junction or resistive-film hot spot. 13.2 TI semiconductor example: the same MOSFET on different copper areas

Predict. A low-resistance MOSFET carries 40 A continuously in a 12 V ORing path. Does its small electrical loss guarantee a cool junction?

Use TI's CSD17570Q5B, a 30 V ORing/hot-swap MOSFET that TI explicitly does not intend for switching applications. The SLPS471D datasheet, pages 1, 3–4, gives:

Published quantity Value / condition
Maximum on-resistance 0.69 mΩ at VGS = 10 V, ID = 50 A, 25 °C
Maximum junction-to-case RθJC 0.8 K/W
Maximum junction-to-ambient board cases in figure 1 50 K/W with 1 inch² of 2 oz copper; 125 K/W with minimum pad area
Maximum operating junction temperature 150 °C

For an authored 40 A, 40 °C ambient scenario, hold resistance at its 25 °C value initially: P = 40² × 0.00069 = 1.104 W. The two board estimates are 95.2 °C and 178.0 °C. The latter exceeds the operating limit; it is a failed screening estimate, not a valid operating point. These boundary-dependent thermal metrics are not interchangeable; see TI SPRA953D.

Change one thing: improve the PCB heat path. Then repeat the loss calculation with temperature-dependent resistance. For sensitivity only, an assumed 1.5× resistance multiplier gives 1.656 W and 122.8 °C / 247.0 °C in the two fixed thermal paths. This multiplier is not a guaranteed TI hot-resistance specification or a converged electrothermal solution.

A real design uses the applicable temperature curve/data, solves loss and temperature consistently and checks margins and current/voltage limits.

Do not replace RθJA by RθJC while keeping ambient as the reference: that discards the case-to-surroundings heat path. A junction-to-case calculation needs the relevant case temperature and boundary conditions. Nor should RθJC be added to RθJA; the latter already represents the junction-to-ambient system under its stated conditions.

PCB copper, vias, airflow and nearby heat sources change the actual result. The ordinary data-sheet θJA value is a comparison/test-condition metric, not a universal property of a package on every board.

Keep the larger-copper path as the candidate for this 40 A example. The minimum-pad case exceeds the junction limit in the stated calculation. A lower-resistance MOSFET is an alternative way to reduce heat, but requires a new electrical and thermal comparison. Neither approach is qualified by the cold on-resistance alone.

13.3 A short power pulse is not a steady-state load

For a constant power pulse starting at ambient, the one-node model gives:

Zth is the thermal step-response impedance in K/W. At long times it approaches Rth. At short times compared with the applicable thermal time constant, little heat escapes and ΔT ≈ E/Cth, where E is the deposited energy. For a normalized manufacturer Zth curve, first multiply by its stated reference thermal resistance. Match the curve's junction/case/ambient reference and boundary conditions to the calculation.

Worked RC example. Assume Rth = 100 K/W, Cth = 0.020 J/K, hence τth = 2 s. A 10 W, 10 ms rectangular pulse deposits 0.1 J and produces 4.99 K rise from cold. Multiplying 10 W by the steady 100 K/W would predict 1000 K and misuse a steady-state equation for this short event.

Repeat the pulse every 1 s. Average power is only 0.1 W, so the eventual mean rise is 10 K, but the periodic temperature moves between 7.73 K and 12.68 K above ambient. The isolated 4.99 K result cannot be reused from a cold baseline on every cycle. For this one-node model:

Adding square-pulse step responses also works for a linear thermal model. A short-pulse junction-temperature check still does not replace semiconductor SOA or resistor electrical pulse limits.

The Rth/Cth values are not fitted to the TI MOSFET or to a catalog resistor. Sections 16.16–16.17 apply pulse-energy reasoning to gate and precharge resistors.

Thermal choice. Use transient thermal response for the 10 ms event; steady thermal resistance gives the wrong temperature estimate. For an isolated pulse, check local pulse capability and stored heat first.

For repeated or sustained operation, improve the heat path or allow more cooling time. The first costs cooling hardware; the second costs throughput. Neither replaces the part's pulse qualification.
