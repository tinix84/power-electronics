16.30 A MOSFET has 95 pF at 400 V: does that determine its stored energy?

Riddle: a data-sheet capacitance is specified at one drain voltage. Can that value be inserted into both E = CV²/2 and transition time t = CV/I over a large voltage swing?

For a voltage-dependent, single-valued differential capacitance, C(v) = dQ/dv. Charge and stored energy require different integrals:

The energy integrand is voltage-weighted. A local small-signal capacitance C(V) is generally neither Q(V)/V nor 2E(V)/V². Infineon's Coss explanation distinguishes output capacitance and effective capacitances used for charge/time and energy calculations. Actual device values must use their stated bias and voltage range.

At 400 V:

Quantity Result What it describes
Local C(400 V) 95.24 pF Differential capacitance at that bias
Q(400 V) 121.78 nC Charge transferred from 0 to 400 V
E(400 V) 13.564 µJ Stored energy in this reversible model
Q/V 304.45 pF Constant capacitance with the same total charge
2E/V² 169.55 pF Constant capacitance with the same stored energy
Incorrect local-C estimate, C(400 V)V²/2 7.619 µJ Underestimates the stated energy by about 44%

With an imposed constant 1 A charging current, transition time is Q/I = 121.78 ns. Using only C(400 V)V/I instead gives 38.10 ns. As the capacitance falls, the voltage slew increases: constant charging current does not imply constant dv/dt. The exact voltage is V0[exp(It/(C0V0)) − 1] over this model's charging interval.

Energy is not automatically loss. If the full 13.564 µJ is dissipated once per cycle at 100 kHz, that particular contribution is 1.356 W. If energy is recovered into an inductive network, or partly dissipated elsewhere, the switch loss differs. Nor is comparing LI²/2 with one Eoss value a complete ZVS design: all relevant capacitances, circuit constraints, current direction and available commutation time must be checked.

The authored model excludes hysteresis, voltage-history dependence, gate coupling and other switching losses.

Change one thing: use the same final voltage but half the charging current. Charge and stored energy stay the same, while transition time doubles. Then compare two C(v) curves with the same local value at 400 V but different low-voltage behaviour. Their total charge and energy need not agree. This is a practical example of why a differential quantity cannot replace its integral.

Capacitance-model choice. Use the local value for a sufficiently small voltage change. Use the charge or energy integral for the large swing shown here. The fuller model needs voltage-dependent data. Halving current extends charging time; it does not reduce the stored energy at the same final voltage.
