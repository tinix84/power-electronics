12.5 Negative incremental resistance and Middlebrook's input-filter criterion

Start with a regulated load. Suppose a converter holds its output power constant and its efficiency is approximately constant over a small input-voltage change. Its input power P is then approximately constant over frequencies where regulation can maintain that behaviour:

The static DC ratio and the incremental resistance have equal magnitudes and opposite signs in this idealization:

Example: 60 W input power at 12 V means 5 A input current, a positive static ratio of 2.4 Ω, and incremental input resistance −2.4 Ω. A 0.1 V input drop calls for approximately 41.7 mA more current in the linearized model. The load still absorbs positive average power. Negative incremental resistance describes the response of a perturbation, not reversal of the DC power flow.

This is why a regulated converter connected after an LC input filter can remove damping: a small decrease in filter-output voltage makes the load demand more current, which can reinforce the voltage decrease. An ordinary positive resistor reduces its current when its voltage falls. The constant-power approximation is local and bandwidth-limited; startup, dropout, current limiting, changing efficiency and high-frequency device/input-capacitor behaviour require the actual converter impedance.

Keeping source impedance well below converter input-impedance magnitude gives useful design margin and is commonly called the Middlebrook impedance-separation rule. For a general model the bound must hold across the relevant frequency range, with the remaining dynamics also accounted for. A comparison of DC resistances alone is insufficient: an input-filter resonance can make source impedance peak far above its DC value.

Magnitude-rule failure is not itself proof of instability. When the ratio reaches or exceeds unity, assess its phase and the Nyquist plot relative to −1, accounting for any right-half-plane poles of the open interaction loop. The full frequency-dependent impedances matter. This source/load interaction loop is also distinct from the converter's internal voltage-control loop.

Classroom screening exercise: at a frequency where the 60 W / 12 V load can be approximated by −2.4 Ω, a source/filter output-impedance peak of 0.24 Ω gives |Tm| = 0.1, or −20 dB. A 4.8 Ω peak gives |Tm| = 2, or +6.02 dB; the conservative magnitude condition fails there, so phase/full-model analysis is needed.

The first value is a check at that frequency, not a certificate for the entire frequency range.

To reduce interaction, lower source impedance, damp the filter resonance, choose a suitable filter corner, and check the converter/input-capacitor combination. A series resistor provides damping but also DC loss. A properly designed series-RC damping branch across the filter output can add damping without the same continuous DC loss, since its capacitor blocks DC. Its values must be designed from the actual filter and load model.

This connects Thévenin → incremental resistance → parallel resonance → Bode/Nyquist → stable input filtering. Middlebrook's original paper develops the interaction problem and emphasizes frequency-dependent converter behaviour. TI's input-filter application note provides an application-oriented follow-up.
