16.33 The unstable pole cancels on paper: is the system internally stable?

Optional advanced riddle: put an unstable plant P(s) = 1/(s − a), a > 0, in cascade with a compensator C(s) = b(s − a)/(s + b), b > 0. The reduced reference-to-output transfer is

It looks like an ordinary stable low-pass. But cancelling a numerator/denominator factor in this zero-initial-state channel does not remove a physical state. Use this deliberately simple cascade counterexample before attempting a full feedback controller; the cascade is not a stabilizing feedback design.

One realization, with reference r and output y = x, is

Its internal eigenvalues are −b and +a. Define e = x − z. Then

With exactly e(0) = 0 and the exact model, the reference does not excite the unstable mode, so y = z and the stable reduced transfer predicts that particular experiment correctly. With a nonzero initial mismatch, a disturbance in the plant, or model error, the growing mode is exposed.

In this realization it is uncontrollable from the nominal reference channel but observable at the output; reduction of the transfer function concealed its existence.

Authored example: choose a = 1000 s⁻¹ and b = 2000 s⁻¹, with a unit reference step. From zero state the nominal output is 1 − exp(−2000t). Compare that with a 1 mV initial x − z mismatch, and separately a plant pole 1% higher, at +1010 s⁻¹, while leaving the compensator zero at +1000 s⁻¹.

Experiment Output after 6 ms
Exact model, zero initial state 0.999994 V
Exact model, 1 mV initial mismatch 1.403423 V
Plant pole +1%, zero initial state 3.808260 V

The full state realization, its resolvent and direct time integration agree. The lesson is stronger than finite numerical precision: even exact cancellation does not establish internal stability. Uncertainty merely makes the problem harder to conceal. The author-hosted Hoagg and Bernstein discussion of nonminimum-phase systems discusses the hidden instability associated with unstable pole–zero cancellation.

Change one thing: keep the same initial error but move the uncancelled internal pole into the left half-plane; the mismatch then decays. For an actual control design, use feedback to stabilize the physical unstable mode and assess all relevant disturbance channels. Stable-pole cancellation has its own robustness limitations, but the unstable case already fails the internal-stability requirement.

This completes §9.1's distinction between a transfer function and the full state dynamics. Saturation, finite precision and sensor noise are excluded from the model; they are not needed to produce the counterexample.

Control decision. Reject the unstable-mode cancellation as a stabilizing solution. Design feedback to stabilize the physical mode, then inspect internal states and disturbance paths. Reducing a transfer function is useful algebra; it cannot replace that check. These two descriptions are not equally valid evidence of stability.
