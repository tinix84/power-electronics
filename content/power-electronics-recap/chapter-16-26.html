16.26 A large input capacitor: why does the battery still supply the switching ripple?

Riddle: a buck draws a pulsed input current. A large capacitor is fitted directly across its input, but the battery current still looks almost like the switching pulses. Does increasing capacitance by ten solve the problem?

The topology matters. Here the resistance is in series with the capacitor, representing its ESR. It is not a resistor inserted in the main battery feed. The battery's relevant impedance is its output impedance plus connections, evaluated at the frequency of interest. This is the same current-sharing problem as §4.1, viewed from the buck input.

The capacitor collects most of the ripple only when its branch impedance is sufficiently smaller than the source branch impedance. A large capacitance alone does not guarantee this. Analog Devices AN44 explicitly discusses input-line ripple when the source impedance, including supply-line inductance, is low.

Authored example: use 100 kHz switching, a 0–10 A input-current pulse at 50% duty, and an effective battery/connection model of 5 mΩ in series with 20 nH. Initially use 470 µF, 0.20 Ω ESR and 2 nH ESL. These are teaching assumptions, not measured battery or component specifications. At 100 kHz,

Although the ideal capacitive reactance is only 3.39 mΩ, the complete capacitor branch is approximately 200 mΩ. The source branch is only 13.52 mΩ. The first harmonic of the ideal 0–10 A pulse has a peak amplitude of 6.366 A; apply the complex divider to this harmonic:

One change at a time Battery ripple / buck ripple at 100 kHz Battery first-harmonic peak
470 µF, ESR 0.20 Ω 97.44% 6.203 A
4700 µF, same ESR 97.35% 6.198 A
470 µF, ESR reduced to 2 mΩ 23.25% 1.480 A
Low-ESR case plus 1 µH / 20 mΩ DCR in the source feed 0.457% 0.0291 A

The capacitance increase barely helps because ESR dominates the branch. Lower ESR changes the current division; a series inductor increases the source-path impedance at switching frequency. The inductor belongs upstream of the local capacitor, so the small local switching loop can supply the buck pulses.

Phasor check: the two complex current fractions add to one. Their magnitudes generally do not. In the low-ESR case the capacitor's first-harmonic magnitude is 107.62% of the imposed buck harmonic while the battery fraction is 23.25%; the phase difference makes KCL hold. Reactive circulation can also produce resonance peaks. This is not power gain.

A quiet input voltage can hide a noisy supply current. Here the original high-ESR case has only 83.9 mV peak of first-harmonic port-voltage ripple, while the battery carries 6.20 A peak at that harmonic. In the limit of zero source impedance, the source supplies all the AC current and the rail-voltage ripple tends to zero. Checking only voltage ripple can therefore miss the input-current filtering problem.

Change one thing: lengthen the battery leads. Their inductance may improve high-frequency current isolation, but it also changes resonances and transient overvoltage. Compare the whole impedance curves, rather than treating long wiring as a qualified filter. Alternatively, add a suitable low-ESR local capacitor and deliberately design the series filter impedance.

Check capacitor ripple-current rating, effective capacitance, ESL and layout; check inductor saturation, DCR and damping. The extra 20 mΩ in this example already dissipates 0.5 W from the 5 A DC component alone, before ripple loss.

The added LC network has a resonance, visible as peaking in the frequency plot. Successful attenuation at 100 kHz does not establish broadband attenuation or converter stability: return to §§12.5–12.6 for the source/filter impedance interaction with the regulated buck. A resistor in the main feed is a different topology and also causes DC voltage drop and loss.

Likewise, the familiar ideal buck input-capacitor RMS formula assumes the source supplies approximately DC; it must not be applied unchanged to the poorly decoupled high-ESR case.

Decoupling choice. Reduce ESR before adding more of the same capacitance. Ten times more capacitance barely changes the battery ripple here; lower ESR reduces the first-harmonic fraction to 23.25%.

If that is still too much, evaluate the added source inductor. It lowers the fraction to 0.457% in this model, but adds DC loss and resonance. Keep that option conditional on damping, saturation and converter-stability checks.
