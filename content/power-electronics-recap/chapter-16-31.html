16.31 The current peak is controlled: why do alternate cycles diverge?

Optional advanced riddle: a fixed-frequency buck operates in continuous conduction with ideal peak-current control. A small valley-current error reverses sign every period and grows, although the output voltage is held constant in the model. How can a controller that checks every peak fail to stabilize the current waveform?

Let m1 > 0 be the current's rising slope during on-time and m2 > 0 the magnitude of its falling slope during off-time. Add a positive compensation ramp ma to the sensed current before comparing it with a fixed threshold. Express all slopes in equivalent inductor-current units.

For the local calculation, assume fixed Vin, fixed Vout, a fixed period T, no comparator delay, and a switching crossing strictly inside the period.

If the initial valley current rises by δin, the comparator crossing moves by δton = −δin/(m1 + ma). The next valley changes by the original error plus the effect of this changed on/off duration:

This is a sampled pole λ. Its stability criterion is |λ| < 1, rather than a negative real part in the continuous s plane. A negative λ alternates the error sign. Without compensation, λ = −m2/m1. In the ideal CCM buck, volt-second balance gives m2/m1 = D/(1 − D), so D > 0.5 makes |λ| > 1.

At exactly 0.5 the ideal multiplier is −1: the perturbation does not decay.

Authored example: Vin = 12 V, L = 10 µH and fs = 100 kHz. Maintain a nominal average current of 10 A and adjust the comparator threshold for each case to retain the same operating point when a ramp is added.

Case m1 m2 Compensation ma λ
D = 0.25, Vout = 3 V 0.9 A/µs 0.3 A/µs 0 −1/3
D = 0.75, Vout = 9 V 0.3 A/µs 0.9 A/µs 0 −3
D = 0.75, same Vout 0.3 A/µs 0.9 A/µs 0.6 A/µs −1/3

At D = 0.75 without compensation, a +1 mA initial error becomes −3, +9, −27, +81, −243 and +729 mA at successive valleys. With the stated ramp, its magnitude instead falls by three each period. Solving the exact ideal comparator crossing and both current ramps reproduces this recurrence over the demonstrated intervals.

For nonnegative ma, the local stability condition is

At D = 0.75 the boundary is 0.3 A/µs; equality is marginal, not a design margin. This is the small-perturbation mechanism behind subharmonic, period-doubling behaviour. TI's subharmonic-oscillation discussion gives the practical context. Do not generalize the 50% statement to all current-control methods or converters.

The growing local map cannot be extrapolated indefinitely: duty clipping, DCM, output dynamics and other nonlinearities change it. The plot stops before the ideal crossing leaves the period and does not claim a simulated final period-two limit cycle. Change one thing: increase ma until λ = 0; in this ideal local map the valley error is removed in one period.

Then investigate a larger ramp: stronger compensation is not synonymous with faster convergence for every ramp value. This connects §16.28's continuous poles to the extra dynamics introduced by switching and sampling.

Ramp choice. Keep the 0.6 A/µs ramp as the stable reference at 75% duty. It changes the multiplier from −3 to −1/3 while retaining the nominal operating point. No compensation is acceptable in the demonstrated 25% case, but fails at 75%.

A ramp giving zero multiplier corrects the ideal local error in one period. Use it as a comparison, not a hardware optimum. More ramp can slow convergence; check the required duty range and implementation limits before choosing it.
