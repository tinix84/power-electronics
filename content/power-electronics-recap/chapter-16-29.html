16.29 The load sees zero volts: why does the bridge still inject a current pulse?

Riddle: a full bridge has two output nodes A and B, each switching between the negative DC rail and Vdc. Both nodes low and both nodes high give zero differential load voltage. Are they the same electrical state?

Reference both node voltages to the negative rail and use §11.7's definitions:

Bridge state (A, B) Differential voltage Common-mode voltage
(0, 0) 0 0
(Vdc, 0) +Vdc Vdc/2
(0, Vdc) −Vdc Vdc/2
(Vdc, Vdc) 0 Vdc

The two zero states are interchangeable only for a model that retains differential voltage alone. TI's single-phase inverter guide gives the active and zero bridge states used in bipolar/unipolar modulation. Their CM consequences follow by applying the definitions above to the node voltages and the actual return network.

Authored example: hold the negative rail and chassis at the same reference and put 1 nF from A to chassis and 1 nF from B to chassis. Impose simultaneous linear 0 → 48 V transitions on A and B in 100 ns. Then hold both high and later return both to zero with the same fall time.

Ignore dead time, leg mismatch and device nonlinearity in this first model. Because the two voltages are identical at every instant, the differential voltage stays zero, including during the edges. Yet the total current into chassis is

during the rising edge. The two capacitor currents add; they do not cancel like an outgoing/return differential load current. The rising-edge charge is 96 nC. The falling edge returns the same signed charge with opposite polarity: zero net charge per complete pulse does not mean zero peak or RMS current.

The high-state energy in the two capacitors is 2 × C × Vdc²/2 = 2.304 µJ. A differential-only load calculation misses this storage and its charging current. This is not automatically the energy dissipated in a particular transistor; that requires the charging/discharging paths.

Change one thing: use only one zero state in a modulation interval, or change the order of zero and active states while preserving the required differential average. Predict the new CM steps before calculating displacement current. An actual modulation scheme must also satisfy load-current, switching-loss and dead-time requirements.

If chassis floats, its potential and the complete return capacitances must be solved instead of imposing the same 0.96 A result. This exercise predicts current in the stated fixture, not radiated EMI or a universal ranking of PWM schemes.

Zero-state choice. Keep both common-mode and differential voltages in the model. The two zero states give the same load voltage but different chassis excitation. A differential-only model is sufficient only when that coupling is outside the question.

For modulation, compare sequences with the same required differential output. Fewer common-mode transitions may help this fixture, but the sequence must also carry load current and meet switching-loss limits. No universal PWM winner follows from this current calculation.
