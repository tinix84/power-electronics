16.1 Did the circuit ring, or did the probe? — Williams-inspired

Predict. A 1 V pulse source has a 2 ns rising edge. A scope shows overshoot. Is this evidence that the switching circuit is unstable? First add the measurement circuit to the schematic.

Use a 50 Ω source, 10 pF probe input capacitance, and either 100 nH or 5 nH effective probe-return inductance. The displayed voltage is across the capacitance. The simplified acquisition transfer function is:

For 100 nH, f₀ = 159.2 MHz and ζ = 0.25. For 5 nH, they become 711.8 MHz and 1.118. The first model is underdamped; the second is overdamped. The finite 2 ns ramp produces a calculated 1.375 V peak in the first case, approximately 37.5% overshoot, while the short-return model approaches 1 V without overshoot.

The ideal-step overshoot formula would give a different value because the applied edge here has finite rise time.

The familiar bandwidth scale 0.35/tr ≈ 175 MHz is a useful single-pole/Gaussian-style estimate, not a hard spectral cutoff. It explains why a fast edge can excite the 159 MHz acquisition resonance even if pulses repeat at only 100 kHz. Shortening the ground connection changes the measurement network, not necessarily the device under test.

Change one thing: compare a long lead with a short ground spring while keeping the source and probe point unchanged. Use a low-voltage, ground-referenced demonstration; floating switching-node measurements require an appropriate differential measurement arrangement. The plot omits scope bandwidth, probe compensation and distributed cable effects and is not a complete commercial-probe model.

Why the basic matters: an observed waveform belongs to the circuit and its measurement connection. Williams, AN47, discusses probe grounding and loading; its probe appendix includes material contributed by Tektronix.

First decision. Change the probe return before changing the circuit. This tests measurement-induced ringing with little disturbance to the board. If the ring persists with a suitable measurement setup, investigate the circuit resonance. A cleaner trace alone does not prove the circuit is stable.
