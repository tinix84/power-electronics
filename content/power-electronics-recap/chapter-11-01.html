Circuit foundations

11.1 R, L and C on logarithmic axes

In sinusoidal steady state, phasors turn differentiation into multiplication by jω. Use and distinguish peak phasors from RMS phasors consistently.

Complex multiplication multiplies magnitudes and adds phases; complex division divides magnitudes and subtracts phases. This is the arithmetic behind impedance dividers and transfer functions.

Element Magnitude versus frequency Phase
R Constant 0°
L Proportional to f: ×10 each decade +90°
C Inversely proportional to f: ÷10 each decade −90°

For impedance magnitude, a logarithmic Ω axis avoids ambiguity. A dB impedance plot needs a reference, such as 20 log₁₀(|Z|/1 Ω). A transfer-function gain plot instead uses a dimensionless ratio. Adding impedance magnitudes generally gives the wrong answer: sum complex impedances in series, or complex admittances in parallel.

Reactance-chart exercise from the lesson. At 10 MHz, estimate the magnitudes for an ideal 1 nH inductor and an ideal 100 nF capacitor, first from a reactance chart and then by calculation:

These answers are impedances in milliohms, not currents in milliamperes. Increase frequency by one decade: the ideal inductive reactance becomes ten times larger and the ideal capacitive reactance ten times smaller. Use the chart to build this intuition, then include ESR and ESL for a real capacitor. The divider and current-sharing equations of section 4.1 explain how these impedances affect decoupling; neither reactance alone gives an attenuation value.

The real-capacitor example uses 100 nF, 20 mΩ ESR and 1 nH ESL. Its self-resonance is approximately 15.92 MHz; below it the impedance is mainly capacitive, near it ESR sets the minimum, and above it ESL dominates. These are illustrative fixed component values, not a particular capacitor's measured curve.
