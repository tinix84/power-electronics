16.32 Only 50 ns of imbalance: why does the transformer flux keep walking?

Riddle: a bridge applies almost equal positive and negative voltages to a transformer. Each cycle looks nearly symmetric. Can the small difference matter after fifty cycles?

Faraday's law applies to the induced core voltage, not blindly to the bridge-terminal voltage when winding resistance and leakage drops are significant:

Start with an ideal imposed core voltage of ±48 V at 100 kHz, N = 8 turns and Ae = 100 mm². Positive duration is 5.025 µs, negative duration 4.975 µs: their difference is only 50 ns. The net increment per period is

The ripple repeats around an increasingly shifted state. With initial B = −0.15 T, the first positive peak is +0.1515 T. The nth positive peak is 0.1515 + (n − 1) × 0.003 T. An assumed 0.30 T screening threshold is crossed during the 51st positive interval, about 0.5 ms after startup.

The threshold is an authored teaching value, not a material saturation rating; the calculation predicts its crossing and does not model the nonlinear current after it.

This separates three ideas: flux excursion within a cycle, initial/remanent flux, and accumulated cycle-to-cycle bias. In a linear magnetic model Lm ∝ N², but the actual B excursion still follows volt-seconds divided by NAe. A nearly symmetric oscilloscope trace can conceal a small integral error. TI's full-bridge design report, §3.2.1 discusses flux walking and volt-second asymmetry in bridge/push-pull structures.

Now distinguish terminal from core voltage. Add an authored winding resistance of 0.2 Ω and a linear 50 µH magnetizing inductance, driven by the same bridge-terminal waveform. For this no-load reduced model,

The terminal waveform has a 0.24 V average. In the eventual periodic linear state, its average is balanced by Rp times the 1.2 A average magnetizing current, leaving zero average induced core voltage. The computed positive peak B tends to 0.2250 T, below the assumed 0.30 T threshold in this particular model.

The bias has not vanished; it has become bounded. A different resistance, excitation or nonlinear core could give a different outcome.

Therefore a bridge-terminal volt-second imbalance does not prove inevitable unlimited flux growth in every physical transformer. Check whether balancing drops or control establish an acceptable periodic state before the material's nonlinear region. A series blocking capacitor can change the bias dynamics, but it creates another state and must be analysed with the rest of the circuit.

Change one thing: reverse the timing imbalance, change the initial B, or halve N while preserving the imposed core-voltage waveform. Predict direction, margin and rate separately. This extends the earlier low-frequency transformer note: winding resistance affects both measurement and operating dynamics.

Flux-margin decision. First correct the cycle-to-cycle induced-voltage imbalance. More turns reduce flux change per volt-second, but do not cancel a nonzero volt-second sum in the imposed-voltage model.

A blocking capacitor is an alternative circuit change; it adds a state whose dynamics must be checked. Keep the winding and bias solution only after an acceptable periodic state is demonstrated. A symmetric-looking trace alone does not establish flux margin.
