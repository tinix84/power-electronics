2.5 Coupled inductors as a basic energy-storage element

What changes from two independent inductors: some magnetic flux produced by either winding links the other. A changing current in one winding therefore contributes to the voltage of the other. Coupling can occur through a shared magnetic core or through the surrounding field. A familiar two-winding symbol does not by itself tell us whether an ideal transformer or a finite-inductance energy-storage model is intended.

Reversing one current reference reverses the corresponding mutual signs; do not change a sign merely because the two coils are drawn on opposite sides of a core. A rising current entering winding 1's dot induces a positive voltage at winding 2's dot under these references.

The coupling coefficient is:

k = 0 gives independent inductors. k near one gives tight coupling; k below one means imperfect flux linkage. For a passive linear pair, M² ≤ L₁L₂ ensures nonnegative stored magnetic energy. The k = 1 idealization makes the inductance matrix singular, so a model that needs its inverse must treat the associated constraint correctly.

Stored energy and the distinction from an ideal transformer

The mutual term can add or subtract, depending on the signed currents. For an allowed passive inductance matrix, the total energy remains nonnegative. Unlike the ideal transformer element, the coupled pair stores finite energy. Even perfect coupling does not remove its magnetizing energy state. Real magnetic saturation, hysteresis and bias dependence also mean that a constant-L energy formula is not valid over every operating condition.

With imperfect coupling, the terminal voltage ratio is not generally fixed by a turns ratio. For example, with winding 2 open, i₂ = 0 and v₂/v₁ = M/L₁ for a changing primary current in this ideal lossless pair. Using √(L₁/L₂) as a turns ratio is only appropriate when leakage is negligible and the inductances correspond to the same common flux path.

At 2 A series current the aiding pair stores 410 µJ and the opposing pair 90 µJ. These values also follow by substituting the signed winding currents into the full coupled-energy expression.

This is the engineering consequence of the dots: the same two windings can present very different inductance when reconnected. Series opposing does not generally mean zero inductance. For equal windings L₁ = L₂ = L, it gives 2L(1 − k); zero occurs only in the ideal k = 1 limit.

Where it is used

  • Flyback: magnetizing energy is stored in one interval and delivered through the other winding in another interval. Use an energy-storage magnetic model with flux reset.
  • SEPIC and Ćuk: coupling can change winding ripple and component requirements; derive the result from the winding voltages and dot references of the actual topology.
  • Coupled multiphase buck: winding orientation determines whether relevant phase-current components reinforce or cancel magnetic flux. Tight coupling alone does not guarantee lower ripple in every mode.
  • Common-mode choke: arrange the intended outgoing and return currents to oppose their core flux, while common-mode currents reinforce. For equal windings, the per-winding modal factors are L − M and L + M, respectively, under the chosen current references. A two-wire loop measurement can introduce an additional factor of two; state which current and voltage define the measured inductance.

Thermal current rating, combined winding ampere-turns, saturation, leakage energy and insulation all need consideration in a real component. Analog Devices' coupled-inductor modelling article provides a SEPIC example; Coilcraft's coupled-inductor guide discusses the physical component and coupling tradeoffs.
