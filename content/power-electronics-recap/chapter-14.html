14. Are linear regulators dead? No: choose the architecture

A load needs 3.3 V, but the available rail is 5 V or 12 V. A battery voltage falls as it discharges. A rectified transformer output has ripple. In each case, the load needs a supply that stays within its limits.

A linear regulator may be the simplest solution. First understand how it controls voltage. Then calculate what it dissipates. That calculation tells us when to keep the linear stage and when to consider a switching converter.

14.1 Principle and specifications

A linear regulator uses a semiconductor as a controllable series resistance between source and load. The output voltage is measured, compared with a reference, and the pass device is driven so that the output stays constant: if the output is too low, the series resistance is reduced, and conversely.

The pass transistor operates in its linear region, not as a saturated switch; the German term Längsregler (series regulator) describes the arrangement.

Read the regulator data sheet in groups:

  • Operating range: output tolerance, maximum input voltage, maximum current and dropout.
  • Regulation: line and load regulation, PSRR versus frequency, noise and load-transient response.
  • Supporting circuit: external divider, quiescent current, required capacitors and reverse-current behaviour.
  • Temperature: dissipation, thermal limits and the actual mounting conditions.

Linear regulator describes the regulation method; the overall regulator is not necessarily a globally linear system. Avoid using one transistor-region label for all BJT and MOSFET pass arrangements.

When is the simple follower enough? Keep it when its voltage variation and reference loading meet the requirement. It uses few parts but offers limited accuracy. Choose a feedback regulator when tighter regulation justifies the added circuit and its stability checks.

14.3 Integrated regulators: fixed, adjustable and low dropout

An integrated regulator contains a reference, error amplifier and pass device. The amplifier adjusts the pass device to correct output error. Typical protection includes thermal shutdown and current limiting. Check how the actual device protects the pass transistor's safe operating area (section 13).

Fixed three-terminal regulators (78xx, 79xx). The suffix gives the output voltage: a 7812 delivers 12 V, an LM78L05 100 mA at 5 V. The basic parts supply 1 A; versions from 100 mA to 5 A exist. In the classic NPN-Darlington structure the minimum input-to-output difference is about 0.7 V across the current-sense resistor plus 1.5 V for two base-emitter junctions plus 0.3 V for the drive current source, about 2.5 V.

That dropout is why a 5 V rail cannot feed a 3.3 V load through a classic 78xx: only 1.7 V of headroom is available.

The lecture data for this family: input-to-output difference up to 40 V and output current up to 1.5 A. Four-terminal adjustable parts with a separate reference pin (for example a 5 V reference with V) preceded this simpler arrangement.

Low-dropout regulators (LDO). A low-voltage rail may not have 2 to 3 V of spare headroom. A PNP or P-channel MOSFET pass stage can operate with a smaller drop, sometimes below 0.5 V. Check dropout at the required current.

A PNP needs base current, which flows to ground. This raises ground-current loss. A Darlington adds another and increases dropout. A small base-path resistor can limit current and reduce oscillation tendency.

A P-channel MOSFET needs less steady drive power. Controllers with an external MOSFET can use a sense resistor to set current limit. Output voltage may be internally fixed or set by an external divider. Read the specific device's circuit before choosing values.

Negative outputs. With a floating input, either output terminal of a positive regulator may be grounded, so no special part is needed. For a symmetric supply from one centre-tapped rectifier, a negative regulator (79xx, LM337) is required; internally it mirrors the positive circuit with the transistor polarities exchanged.

14.4 References: Zener and bandgap

Every regulator, linear or switching, ends its feedback path at a reference.

Zener with series resistor. The simplest reference is a Zener fed through a resistor from the input; its Thévenin model is in series with and its input rejection is the ratio of section 14.2. Driving the Zener from a constant current (an op-amp and the Zener's own output) removes the input dependence and reaches about 80 dB of line rejection.

The Zener voltage has a temperature coefficient that is negative below about 6 V, positive above and close to zero near 6 V, which is why 6 V Zeners are preferred for stabilisation.

Bandgap reference and shunt regulator. A base-emitter voltage falls with temperature. The difference between two base-emitter voltages supplies a term proportional to absolute temperature. Adding the two in suitable proportions gives a compensated reference near 1.2 V.

The TL431 combines a reference and amplifier in a three-terminal shunt regulator. Its internal reference is 2.5 V. A divider sets the cathode voltage through the reference pin. Setting avoids divider tolerance.

A series resistor must provide the minimum cathode current stated in the data sheet. Include the reference-pin current, typically a few microamperes, in the divider calculation. The TL431 returns later as a feedback element in isolated flyback converters.

14.5 Three architecture choices, and where linear is better

Teaching scenario Calculation neglecting quiescent current Design interpretation
12 V → 3.3 V at 1 A 8.7 W loss; 27.5% efficiency A switcher is strongly motivated by dissipation
5 V → 3.3 V At most 66% efficiency; needs an LDO because 1.7 V headroom is below a classic 2.5 V dropout Even a stable input does not rescue a linear stage with a large voltage ratio
3.6 V → 3.3 V at 20 mA 6 mW loss; 91.7% efficiency A linear stage can be attractive if minimum headroom and accuracy are sufficient
12 V → 3.6 V switcher, then 3.3 V LDO 90% × 91.7% = 82.5% overall Efficient bulk conversion and local low-noise regulation can coexist

For the third case, assume for illustration an LDO with 10 µA quiescent current. At 20 mA its efficiency is approximately 91.6%; at 10 µA output current it falls to approximately 45.8%. Quiescent current can dominate a sleeping load. This is a numerical illustration, not a specification of the device below.

Consider a 3.6 V battery supplying a 3.3 V, 20 mA sensor or ADC front end. An LDO loses 6 mW in its pass device. A buck reaching 85% at this load loses about 11.6 mW. The 85% value is a teaching assumption. The buck also introduces switching noise near the measurement circuit.

Here the LDO deserves consideration. With a larger voltage drop or more current, its heating may instead favour a switcher. Calculate both candidates rather than choosing from the product category.

The fourth case, post-regulation, uses both: the switcher does the bulk voltage conversion efficiently and the LDO removes its ripple. With 40 dB of PSRR at the switching frequency, a 50 mV ripple component becomes 0.5 mV. PSRR falls with frequency, so read it at the actual switching frequency, not at 120 Hz.

As a concrete device reference, the LT3045 targets low-noise instrumentation, data converters and switcher post-regulation. Its published figures include 0.8 µV RMS output noise over 10 Hz–100 kHz and high PSRR under specified conditions. These figures are device- and test-condition-specific; an LDO neither removes all input ripple nor guarantees negligible output noise.

Start-up time: how fast must the rail be valid? Efficiency is not the only rail specification. A handheld instrument may power its measurement circuit only when a button is pressed and expect a result within a few hundred microseconds. Then the time from enable to a valid output decides the architecture.

A switching regulator must start its oscillator and reference, then ramp the output under soft-start so the inrush current does not trip its current limit. Soft-start times are commonly fixed or capacitor-set in the millisecond range; read the actual controller data sheet. A linear regulator has no switching loop to settle. Its start-up is mainly the time its current limit needs to charge the output capacitor.

That charge is the hard limit for either architecture. Bringing 100 µF to 3.3 V needs : 0.33 A over 1 ms, 1.1 A over 300 µs and 6.6 A over 50 µs. A fast start therefore needs a source and regulator that can deliver this current, and a small output capacitance.

Which rail meets a 300 µs requirement? Keep the switcher running in a low-power mode, if its standby current fits the battery budget. Use a linear regulator with a small output capacitor if the voltage drop is small. Accept the switcher's soft-start only if the requirement can be relaxed. Each choice trades standby current, heat or response time; calculate the one the specification constrains.

Architecture decision. Compare linear and switching candidates at the actual voltage ratio and load. Linear regulation avoids switching ripple but dissipates the voltage drop. A switcher may reduce that heat while adding filtering and control requirements. Let the rail specifications determine the choice.

For example, 40 dB rejection turns a 10 mV ripple component into 0.1 mV through that input-to-output path. Intrinsic regulator noise and other coupling paths remain. PSRR depends on frequency, headroom, load and external components.

A linear regulator is a feedback loop (section 12.4). Long input leads add inductance and can contribute to oscillation. A nearby bypass capacitor may help, but take its value and placement from the device data sheet.

The output capacitor also affects the loop. Some LDOs require an ESR range; others support ceramic capacitors. A generic instruction to add 100 nF on each side cannot establish stability. TI's regulator-stability guidance explains the role of output-capacitor ESR.

A load some distance from the regulator sees the copper and contact resistance of the connection in series with the regulator's low output resistance, which can undo the regulation entirely. Remote sensing brings the voltage measurement to the load with separate sense connections S+ and S−; these carry almost no current, so the drop along them is negligible and the drop along the force leads is regulated out.

With adjustable regulators this can be arranged by connecting the external divider at the load. It is the same four-wire idea as the Kelvin current-sense riddle in section 16.2.

14.7 Worked small-power design: 12 V → 5 V at 0.5 A

Design the adjustable regulator of section 14.3 for 5 V at 0.5 A from a 12 V input, with 5 mA ground current, and check the thermal path.

  • Divider: Ω, Ω, output 5.036 V including the adjust current. Headroom 7 V, well above the 2.5 V class dropout, so a standard regulator is sufficient here.
  • Loss and efficiency: pass loss 7 V × 0.5 A = 3.5 W, quiescent loss 12 V × 5 mA = 0.06 W, total 3.56 W; efficiency 2.5 W / 6.06 W = 41%.
  • Without a heat sink: at an illustrative K/W for a TO-220 in free air, the junction rises 178 K above a 50 °C ambient, to about 228 °C. Not possible: the thermal shutdown would act first.
  • Heat-sink requirement: for °C at 50 °C ambient the total thermal resistance may not exceed 75 K / 3.56 W = 21.1 K/W. With an illustrative K/W and 1 K/W for the mounting interface, the heat sink must be below about 16 K/W. Take all three thermal values from the actual data sheet and heat-sink catalogue; the numbers here show the method.

A 90% efficient buck delivering the same 2.5 W loses about 0.28 W. This greatly reduces the thermal burden. It leads to the next lecture's buck design.

Keep the linear regulator among your options when the voltage drop is small, the current is low or noise matters. Check its thermal path before deciding. scripts/linear_regulator_example.py records this chapter's calculations in linear-regulator-checks.json.

Keep linear or change architecture? The linear design can work only with the calculated thermal path; its 3.56 W loss cannot be ignored. Keep it when simplicity or noise performance justifies that heat.

If efficiency is the priority, take the assumed 90% buck forward for design: its estimated loss is about 0.28 W. That is a reason to investigate it, not a completed noise, transient or component check.
