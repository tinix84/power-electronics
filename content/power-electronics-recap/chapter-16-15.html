16.15 Both capacitors meet their voltage ratings: why does the connector spark?

Predict. Two 1 mF capacitors share a common return. One is charged to 48 V, the other to 0 V. Connect their positive terminals through a resistive path, with no external source attached. Assume constant capacitance and enough resistance for eventual settling. What is the final voltage, and where does the energy go?

Charge conservation fixes the final common voltage:

Initially the capacitors store 1.152 J in total. At 24 V they store 0.576 J. The other 0.576 J is dissipated during equalisation. More generally, defining the series-equivalent capacitance for the voltage difference:

This is not missing energy: charge conservation determines the endpoint, while resistance dissipates energy on the way. With loop inductance, energy can oscillate between L and C before losses settle it. A perfectly lossless LC connection does not settle to the common final voltage assumed here.

Add a precharge resistor. In the resistance-dominated model, choose 10 Ω between the positive terminals. Since Ceq = 0.5 mF:

Initial current is 4.8 A, initial resistor power is 230.4 W, and the resistor absorbs 0.576 J over the complete equalisation. After five time constants, 25 ms, the remaining terminal-voltage difference is approximately 0.323 V. Each capacitor approaches 24 V; the initially empty capacitor does not charge to 48 V in this isolated two-capacitor circuit.

Increasing resistance reduces peak current and peak power and lengthens the transient. It does not change the total equalisation energy for these fixed initial/final states. Check resistor pulse-energy and repetitive thermal capability; a low cycle-average wattage alone does not qualify the resistor. Real connection inductance also prevents an instantaneous current jump, so the exponential model applies where that inductance is negligible on the RC time scale.

Change one thing: precharge through a resistor and bypass it only after the terminal-voltage difference is sufficiently small for the actual bypass path. A fixed five-time-constant delay is an illustration, not a universal closing criterion; residual current depends on the remaining voltage difference and the bypass impedance.

Distinguish a stiff source. Charging one empty 1 mF capacitor from an ideal fixed 48 V source through resistance is a different problem. The capacitor ends at 48 V, stores 1.152 J, and the resistance dissipates another 1.152 J. The source supplies 2.304 J. Ask students to explain why the final voltage and dissipated energy differ from the isolated two-capacitor case before calculating a precharge resistor.

Why the basic matters: voltage ratings do not limit equalisation current. The initial voltage difference, complete connection impedance and stored energy determine the connection event. These are calculation exercises; predict the waveforms with a circuit model or a suitably scaled, current-limited demonstration rather than deliberately arcing charged capacitors.

Precharge decision. Keep 10 Ω as the reference precharge path. It limits the initial current to 4.8 A and spreads the 0.576 J event over time. More resistance lowers the peak further, but increases the waiting time.

Bypass only when the measured residual voltage permits the actual connection current. A fixed 25 ms delay leaves about 0.323 V here; it is not proof that every bypass path can close acceptably. Direct connection gives faster equalisation at the cost of uncontrolled peak current.
