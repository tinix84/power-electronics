16.27 The secondary is shorted: why can a common-mode injector still disturb the primary?

Riddle: join the two secondary terminals and drive that joined node with a voltage injector relative to chassis. The secondary's terminal-to-terminal voltage is zero. Should every voltage on the primary also be zero?

Specify the measurement before answering. Shorting the secondary removes its differential voltage, but does not clamp its common-mode voltage to chassis. Interwinding capacitance provides an AC displacement-current path across the isolation barrier. It is not a galvanic short circuit or evidence of failed insulation. A finite capacitance becomes a low impedance only relative to the other impedances and at sufficiently high frequency.

TI's transformer common-mode measurement discussion describes a function-generator/oscilloscope test of this coupling; TI's isolated-bias transformer discussion explains the interwinding-capacitance current path.

Define a reproducible fixture. Tie secondary terminals s1 and s2 together at node s and impose Vin between s and chassis. Terminate primary terminals p1 and p2 with one resistor R each to chassis. These resistors are external fixture impedances, not resistances through the isolation barrier.

Put C1 from s to p1 and C2 from s to p2. The primary winding, with the secondary shorted, presents differential impedance Zw between p1 and p2:

Both primary resistors return to the same chassis/injector reference. This closes the displacement-current loop. Grounding, floating or probing a different node changes the circuit and the answer. The injector is an imposed ideal source in this exercise; its actual source impedance would be another part of the fixture.

With negligible secondary resistance this reduces to primary resistance plus the short-circuit leakage inductance. For the worked example use the reduced approximation Zw = rw + sLsc. An ideal perfectly coupled, lossless transformer with its secondary shorted instead has Zw = 0 and clamps the primary differential voltage to zero. It still does not constrain the primary's absolute common-mode potential.

Both primary terminals move together, so no current flows through Zw. At high frequency the primary common-mode voltage can approach the injected voltage even though magnetic transfer of the imposed secondary differential voltage is zero. With unequal coupling capacitances, finite Zw allows different primary-node voltages: common-mode excitation is converted into differential disturbance. Unequal terminations can also break symmetry, but are held equal here to isolate the capacitance effect.

Worked frequency and time-domain comparison. Use R = 1 kΩ per primary terminal and Zw = 2 Ω + s × 10 µH. Compare 100/100 pF with 150/50 pF, keeping total interwinding capacitance at 200 pF. These are authored lumped-model values. In the time-domain test, drive a 100 V pulse with a 100 ns linear rise, a 1 µs flat top and a 100 ns fall; start from zero stored energy.

For example, a 10 V peak sinusoidal injector at 1 MHz gives approximately 5.34 V peak primary CM and 0.170 V peak primary differential voltage in the unbalanced case. The 6.12 V pulse result includes the broadband edges and the network dynamics; it is not obtained by multiplying the pulse height by the 1 MHz gain.

Change one thing: balance C1 and C2 without changing their sum. The differential response disappears in this fixture while common-mode feedthrough remains. Next reduce both capacitances, or reduce Zw toward an ideal short: predict separately the two voltages and the injected current. Finally, change R or add probe capacitance to chassis.

This illustrates why a transformer is a four-terminal object whose common-mode coupling is not captured by a differential-only two-port model.

The reduced circuit excludes distributed winding capacitance, core loss, self-resonance, nonlinear effects and real injector/probe models; the plotted frequency sweep is the mathematical model's response, not a qualified transformer bandwidth. Electrostatic shielding changes the capacitance and return network and must be modelled with its actual connection. This exercise extends §§2.6 and 16.5; it does not predict insulation compliance or hardware stress ratings.

What should change? For differential pickup in this fixture, first balance the coupling capacitances. This removes the differential response without reducing their sum. It does not remove common-mode motion.

If common-mode current is the limiting disturbance, reducing total coupling addresses the relevant quantity. A shield is another possible route, but its connection changes the return network. Choose from the measured mode before changing the transformer geometry.
