16.5 How can DC isolation transmit switching current? — Ott-inspired

Predict. An isolated transformer or insulated heatsink has no intentional conductive connection across its barrier. Why does a cable current probe still see switching pulses?

Represent winding-to-winding or switch-to-heatsink coupling by 100 pF. Apply a 48 V waveform at 100 kHz with 10 ns linear rising and falling edges. The capacitance experiences 4.8 V/ns, so each edge produces a 0.48 A peak displacement-current pulse:

There are two opposite-polarity pulses per 10 µs cycle. Their signed average is zero. Their rectified mean is 2Qf = 0.96 mA, and their ideal rectangular-pulse RMS is:

Change one thing: reduce coupling capacitance, slow the edge, or deliberately manage the current's return route. Reducing plate spacing can increase capacitance; an insulation barrier's DC rating does not specify zero high-frequency coupling. A cable carries common-mode current only as part of the complete return network.

The plotted current assumes an ideal return that permits the prescribed voltage across the capacitance; a floating chassis can move in voltage, changing the result.

Why the basic matters: distinguish galvanic conduction, displacement current, peak, signed average and RMS. This is not a prediction of touch-current compliance or radiated EMI. Ott's common-mode current measurement tip explains measuring net cable current by enclosing the relevant conductors together so differential currents cancel.

Which change first? Reducing the 100 pF coupling lowers both edge charge and current. Slowing the edge lowers peak current but leaves charge per transition unchanged; it can also increase switching loss.

First investigate the coupling geometry while preserving the required insulation. If it cannot change, evaluate slower edges. In either case, trace the return path before predicting what a cable probe will measure.
