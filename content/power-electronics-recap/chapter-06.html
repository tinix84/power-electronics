It does not need to generate new harmonic orders to cause harmonic current; real control errors and switching add further distortion. Input-voltage disturbances contaminating the current reference are also discussed in TI's PFC guidance.

The PF calculation is correct; it measures normalized voltage–current alignment, not waveform purity or harmonic compliance. Conversely, a controller that extracts the fundamental voltage and commands a clean in-phase sinusoidal current can have THD-I = 0 yet PF below one on distorted voltage.

With no DC, its PF is 1/√(1 + THD-V²).

The design objective therefore matters: proportional conductance gives minimum total RMS current for a specified total active power at a fixed terminal-voltage waveform. A clean fundamental current instead avoids copying supply harmonics. High PF, low THD and minimum frequency-weighted cable loss are related objectives, but are not interchangeable. Specify the actual waveform and harmonic-current requirements when selecting or testing PFC control.

Cable heating: count each harmonic at its own AC resistance

For a linear conductor model at a fixed temperature, with each resistance representing the complete outgoing/return path:

There are two effects. Adding harmonic current while holding the fundamental current fixed increases total RMS and heating even if resistance is constant. Skin and proximity effects can additionally increase AC resistance at higher frequencies, so high-order currents can cost more watts per ampere squared.

The increase depends on conductor size, arrangement and frequency; it is not necessarily large for every cable at low harmonic orders. Sections 2.7–2.8 explain the mechanisms. See also Schneider Electric's harmonic-loss explanation.

Even identical RMS current and THD can produce different heating if their harmonic content occurs at different frequencies. PF alone does not contain the cable's frequency-dependent resistance. At fixed total power and distorted voltage, do not assume that copying harmonics always increases heating: with frequency-independent resistance, proportional current actually minimizes it. Frequency-dependent resistance can reverse that comparison.

Worked counterexample: PF = 1 with greater cable loss

Use a prescribed 50 Hz voltage at the load port with 230 V RMS fundamental and 23 V RMS fifth harmonic, hence THD-V = 10%. Compare two ideal current-reference strategies delivering the same 2.30 kW at that port. Assume a loop resistance of 0.20 Ω at 50 Hz and 0.60 Ω at 250 Hz.

These deliberately illustrative resistance values demonstrate the weighting; they are not a claim about a particular cable. Hold the terminal waveform fixed and exclude PFC internal losses and switching ripple.

Its fifth-harmonic current adds approximately 0.588 W of cable heating, enough here to outweigh the reduction in fundamental-current heating. If both harmonic resistances were instead 0.20 Ω, its cable loss would be 19.80 W, below the clean-current case's 20 W.

This makes the limitation precise: perfect PF does not guarantee low THD or minimum AC cable loss, but harmonics do not automatically make conductance control the worse choice under every comparison.

Thus DC can transfer more average power when AC and DC share the same peak limits. But the third comparison gives DC 20 A RMS versus only 14.14 A RMS for AC; DC conductor heating is then twice as high for the same resistance. It is not a comparison at equal thermal utilization.

Wires and fuses: continuous conductor heating is principally an RMS-current issue, with additional AC skin/proximity effects where relevant. Fuse operation depends on thermal history, time-current characteristics, fault conditions and pulse , rather than a universal peak-current threshold. AC and DC interruption ratings are also different; DC has no natural periodic current zero.

Do not infer a DC rating by multiplying an AC fuse rating by √2. Eaton's fuse technology guidance distinguishes current and voltage ratings; its high-speed fuse guide discusses AC/DC application limits.

Grid connection: AC made voltage transformation straightforward with transformers and remains widely used for generation and distribution. HVDC can be advantageous for long cables, long-distance transfer and asynchronous interconnections; cable charging current and reactive exchange are major differences. Converter cost and loss, insulation, protection, conductor count and topology also matter.

A two-wire peak-limit example does not establish that every AC system is oversized or compare it fairly with three-phase transmission. See ABB's discussion of offshore HVDC.

Compare under one constraint. State the held voltage, current, insulation or conductor limit before choosing AC or DC. A benefit under one constraint may disappear under another. Use the same delivered-power requirement and loss accounting for both candidates.
