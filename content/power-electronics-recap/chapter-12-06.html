12.6 Worked LC input filter: unstable without added damping, stable with an RC branch

This is an optional worked extension to the two-session recap. We calculate the source output impedance, connect it to the converter's incremental input impedance, and verify stability from the connected system's poles. The plots use a local continuous-time constant-power model; they do not represent a switching-converter startup simulation or measured hardware.

A. Specify the circuit and the operating point

The converter draws a constant input power of 60 W around a 12 V input operating point. It therefore draws 5 A and has incremental input resistance −2.4 Ω. Assume this approximation is valid around the filter interaction frequencies, roughly 1–2 kHz here. A physical design must use the actual converter input impedance and control dynamics outside that approximation.

Parameter Value Role
L 100 µH Input-filter series inductance
C 100 µF Input-filter shunt capacitance
r 0.05 Ω Total series winding/wiring resistance
P 60 W Converter input power at the chosen operating point
V 12 V Voltage at the converter input
Rn = V²/P 2.4 Ω Magnitude of the negative incremental input resistance
Optional damping resistance 1 Ω Series resistor in a shunt RC branch
Optional damping capacitance 400 µF Blocks steady DC current through that branch

The ideal source is 12.25 V, rather than 12 V, so that the 5 A DC drop across r leaves 12 V at the converter input. The 100 µF capacitor belongs to the source/filter side of the interface. Any real converter input capacitor must be included consistently in the chosen model; it is not silently added a second time here.

The curve is not an assertion that a real converter remains a constant-power load at every plotted frequency.

All have negative real parts. As a separate algebraic check, the cubic has positive coefficients and satisfies a₂a₁ > a₀, the additional Routh-Hurwitz condition for a normalized real cubic. The plotted transient starts with +1 mV on the main filter capacitor, with zero incremental inductor current and zero incremental voltage on the damping capacitor. The source perturbation is zero; the nominal DC source and load remain present.

Over the plotted 2 ms, the case without added damping grows to about 38 mV maximum absolute deviation; the damped case decays. These deviations remain small relative to 12 V. The growing linear response cannot predict what happens after a real converter reaches current limits, dropout or another nonlinear operating condition.

Power and implementation checks: the 0.05 Ω main-path resistance dissipates 1.25 W at 5 A in both cases. The ideal RC branch adds no steady DC load after its capacitor is charged, but its resistor dissipates AC ripple/transient energy. Its capacitor also stores energy, draws startup charging current, and needs appropriate voltage, ripple-current and ESR ratings.

The chosen values demonstrate the mechanism; they are not a general damping recipe or an EMI-compliance result.

The reproducible script scripts/middlebrook_example.py computes both impedance peaks, checks state-matrix poles against the independently derived characteristic polynomials, and plots the small-signal initial-condition responses. Its numerical results are recorded in middlebrook-checks.json. Use the actual measured/modelled converter impedance and parameter tolerances before applying the result to hardware.

Our damping choice. Keep the 1 Ω / 400 µF shunt branch in this model. The undamped connection is unstable; the added branch brings all connected-system poles into the left half-plane without extra steady DC series drop.

More main-path resistance is a simpler alternative, but costs DC power. The shunt costs a capacitor and AC dissipation. For hardware, check the actual converter impedance and component range before carrying over these values.
