16.28 Identical pulses, different LC trajectories: did the phase or the poles change?

Two linked riddles: why do identical voltage pulses produce different LC waveforms during startup, and how can feedback turn the same energy-storage elements from lightly damped to rapidly settling—or unstable? Use initial-state analysis for the first question and pole location for the second. Changing an initial condition does not move the poles of a fixed linear model.

Define an LC with two independent ports

An imposed voltage u drives a series R–L branch into a capacitor node v. A prescribed load current iℓ leaves that node; inductor current i enters it:

In the normalized plane (v, Z0i), the orbit is a circle centered at (U, Z0Iℓ). It is an ellipse if unscaled amperes and volts are used as axes. Nonzero sources shift the center; they do not invalidate the natural LC frequency. With two imposed bridge voltages across a resonant branch, the net branch drive can be their signed difference; actual converter conduction intervals and load laws must be derived for the chosen topology.

Both initial capacitor voltage and initial inductor current determine the resulting sine/cosine mixture. Reading this mixture as merely an input/output phase lag can hide the accumulated state. A sinusoid about a fixed center can of course be written with an amplitude and phase; that parametrization does not make the changing startup state a new steady-state transfer phase.

The shifted quadratic invariant is not a claim that the physical stored energy stays constant with active sources. For R = 0 the actual energy obeys dE/dt = ui − viℓ; the two imposed ports can exchange energy with the tank.

Three consecutive rectangular pulses: carry the state forward

Use L = 10 µH, C = 1 µF, R = 0, Iℓ = 0.5 A and initial v = i = 0. Apply three identical 12 V, 2 µs rectangular pulses, separated by 2 µs at zero volts. These are finite voltage pulses, not mathematical Dirac impulses. Here Z0 = 3.162 Ω and f0 = 50.33 kHz.

Pulse Rising-edge time Initial v Initial i v at end of 2 µs on interval i at end of on interval

Every pulse begins at the state left by the preceding on/off intervals. Voltage and current remain continuous at these switching instants because the model applies no Dirac current to C or Dirac voltage to L. The orbit center changes between (12 V, 1.581 V) and (0 V, 1.581 V), while the state stays at the same point at the boundary.

Starting each pulse calculation again from zero would describe a different physical experiment.

This is the geometric basis of a state-plane trajectory construction: join interval solutions using the actual boundary state and the correct source values. It is useful for resonant converters, but does not substitute for their diode, switch, clamp and load-dependent mode conditions. In this ideal lossless tank startup terms do not decay; do not assume eventual convergence to a unique periodic steady state. Adding damping changes that property.

Real actuators have voltage/current limits, delay, sampling and unmeasured states; moving poles arbitrarily far left is not a physically free operation. Reference tracking, load-disturbance rejection, zeros and robustness need additional analysis. See MIT's state-feedback and pole-placement lectures 11–12.

What does an imaginary-axis plot fail to settle by itself?

For a particularly clear counterexample, compare the rational expressions 1/(LCs² + 0.5Cs + 1) and 1/(LCs² − 0.5Cs + 1). Their algebraic magnitudes on jω are identical, although one has damped poles and the other growing poles. Their phases differ. The unstable causal system's growing impulse response has no ordinary Fourier transform, so its rational jω evaluation must not be presented as a stable measured steady-state frequency response.

This does not prove that Fourier contains no stability information. Full complex frequency data, causality and an appropriate model support stability analysis; an FFT of a growing finite record also shows features of that record. It does show why magnitude alone, or a formal substitution s = jω without convergence and natural-mode analysis, is insufficient. Laplace and state space make the exponential real part and the initial state explicit.

Change one thing: first change only the state at the second pulse and keep A fixed; its poles remain unchanged. Next change only ki; the damping and poles change. Finally compare Fourier spectra before and after startup exclusion in a damped version. Ask which differences arise from initial state, forcing, damping or the acquisition window.

Timing or damping? To explain the different startup traces, keep the poles fixed and carry each pulse's final state into the next interval. Changing feedback would answer a different question.

For a faster settling requirement, use the pole-placement model and include the sensing and control cost. Pulse timing can change the orbit, but cannot by itself move the poles of this fixed linear LC model.
