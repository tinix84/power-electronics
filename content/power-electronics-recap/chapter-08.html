8. Linearity, time invariance and the scope of a model

A linear input-output operator obeys additivity and scaling:

Time invariance means that delaying the input delays the corresponding output without otherwise changing its shape. It does not mean that the output is constant. Initial conditions must be handled consistently; fixed nonzero stored energy adds a natural response to a zero-state input-output model.

An LTI circuit can be described by an impulse response and, where the transforms exist, a transfer function. Fourier analysis also applies to signals produced by nonlinear systems. Nonlinearity prevents a general description by one input-independent linear transfer function; it does not forbid taking a Fourier transform of the resulting waveform.

A switching power converter is generally not globally LTI. A prescribed switching schedule may yield a linear time-varying circuit, while modulation, feedback and device characteristics introduce additional nonlinearities. This local averaged LTI model has an operating range and frequency range, usually sufficiently below the switching frequency. Large startup excursions, saturation, mode changes and switching-frequency detail may need another model.

8.1 Why a switching simulation can be expensive

A converter combines several time scales: the switching period, the much shorter transition time, and the longer startup or control response. A 100 kHz switching frequency gives a period of 10 µs. If an edge lasts 100 ns, a maximum time step of 10 ns gives only ten intervals across that edge.

This is a starting resolution choice, not an accuracy guarantee; faster ringing may require a smaller step. Repeat with a smaller maximum step and compare the quantities that matter.

For scale, a fixed 10 ns step over 100 ms requires 10 million steps; over 10 s it requires one billion steps. Adaptive solvers do not necessarily take that many steps, because they can take larger steps between events, but repeated switching transitions and nonlinear device models still create computational work.

DC is the zero-frequency component; it does not require an infinite simulation. The required duration follows from settling time or the transient being investigated.

An edge also introduces spectral content far above the switching repetition frequency. For a 100 ns edge, 1/tr = 10 MHz is a reciprocal-time scale, not a separate oscillation or a hard bandwidth cutoff. Section 16.1 explains the different 0.35/tr bandwidth estimate for specified response shapes. The simulator must resolve the fastest relevant dynamics, not simply sample the 100 kHz repetition frequency.

Choose the model for the question: use an averaged model for slow startup/control behaviour where its assumptions hold, a switching model for ripple and commutation, and a detailed parasitic model for edge ringing or coupling. Simulate only as long as needed for that question, and distinguish the solver's internal step from the saved waveform sampling used for an FFT.

Simulation choice. Use an averaged model when the question concerns slow dynamics. It saves computation but hides individual switching events. Use a switched model when those events matter. Keep the operating mode consistent and compare a shared prediction before increasing model detail.
