16.9 Optional advanced extension: the capacitor that remembers — Pease-inspired

Outside the two-session core. Predict. Charge a capacitor, briefly short its terminals and disconnect the short. Why can terminal voltage reappear even though an ideal capacitor would remain at zero?

Model a 1 µF main capacitor C0 in parallel with a 1 MΩ–10 nF series branch. The branch is a crude representation of slow polarization, not a universal dielectric model. Charge fully to 5 V, then short the external terminals for 1 ms. The main capacitor falls to zero, but the hidden capacitor decays with RDA CDA = 10 ms:

No energy is created: the hidden polarization branch retained charge and energy. A 10 MΩ meter also discharges the network, so its trace differs from the ideal unloaded recovery and eventually returns to zero. Dielectric absorption, steady leakage and ESR are distinct effects, even though more detailed equivalent circuits can contain several dissipative branches.

Change one thing: lengthen the short, change the sample history, or attach a finite-impedance measurement. Discuss errors in sample-and-hold circuits, reset integrators and precision voltage sampling. Real absorption spans multiple time constants; these values are not a specified recovery percentage for any material. See Pease's What's All This Soakage Stuff, Anyhow?, collected in Volume 2.

Accuracy or waiting time? Lengthening the short lets hidden charge decay without changing the capacitor, but delays the next measurement. A lower-absorption capacitor is another candidate when the cycle cannot wait. It still needs an error check with the actual charging history.

For this demonstration, change the short duration first. If the resulting recovery still exceeds the measurement budget, reconsider the capacitor. Changing the meter resistance also changes the observed recovery; it does not remove the stored-charge mechanism.
