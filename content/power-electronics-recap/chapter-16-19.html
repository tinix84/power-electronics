16.19 The high-side MOSFET switches correctly: why can it not stay on?

Predict. A conventional bootstrap-fed high-side driver works at moderate duty ratio but fails near continuous on-time. The gate draws little DC current, so why does its supply run down?

The bootstrap capacitor supplies gate charge plus driver bias/leakage while the switching node is high. In a simple charge-budget model:

Use authored values Cboot = 100 nF, Qg = 60 nC, on-state bias 100 µA, and an initial fully charged bootstrap voltage of 11.3 V relative to the moving source/switch node. A 10 µs on-interval removes 61 nC, giving 0.61 V droop. With an assumed 2 V droop allowance, one fully charged capacitor can support at most 1.4 ms in this model.

Gate charge is an event cost; bias current keeps consuming charge for the entire interval. A larger capacitor delays discharge but does not provide an indefinite supply.

Recharge is not instantaneous. Suppose the switch node is clamped low during every off interval, allowing recharge toward 11.3 V through 10 Ω. The recharge time constant is 1 µs. At 100 kHz and 95% duty, the 0.5 µs off-time recovers only 39.35% of the voltage deficit, rather than fully recharging the capacitor.

Let δ be the charge-loss voltage per on-event, and let a = exp(−toff/(Rcharge Cboot)). If d is the voltage deficit at the beginning of an on-event, then dnext = a(d + δ). The periodic end-of-on deficit is δ/(1 − a):

Duty at 100 kHz Off-time On-event droop δ Predicted periodic end-of-on bootstrap voltage
95% 0.50 µs 0.6095 V 9.751 V
99% 0.10 µs 0.6099 V 4.891 V, an invalid-operating extrapolation

With an assumed 9.3 V minimum usable bootstrap voltage, the 95% case passes this simple supply check, but the 99% case does not. The on-event charge loss barely changes; reduced recharge time causes the failure. An actual driver may enter undervoltage lockout or change operation before reaching the tabulated low-voltage extrapolation. These values and threshold are hypothetical, not specifications for a TI part.

Change one thing: compare longer recharge intervals, different charging impedance/capacitance, or a driver architecture with a charge pump or independent high-side supply. Conventional bootstrap operation needs an available recharge path; some devices explicitly support 100% duty by other means. Read the actual architecture rather than assuming every high-side driver has the same limitation. See TI, Understanding 100% mode in low-power DC/DC converters.

Why the basic matters: use voltage relative to the MOSFET source, not ground, and count both charge removed and charge restored. The example omits off-state bias, voltage-dependent gate charge, diode dynamics and switching-node ringing; those refine the budget rather than replace charge conservation.

Driver choice. Retain bootstrap operation at 95% duty for this model: 9.751 V remains above the assumed 9.3 V threshold. Reject the unchanged circuit at 99%. Its shorter recharge interval causes the failure.

Longer off-time is the simplest remedy if the output requirement permits it. If continuous ON is required, choose a driver supply architecture that supports it, accepting the added supply or charge-pump requirements.
