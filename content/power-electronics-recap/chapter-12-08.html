12.8 Real 0805 bead: manufacturer curves, DC bias and a 1 MHz comparison

Now replace the illustrative bead with a real Würth Elektronik WE-CBA 782853561, size 0805. Its manufacturer datasheet, revision 001.010 dated 2019-09-18, specifies 560 Ω ±25% at 100 MHz, 0.1 Ω maximum DCR at 20 °C, and 1.5 A rated current for a 40 K rise under its stated conditions.

Page 2 supplies typical zero-bias Z/R/reactance curves and magnitude curves at 0.5, 1 and 1.5 A. These are typical characteristics, not guaranteed bias-dependent limits.

Question: with the same 1 µF local capacitor, how does its 1 MHz supply-noise attenuation change as the DC load increases? Keep the source resistance at 0.05 Ω, the incremental load at 1 kΩ, capacitor ESR at 20 mΩ and total ESL at 0.5 nH.

Keeping the incremental load fixed isolates the bead-bias effect; the DC current is an independent operating-point parameter. This is the same small-signal voltage-divider experiment as section 12.7, not a 50 Ω insertion-loss test.

A. Read what the manufacturer actually provides

Approximate readings at 1 MHz are 20 Ω at zero bias and 1.3 Ω at 0.5 A. The zero-bias real part is roughly 3 Ω. The loaded curves provide magnitude only. Furthermore, the 1 A and 1.5 A traces fall below the chart's 1 Ω vertical limit at 1 MHz. Reading a precise endpoint there would invent data.

For those two currents, read the visible 2, 5 and 10 MHz portions instead and make an explicit local RL approximation:

Use Rb = 0.1 Ω as a modelling assumption for the loaded nominal estimates. A maximum DC resistance is not a measurement of AC resistance. The resulting inductance estimates are approximately 0.121 µH at 1 A and 0.092 µH at 1.5 A. Extrapolating this local model down to 1 MHz gives approximately 0.77 Ω and 0.59 Ω.

Those two values are inferred estimates, not manufacturer 1 MHz readings. No full-band RLC fit or loaded phase measurement is claimed.

Solid markers on the left show coarse chart readings; open markers and dotted segments below 1 Ω identify the extrapolation. Connecting the frequency samples is a visual guide. On the right, bars vary impedance magnitude by ±15% and allow any phase from 0° to +90° at 1 MHz.

This is an engineering sensitivity study under an inductive, passive-port assumption. It is neither a confidence interval nor a manufacturing-tolerance envelope, and does not bound model-extrapolation error or temperature effects.

For 10 mV RMS input ripple, the nominal output estimates are approximately 0.079, 1.36, 2.51 and 3.45 mV RMS, respectively. The 10 Ω RC reference remains −36.13 dB, or 0.156 mV RMS, in the fixed linear model. Its frequency response does not acquire magnetic bias dependence.

This particular bead therefore gives more attenuation than RC at zero bias, but much less at the sampled high-current operating points. The change is large even though its DC drop remains small. The curves demonstrate why the 100 MHz impedance rating alone is insufficient for a 1 MHz filter.

Do not turn this into an unfair current comparison: the 10 Ω resistor's 100 mV headroom budget permits only 10 mA. The dashed RC line beyond that current is a mathematical small-signal reference, not a viable rail design. At 0.5 A it would require a 5 V DC drop and dissipate 2.5 W.

The bead's 20 °C maximum-DCR calculation gives 50 mV and 25 mW there. At 1 A its corresponding values are 100 mV and 100 mW; at 1.5 A they are 150 mV and 225 mW. The last case also exceeds our chosen 100 mV component-drop budget.

Source/wiring drop and hot resistance require additional margin.

The real bead is not subject to section 12.7's hypothetical 125 mW package allowance: use its actual current rating, thermal conditions and derating. Its 1.5 A rating does not promise preserved impedance. Conversely, these coarse curves provide no resolved behaviour between zero and 0.5 A.

They cannot establish whether RC outperforms this bead at 1–10 mA; obtain finer low-bias manufacturer data or measure the intended operating point. No current interpolation is presented as manufacturer evidence.

C. Reproduce and extend the exercise

The manufacturer-authored PDF is retained as references/WE_782853561_datasheet.pdf. The saved copy came from a distributor mirror after direct download was denied. Its title, part number, revision and curve legends were checked against the accessible manufacturer PDF.

The approximate curve readings are in references/WE_782853561_curve_readings.csv. scripts/real_bead_example.py generates figure 33 and checks the voltage divider against nodal admittance. real-bead-checks.json records the parameters, sensitivity checks, methods and PDF SHA-256.

Student extension: repeat with measured complex bead impedance at the actual current and temperature, include the regulator and capacitor tolerances, and check both incoming-voltage-noise attenuation and load-current-induced rail droop. A low DC resistance and a good 100 MHz rating answer only part of that design problem.

Catalog number or loaded curve? Reject the zero-bias attenuation as evidence for the loaded rail. At 0.5 A, the nominal result changes from −42.0 dB to −17.3 dB.

Use the biased curve for screening, then measure loaded impedance if its uncertainty could decide the result. Choosing another bead is possible, but its own bias behaviour must be checked; a better nominal rating alone does not resolve this example.
