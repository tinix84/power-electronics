12.9 Pulsed digital-IC load: rail ripple, droop and recovery

The previous experiment injected voltage noise at the source. Now keep that source quiet and let the IC draw current pulses. This tests whether the local supply can deliver charge while maintaining its voltage. A filter that attenuates incoming voltage noise well can still have a large driving-point impedance and ring under load-current excitation.

A. Use the same capacitor and a fair low-current load

Compare a 10 Ω series resistor with a model derived from the real 0805 Würth 782853561. Both use an ideal 3.3 V source, 0.05 Ω source resistance and 1 µF local capacitance with 20 mΩ ESR and 0.5 nH total ESL. The IC is an imposed current load; the previous example's separate 1 kΩ incremental shunt is omitted. No regulator-control dynamics are included.

The pulse adds 8 mA to the idle current. At 9 mA, even the resistor's eventual DC drop would be only 90 mV, within the chosen 100 mV component-drop budget. This avoids comparing a usable bead with a resistor that has already exhausted its voltage headroom.

The pulse train is intentionally near the approximate bead/capacitor resonance, making the distinction between attenuation and transient response visible. Its 100 kHz repetition rate is separate from the 1 MHz characterization point used below.

B. Build a causal bead model before simulating

An impedance magnitude at one frequency cannot be substituted directly into a time-domain differential equation. Use a passive network:

Match the approximate zero-bias 1 MHz chart readings from section 12.8, Z ≈ 3 + j19.77 Ω, using RDC = 0.1 Ω. From the admittance of Z − RDC, obtain Lb ≈ 3.215 µH and Rloss ≈ 137.7 Ω. The resistor is in parallel with the inductance; it is not a 137.7 Ω DC drop. At DC this network reduces to RDC. Its real impedance increases with frequency.

Model boundary: this is a causal, manufacturer-curve-derived teaching approximation, not a manufacturer-validated transient model or a measurement of the real bead. It matches one complex point, does not match the full MHz/GHz curves, and extrapolates below the chart's 1 MHz lower limit. The same zero-bias parameters are assumed throughout the 1–9 mA pulse excursion; the available bias curves do not resolve that range.

Saturation, temperature, extra PCB parasitics and parameter tolerances are omitted. The precise ringing and settling numbers below depend on these assumptions.

The ripple cycle is 120–130 µs; it is a specified late-burst observation, not an assertion of exact periodic steady state after infinitely many cycles. The recovery window extends to 360 µs. Settling is measured against each circuit's idle rail, not against 3.3 V: the RC circuit retains its idle DC drop after the pulses stop.

The bead model has much less DC drop but more ringing and pulse ripple. It can overshoot the source voltage because stored magnetic energy returns to the capacitor. The resistor dissipates that oscillatory energy and gives a largely monotonic recovery. Neither minimum voltage, peak-to-peak ripple nor settling time alone captures all three behaviours; state the reference and measurement interval.

This explains why the incoming-voltage-noise attenuation chart cannot predict the current-pulse result by itself. In this example the 100 kHz load spectrum excites the bead model near resonance; RC instead trades voltage headroom for damping. Increasing capacitance changes both the charge droop and resonance. Adding a designed damping branch, moving the repetition frequency or changing the source impedance can change the comparison.

scripts/pulsed_load_example.py simulates the finite-edge load using exact linear-state propagation between input samples. It verifies the bead's complex 1 MHz match, stable model poles, circuit KVL and integrated capacitor charge, then halves the time step from 5 ns to 2.5 ns to check peak and ripple convergence.

Parameters and results are retained in pulsed-load-checks.json. Before using the numbers for hardware sign-off, replace the teaching bead model with measured or vendor-supported complex impedance over the transient's relevant frequency/current range.

For lower cycle ripple and faster recovery, keep RC as the reference. It gives 12.47 mV ripple and 31.51 µs recovery, but loses more DC voltage. The bead-derived model gives less drop below 3.3 V, yet more ringing and slower recovery.

Choose it only if that headroom benefit matters more and its excursion limits pass. No rail-tolerance specification is given, so these results rank candidates without qualifying either supply.
