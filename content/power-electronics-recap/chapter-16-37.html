16.37 A LISN presents 50 Ω to the DUT: does all its disturbance power reach the receiver?

Predict. A line impedance stabilization network (LISN) sits between the mains supply, the device under test (DUT) and an EMI receiver. The data sheet says it presents 50 Ω to the DUT at radio frequencies. Does the receiver therefore absorb all the disturbance power that the DUT generates? Sketch your answer before reading on.

The LISN has three functional connections. Number them as ports of one network, with every port current entering and a common reference conductor:

  • Port 1, supply: the external mains or DC source.
  • Port 2, DUT: the device under test, the source of conducted disturbance.
  • Port 3, measurement: the EMI receiver or spectrum analyser, normally a 50 Ω input.

The network has three jobs. It passes supply power to the DUT with low impedance at the supply frequency. It keeps supply-side RF disturbance away from the receiver. It couples the DUT's RF disturbance into the receiver. A fourth property matters as much: it presents a defined, reproducible RF impedance to the DUT, so that measurements in different laboratories agree. A LISN is not an ideal high-pass and low-pass pair.

This exercise uses the same conventions as §11.4 and the same reduction tools as §16.21 and §16.22. Level 1 is an idealized three-element network that students solve by hand. Level 2 adds the shunt elements of a 50 Ω / 50 µH + 5 Ω V-network and is solved numerically.

Level 1: build the Y matrix from Kirchhoff's current law

Take L = 50 µH between the supply and the DUT, C = 100 nF between the DUT and the measurement port, and an external RM = 50 Ω receiver termination. The values are inspired by the 50 µH / 50 Ω networks; the circuit is not a complete LISN.

Port 1 (supply) -- L --o-- C -- Port 3 (measurement, RM external)
                       |
                 Port 2 (DUT)

                 common reference

With YL = 1/(jωL) and YC = jωC, write the entering currents as functions of the port voltages:

[ I1 ]   [  YL     -YL        0   ] [ V1 ]
[ I2 ] = [ -YL   YL + YC    -YC   ] [ V2 ]
[ I3 ]   [   0     -YC       YC   ] [ V3 ]

Each row is KCL at one port; each off-diagonal entry is minus the admittance that joins two ports. Every row sums to zero, so Y·[1, 1, 1]ᵀ = 0 and det Y = 0. The matrix is singular because no element connects a port to the reference: raising all three voltages together drives no current. A finite Z = Y⁻¹ does not exist for the isolated three-port. That is a valid singular representation, not an error, as in §16.21.

This Kron reduction gives a two-port between the supply and the DUT, whose entries now depend on the removed termination. For this circuit it reproduces the direct result: a series L between ports 1 and 2 with the branch ZM = RM + 1/(jωC) shunting port 2. At 150 kHz, ωL = 47.12 Ω and 1/(ωC) = 10.61 Ω, so ZM = 50 − j10.61 Ω and:

ABCD = [ 0.809 + j0.902     j47.12 ohm ]       det = 1

Z exists once a termination makes the reduced Y invertible. ABCD follows the series-impedance/shunt-admittance product of §16.22. S follows the equal-reference conversion of §11.4; with the 50 Ω receiver inside the network, SᴴS is no longer the identity, because RM dissipates.

Current partition versus power partition

For the RF analysis, short the supply at port 1, drive port 2 with an ideal disturbance current source IN and keep the 50 Ω receiver. The current divides between the supply path ZS = jωL and the receiver path ZM:

The same ratio follows from the reduced two-port with V₁ = 0: IN = Y₂₂ V₂ and IM = V₂/ZM. At 150 kHz:

Quantity, 150 kHz Value
|IM/IN| 0.761, or −2.37 dB
|IS/IN| 0.826
Sum of the two magnitudes 1.59
Active power in the receiver, per A² of IN 28.97 W
Active power in the ideal supply path 0

The magnitudes add to more than one because the two currents are not in phase; only the phasors add to IN. The receiver branch and the inductor resonate in series at 1/(2π√(LC)) = 71.18 kHz, where |IM/IN| = ω₀L/RM = 0.447. Above that frequency the ratio rises towards one; it never exceeds one, because RM adds a positive term to the denominator.

Now the answer to the opening question. In this ideal model the supply path carries 83 % of the disturbance current and dissipates nothing, because an ideal inductor has no real part. Every watt of active disturbance power reaches the receiver, although most of the current does not:

Add 5 Ω of real part to the supply path and the two partitions separate numerically. At 150 kHz the receiver then carries 0.718 of the current, the supply path carries 0.774, and the receiver absorbs 89.6 % of the active power. Current partition and power partition are different questions about the same network.

Level 2: a 50 µH V-network with its shunt elements

Replace the three-element model by the nominal elements of a 50 Ω / 50 µH + 5 Ω V-network: L₁ = 50 µH between supply and DUT; C₁ = 8 µF in series with R₁ = 5 Ω from the supply node to the reference; C₂ = 0.25 µF from the DUT node to the receiver terminal; R₂ = 1 kΩ from the receiver terminal to the reference; the 50 Ω receiver remains external. The shunt elements make the three-port Y invertible, so Z and the full 3 × 3 S matrix exist without any termination. The nominal impedance that the standard assigns to the DUT port is (R₁ + jωL₁) ∥ 50 Ω, with a ±20 % magnitude tolerance.

Supply termination Largest deviation from nominal |Z|, 150 kHz to 30 MHz Largest deviation, 9 kHz to 150 kHz
Short 7.5 % 44 %
Open 4.8 % 7 %
50 Ω 4.8 % 12 %

Nominal |Z| is 32.7 Ω at 150 kHz and 50.0 Ω at 30 MHz. The 8 µF + 5 Ω branch is what makes the DUT-side impedance nearly independent of whatever the mains presents above 150 kHz. Below 150 kHz the level-1 model and a shorted mains both leave the tolerance band, which is why low-frequency conducted-emission work needs a different network, such as the 250 µH variants discussed in the references.

With 50 Ω references at all three ports, the S matrix of the level-2 network at 150 kHz has |S₃₂| = 0.848 (−1.4 dB, DUT to receiver), |S₃₁| = 0.082 (−21.8 dB, supply to receiver) and |S₂₂| = 0.414. The isolation improves to −37 dB at 1 MHz. The reflection |S₂₂| is not zero at 150 kHz because the nominal impedance there is 32.7 Ω, not 50 Ω; the DUT port is matched only at high frequency, where |S₂₂| falls to 0.025. The voltage division factor V₃/V₂ with the receiver attached is −0.03 dB at 150 kHz and −5.1 dB at 9 kHz: the 0.25 µF coupling capacitor and the 50 Ω ∥ 1 kΩ load form a high-pass divider.

Change one thing: terminate port 3 with 1 kΩ instead of 50 Ω and repeat the reduction; remove the 8 µF branch and watch the shorted-supply curve leave the band; replace the ideal current source by a DUT with 10 Ω source impedance. Ask students to predict which of the four panels moves before running the script.

Recall questions: Why is the ideal three-port Y singular, and which single element would make it invertible? Why does the reduced two-port depend on the termination at the removed port? Why can |IM/IN| + |IS/IN| exceed one? Which representation answers "what impedance does the DUT see", and which answers "how much of the supply-side disturbance reaches the receiver"? Finally, what does |S₃₂|² mean, and under which terminations does it equal a power ratio?

Representation choice. Build Y from KCL when the circuit is given as elements between nodes. Reduce to Z or ABCD once the terminations are fixed, because those matrices answer impedance and cascade questions directly. Use S when the receiver, cables and the standard all speak in 50 Ω reference waves. The impedance seen by the DUT, the transfer to the receiver and the isolation of the supply are three different properties of the same multiport. A 50 Ω data-sheet value fixes the first and says nothing by itself about the other two. To speak of disturbance power through S parameters, distinguish incident waves, reflected waves and absorbed power at stated reference impedances; |IM/IN| is a current ratio, not a power ratio.

Both levels are authored lumped linear models with the nominal element values of a 50 Ω / 50 µH + 5 Ω network.
