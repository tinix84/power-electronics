16.4 Why did splitting the ground plane make the system worse? — Ott-inspired

Predict. A designer separates analog and digital ground copper, then routes a clock across the gap. Ask students to draw the forward and return path before deciding whether the partition helps.

Use a stipulated effective path model to quantify the consequence: R = 5 mΩ, with L = 5 nH for the nearby return and 50 nH for the detour. These are authored example values, not inductances extracted from the illustrated geometry. For a 1 A / 20 ns rising current edge:

The inductive contribution is 0.25 V or 2.5 V, respectively; after the edge, the inductive contribution vanishes and the resistive drop is 5 mV. The specified linear ramp has abrupt changes of slope, so the plotted path voltage has idealized jumps at its ends. The model stipulates the current waveform; it does not claim a real logic driver can maintain that waveform against any resulting impedance.

Change one thing: provide a continuous nearby return, or route signals through a deliberately designed bridge with their return path. Component placement and current-loop organization remain necessary on an unbroken plane. At low frequencies current distribution is influenced strongly by resistance; at high frequencies inductance and field coupling become important. Avoid treating “current always takes the shortest path” as a universal law.

Why the basic matters: KCL still requires a closed path when the drawing hides it behind ground symbols. The simple lumped model illustrates voltage consequences, not radiated-emission levels. See Ott's slots-in-ground-planes explanation and PCB layout/stackup chapter description.

Layout decision. Keep a continuous return under the clock in this example. It avoids the detour but still requires sensible placement. If a partition is required, route the crossing with a designed return bridge. The partition alone is not an interference remedy.
