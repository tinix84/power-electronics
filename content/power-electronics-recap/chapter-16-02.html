16.2 Why does a precise current shunt give a 50% error? — Pease/Ott-inspired

Predict. A 2 mΩ shunt carries 20 A. The amplifier has negligible offset and input current. It reports a voltage that corresponds to 30 A. Find the missing resistance before changing the amplifier.

The intended signal is 20 A × 2 mΩ = 40 mV. If the sense wires include 1 mΩ of current-carrying copper, the amplifier sees 60 mV. Dividing by the nominal 2 mΩ produces 30 A, a 50% error. The amplifier can accurately measure a voltage that is not the voltage we intended.

Now ramp the current from 0 to 20 A in 100 ns. A shared 5 nH adds 1 V during the ramp, much larger than the useful millivolt signal. The ideal Kelvin connection excludes the shared-path contributions, giving only the 2 mΩ resistive signal in this model.

Change one thing: move the sense endpoints directly to the shunt's sense terminals. Does the error disappear? Still check shunt inductance, amplifier common-mode limits and magnetic pickup in the sense loop. A 1 V spike may overload the amplifier.

Why the basic matters: a ground reference and a component terminal are physical locations. Draw the voltage actually measured. Pease's noise-rejection discussion, collected in Volume 3 and Ott's mixed-signal grounding discussion motivate this treatment of reference nodes; this particular shunt exercise is authored here.

Kelvin sensing excludes the shared copper drop at the measurement points. Choose it for accurate sensing through fast current changes. The cost is separate sense routing and careful endpoint placement.

Calibration keeps the existing wiring. Scaling the reading by 2/3 corrects this example's DC error while the resistance ratio stays constant. It can be acceptable for steady or slowly varying current. Verify the error across the required current and temperature range. A fixed gain correction cannot remove the inductive spike or prevent amplifier overload.

Our decision. Use Kelvin sensing here because the measurement must follow the 100 ns current ramp. Calibration alone cannot meet that requirement. For a DC-only measurement on an existing board, calibration remains an option if its verified error meets the specification.
