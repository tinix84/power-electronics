2.4 Transformer as a basic two-port element

What it does: a transformer transfers electrical power between winding circuits through a common magnetic field. The ideal element changes voltage, current and impedance according to its turns ratio while conserving instantaneous power. Separate primary/secondary windings allow galvanic isolation; the physical insulation construction determines the actual isolation rating. Interwinding capacitance still provides a high-frequency coupling path.

Ideal model and dot convention

The minus sign follows from the two passive port references: when the primary absorbs power, the secondary delivers it. If instead the secondary current arrow points out of its dot toward the load, the delivered-current relation is i₁ = iout/n. Reversing a winding connection reverses the corresponding voltage polarity. Dots identify relative winding polarity, not protective earth or a common electrical node.

The ideal transformer has perfect coupling, no winding resistance, no leakage and no magnetizing-current requirement. It stores no energy by itself. A pair of perfectly coupled inductors with finite inductance still has a magnetizing energy state, so it is a different model.

Resistance, reactance and complex impedance all reflect with n² when moved from secondary to primary. Moving an impedance from primary to secondary divides it by n². This scaling also applies to a local incremental impedance; it does not change the sign of a negative incremental resistance.

Example: an ideal 4:1 transformer is driven with a 48 V RMS sinusoid and supplies a 6 Ω secondary resistor:

Quantity Calculation Result
Secondary RMS voltage 48 V / 4 12 V
Delivered secondary RMS current 12 V / 6 Ω 2 A
Primary RMS current 2 A / 4 0.5 A
Reflected input resistance 4² × 6 Ω 96 Ω
Input and output active power 48 V × 0.5 A = 12 V × 2 A 24 W

The current and voltage ratios are inverse; both cannot increase while conserving power. With the signed port-current references above, the secondary current phasor is opposite to the delivered load-current phasor. The table uses current magnitudes.

From the ideal element to a practical transformer

The figure's approximate model refers the load and winding losses to the primary, so no turns-ratio element remains in that drawing:

  • Magnetizing inductance Lm: requires current to establish flux, including at no load. It stores magnetic energy. For an idealized sinusoidal winding voltage, its RMS current magnitude is V/(ωLm).
  • Core-loss resistance Rcore: represents average core loss approximately around the selected waveform, frequency and flux swing. A fixed resistance is not a universal model of hysteresis and eddy-current losses.
  • Leakage inductance Lσ: represents flux that does not link both windings. It causes load-dependent voltage drops, switching spikes and resonances with capacitance.
  • Winding resistance: produces copper loss and load-dependent voltage drop. In a primary-referred approximation, Rcu ≈ R₁ + n²R₂; leakage terms can be referred in the same way when the chosen equivalent circuit permits their combination.
  • Winding and interwinding capacitance: becomes important at high frequency and across an isolation barrier; it is omitted from the simple low-frequency drawing.

For coupled-inductor descriptions, mutual inductance is M = k√(L₁L₂), with coupling coefficient between zero and one for a passive reciprocal pair. The detailed equivalent model and parameter reference side must be stated. Combining all series losses ahead of the magnetizing branch, as in the figure, is an approximation suitable when magnetizing current is small relative to referred load current.

Example: 48 V across a 20-turn winding with 100 mm² effective core area for 5 µs changes flux density by 0.12 T. An equal negative volt-second interval brings B back to its starting value. This specifies a flux change, not its absolute peak: initial flux, DC bias and the material's usable operating range still matter.

With significant copper drop, integrate the induced/core voltage rather than treating the terminal voltage as identical to it.

A repeated nonzero net induced volt-second area causes flux to walk from cycle to cycle. Sustained DC voltage applied to a real finite-core winding ramps flux and can drive saturation; the algebraic ideal-transformer ratios alone do not expose that failure. Periodic operation needs flux reset, provided by the topology and drive waveform.

Converter connection: a forward-type stage transfers energy to the secondary during the transfer interval and must also reset magnetizing flux. A flyback deliberately stores energy in magnetizing inductance, often largely in a core gap, and releases it in another interval. Treating the flyback magnetic component as only an ideal turns-ratio element misses its main energy-storage function. MIT's magnetics notes provide the next reading step.

Which transformer model? Start with the ideal ratio for reflected load and power. It is easy to inspect but misses magnetizing current and parasitic drops. Use the practical model when those effects can change voltage stress, loss or flux margin.
