16.16 Can a gate resistor see 18.8 W while dissipating only 56.4 mW on average?

Predict. A 0–12 V gate driver charges and discharges an ideal 5 nF capacitor at 100 kHz. The external gate resistor is 4.7 Ω, with another 1.3 Ω in the driver/internal gate path. Assume the same total 6 Ω for both edges and enough time for each charge/discharge to complete. Is a resistor's continuous wattage rating sufficient to choose it?

The electrical time constant is 30 ns, and initial current is 2 A at each edge. External-resistor initial pulse power is 2² × 4.7 = 18.8 W. It decays exponentially with a 15 ns power-decay time constant, since power is proportional to current squared. Each complete charge or discharge dissipates half the capacitor's CV² across all series resistance. The external resistor's share is:

There are two edges per cycle, giving 0.564 µJ per cycle and 56.4 mW average at 100 kHz. At 1 MHz, with the same complete transitions, average loss becomes 0.564 W although individual pulse energy and peak power remain the same. The 56.4 mW value is only the external resistor's loss; the idealized complete gate path dissipates CgVg²fs = 72 mW at 100 kHz.

Driver and internal gate resistance dissipate the remainder.

Change one thing: increase external resistance. This lowers peak current/power and stretches each pulse, but in this fully settled linear-capacitor model the complete path's energy per cycle remains CgVg². The external resistor's fraction changes with its share of total resistance. Meanwhile slower switching can increase MOSFET drain-side overlap loss (§16.8). A lower peak gate current therefore does not automatically mean a more efficient converter.

For a real MOSFET, gate charge and Miller behaviour replace the single fixed Cg model; driver source/sink impedances and turn-on/off resistance may differ. Qg × drive swing × switching frequency is a useful conventional total gate-drive estimate under its stated drive conditions, not a universal external-resistor loss formula. The nonlinear gate waveform determines the actual external resistor integral of i²R.

Thermal lesson: use pulse energy and the relevant local thermal response, not peak power times steady Rth. Conversely, low average watts do not qualify a nanosecond pulse. At such short times the resistive element and hot spots matter; whole-package mass × specific heat can overestimate the available effective Cth.

Check the resistor manufacturer's pulse curve, peak voltage/current, repetition and mounting conditions. Do not extrapolate a microsecond pulse curve into nanoseconds without supporting data. Vishay's pulse-capability note explains the separate pulse-energy, voltage and average-power checks; it does not validate the 30 ns teaching case.

Gate-path choice. First seek a resistor qualified for the stated pulse waveform and repetition rate. Its 56.4 mW average does not settle the selection. Keep 4.7 Ω only if that pulse check passes.

Increasing resistance is an alternative when the peak is limiting, but it stretches the pulse and may increase transistor overlap loss. Recheck both components before treating the lower gate-current peak as an improvement.
