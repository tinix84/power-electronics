The transformer is a two-port element that couples two winding circuits. Its last-column expression is the input impedance with a specified secondary termination, rather than a standalone one-port impedance like R, L or C. Section 2.4 defines its voltage/current references and practical equivalent circuit.

Finite voltage cannot change ideal inductor current instantaneously. Finite current cannot change ideal capacitor voltage instantaneously. Opening an inductive current path therefore creates a voltage until another path conducts, a parasitic capacitance charges, or breakdown occurs. Connecting an ideal voltage source directly to a capacitor at a different voltage requires an impulse of current in the ideal model.

Waveform pairs to sketch. Because , a constant voltage across an inductor produces a linear current ramp: 12 V across 10 µH for 2 µs raises the current by A. A square voltage therefore gives a triangular current, which is the inductor-current ripple of every PWM converter.

Dually, a constant current into a capacitor ramps its voltage: 1 A into 10 µF for 10 µs gives V, and a triangular current gives a parabolic voltage, the output-capacitor ripple of a buck. Read the pairs in both directions as well: a triangular inductor current implies a square inductor voltage, and a voltage step forced onto an ideal capacitor implies an impulse of current.

The volt-second and ampere-second products in these sketches are the quantities balanced in section 7.

Real inductors have winding resistance, core loss, saturation, leakage flux and interwinding capacitance. Real capacitors have ESR, ESL, leakage, voltage dependence and finite ripple-current capability. An apparent wire is part of a current loop with resistance and inductance.
