12.3 Why Class-D uses LC filtering

A Class-D output stage creates a switching waveform whose modulation represents the desired audio. A suitable LC network passes the audio band and attenuates switching components. Ideal L and C exchange stored energy; their filtering does not require a large dissipative series resistor. Real losses remain in winding resistance, core loss, capacitor ESR, switches and the load.

Why not a Class-A amplifier? A Class-A stage biases its transistor in the middle of the output characteristic (section 13), so it carries a standing current even in silence. Predict its best efficiency before reading on.

For a single 24 V supply driving an 8 Ω resistive load directly, the bias is half the supply across the load: = 24 V / (2 · 8 Ω) = 1.5 A. The supply always delivers 24 V × 1.5 A = 36 W. The largest undistorted sine gives the load = 9 W. The theoretical maximum is therefore 25 %, and it falls further with quieter music, because the 36 W stays. Coupling the load through an inductor or transformer lets the collector swing to twice the supply and raises the ceiling to 50 %. Class B, with two transistors each conducting half the cycle, reaches = 78.5 % at full sine output, at the price of crossover distortion; Class AB trades some of that efficiency back for linearity.

Every one of these classes keeps the transistor in the region where voltage and current coexist. Class D leaves it: the transistor only switches, and the LC filter rebuilds the signal. Practical Class-D amplifiers reach about 90 % at moderate power. The cost is the filter, switching-frequency EMI and distortion from dead time. Keep Class A or AB when the power is small and linearity matters more than heat, as in a headphone driver. Choose Class D when battery life or heat sinks set the limit.

At 20 kHz the LC gain is approximately −0.263 dB. At 400 kHz it is approximately −40.0 dB, a voltage factor near 0.01. A one-pole RC with the same nominal corner gives only about −20.0 dB at 400 kHz. These are individual-frequency gains, not a guarantee that all output ripple or EMI has those attenuations.

The load supplies damping in this simple LC model. Changing speaker impedance changes Q; an open load removes that damping and can produce severe peaking in the idealized model. Actual speakers are frequency-dependent loads. Bridge-tied-load and different PWM modulation schemes require the correct differential/common-mode equivalents; do not copy these single-ended L/C values into a bridge without conversion.

Some Class-D products use filterless arrangements for specific loads and cable lengths. See TI's LC filter design note.

RC or LC? LC avoids a large dissipative series resistor, but adds resonance and load-dependent damping. RC is simpler when its voltage drop and loss are acceptable. Check attenuation, load range and current stress before choosing either for a power output.
