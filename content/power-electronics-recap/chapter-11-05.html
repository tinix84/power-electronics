11.5 Miller theorem: a bridge impedance seen through voltage gain

Connect a bridge impedance Zb between port nodes V₁ and V₂. Let Yb = 1/Zb and let Av(s) = V₂/V₁ be the actual loaded small-signal node-voltage ratio. The bridge current entering at node 1 is:

Hence its input contribution is admittance multiplied by 1 − Av, or equivalently impedance divided by 1 − Av:

At node 2, for Av ≠ 0, the corresponding contribution is Yb(1 − 1/Av), or Zb/(1 − 1/Av). These are the two grounded Miller equivalents. The result is not limited to capacitors: an ideal 100 kΩ bridge with Av = −9 contributes 10 kΩ at the input and 90 kΩ at the output.

Connection to a π two-port. Include input/output shunt admittances Yi and Yo around the bridge:

          Zb
V1 o------[]------o V2
   |              |
   Yi             Yo
   |              |
   +-- reference--+

Y = [ Yi + Yb    -Yb   ]
    [   -Yb     Yo + Yb ]

Substituting V₂ = AvV₁ into the first row gives Yin = Yi + Yb(1 − Av). The bridge alone has the singular Y matrix discussed in §16.21, yet its port currents are well defined. The gain relation supplied by the surrounding active circuit lets us evaluate the admittance seen along that operating condition.

For a bridge capacitor Cb and approximately real, frequency-independent gain over the band of interest, the equivalent input capacitance is Cb(1 − Av). Inverting gain −9 gives 10Cb. A follower with Av approaching +1 suppresses the bridge current, the basis of bootstrapping; exact +1 makes this branch's input admittance zero.

Non-inverting gain above one can produce a negative equivalent capacitance, requiring the active circuit and a stability analysis. With complex, frequency-dependent Av, use the complete admittance expression rather than assuming a constant physical capacitor.

The replacement is conditional on the stated voltage ratio. It is not a universal diagonal two-port matrix valid when V₁ and V₂ are varied independently. If adding the bridge changes gain, solve for the loaded gain consistently; do not substitute an unloaded gain without checking the approximation. See MIT 6.776, lecture 7, Miller effect, slides 8–10.
