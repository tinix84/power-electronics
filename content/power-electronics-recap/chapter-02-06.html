2.6 Real transformer: voltage drop, losses and operating limits

Why the turns ratio is not enough: it relates the internal induced voltages. Winding drops separate those voltages from the terminals; excitation current also flows when the secondary is open. Start with section 2.4's ideal element, then include the effects needed at the operating frequency.

A circuit with separate winding impedances

The primary winding impedance Z₁ precedes the excitation branch; the referred secondary impedance Z′₂ follows it. This T equivalent circuit retains the difference between primary current I₁ and referred delivered load current I′₂:

The physical delivered secondary current is I₂,out = nI′₂. In open circuit, I′₂ = 0 but I₁ = I₀ generally remains nonzero. The real part of excitation admittance accounts for core dissipation; its inductive part accounts for reversible magnetic energy storage. The internal induced voltages obey E₁ = nE₂, while loaded terminal voltages generally do not obey V₁ = nV₂.

This is often called the exact equivalent circuit in introductory transformer analysis: exact within the chosen linear lumped model. It still omits saturation, distributed capacitance, and frequency-dependent loss. Section 2.4's combined-series circuit is a further approximation; retaining separate branches avoids assigning secondary copper loss to the no-load primary current.

The square-wave voltage is its constant magnitude in each half-period. Both expressions assume centred periodic flux; DC offset or incomplete reset requires tracking absolute B as well. The sine formula's 4.44 factor must not be applied to arbitrary PWM. At unchanged induced voltage, reducing frequency increases flux swing.

Saturation reduces incremental magnetizing inductance and can cause a steep current rise. Switching near an unfavourable mains phase, together with remanent flux, can also cause inrush even when the final periodic operating point is acceptable.

Higher switching frequency allows fewer turns or a smaller core area for the same voltage and flux swing, but raises other constraints. Core loss depends on material, frequency, temperature and the actual B waveform. Skin and proximity effects make winding AC resistance frequency dependent; for a linear harmonic loss model, winding loss is a sum of Iₖ,rms²Rac(kf).

Section 2.7 develops this conductor-loss calculation. Insulation thickness, winding arrangement and cooling also matter. A constant Rcore and DC winding resistances cannot predict all of these effects. TI's Power Transformer Design note discusses the loss and thermal tradeoffs.

Switching edges: leakage energy and capacitive feedthrough

At a switching transition, leakage inductance resists rapid current change. Its stored energy must transfer to another element or dissipate:

As a separate switching example, 2 µH carrying 3 A stores 9 µJ. If one such event per 100 kHz cycle is fully dissipated in a clamp, its average contribution is 0.90 W. If that energy instead charges an initially uncharged ideal 200 pF capacitor, the energy-only estimate gives 300 V and a natural frequency of 7.96 MHz.

These are model scales: initial capacitor voltage, rail connections, nonlinear device capacitance, damping and the clamp determine the actual switch peak. Do not simply add 300 V to a rail without deriving the circuit.

For 20 pF subjected to 10 V/ns, the instantaneous current scale is 0.20 A. This is edge current, not continuous load current. Its return path determines common-mode EMI. Interleaving windings can reduce leakage while increasing interwinding capacitance; electrostatic shields can redirect capacitive current but need a defined connection.

Insulation and dielectric distance affect both capacitance and isolation construction, linking back to section 3.3. These capacitances are omitted from the 50 Hz T model above; add them when analysing fast switching edges.

How the model parameters are estimated

  • Winding resistance measurement: estimates R₁ and R₂ at the measurement temperature; operating-temperature and AC loss can differ.
  • Open-circuit test: at the intended sinusoidal frequency and voltage, measure Voc, Ioc and Poc. When series drops and no-load copper loss are small, Rcore ≈ Voc²/Poc, Im ≈ √(Ioc² − (Poc/Voc)²), and Lm ≈ Voc/(ωIm).
  • Short-circuit test: with one winding shorted, apply a reduced voltage at the test frequency to obtain the selected test current. With excitation current negligible, Zeq ≈ Vsc/Isc, Req ≈ Psc/Isc² and Xeq ≈ √(Zeq² − Req²). These give the combined series parameters referred to the driven side; they do not uniquely split primary and secondary leakage.

The open/short estimates and their assumptions are described in Rose-Hulman's notes, pages 12–13. A switching-frequency component also needs impedance, capacitance, loss and bias information relevant to its operating waveforms.

Classroom check: why can the loaded real transformer draw more primary current than the ideal example while delivering less power? Its primary current supplies the referred load, core loss and magnetizing excitation; winding losses also consume input active power. Use phasor addition for currents and an active-power balance for watts.
